## [global politics] WHO declares Bundibugyo Ebola outbreak in DRC and Uganda a global health emergency — third-ever outbreak of a strain with no approved vaccines

**Source:** https://www.who.int/news/item/17-05-2026-epidemic-of-ebola-disease-in-the-democratic-republic-of-the-congo-and-uganda-determined-a-public-health-emergency-of-international-concern

**Lead:** The World Health Organization declared the Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of Congo and Uganda a public health emergency of international concern on May 17, 2026, after cross-border cases appeared in Kampala — the first known incursion of the rare Bundibugyo ebolavirus, for which no approved vaccines or treatments exist, into a major African capital.

**Context:** The PHEIC declaration was triggered by three factors: cross-border spread from DRC's Ituri Province into Uganda's capital Kampala, unexplained clusters of deaths within the DRC, and major uncertainty about the true scale of the epidemic. As of May 16, the DRC had confirmed 8 laboratory cases and recorded 246 suspected cases and 80 suspected deaths across at least three health zones — Bunia, Rwampara, and Mongbwalu — in the eastern Ituri Province, a gold-mining region near the Ugandan border characterized by weak health infrastructure and high population mobility. Uganda reported two lab-confirmed Kampala cases within 24 hours of each other, with no apparent epidemiological link between them, suggesting at least two independent importation events. The pathogen — Bundibugyo ebolavirus (BVD) — is rare even by Ebola standards: this is only its third documented outbreak ever, first identified in Uganda's Bundibugyo District in 2007 and last seen in DRC in 2012. Unlike the far more common Zaire ebolavirus strain, for which the rVSV-ZEBOV-GP vaccine and the monoclonal antibody cocktail Inmazeb provide proven medical countermeasures, BVD has no approved vaccines or therapeutics — leaving containment entirely dependent on isolation, contact tracing, safe burials, and behavioral change communication. The WHO determined the outbreak does not yet meet the threshold for "pandemic emergency," the organization's highest alert level.

**Why it matters:** The PHEIC declaration activates international coordination mechanisms: it instructs WHO member states to activate emergency operations, permits WHO to issue temporary travel and trade recommendations, and typically unlocks emergency funding from CEPI and bilateral donors. The absence of any approved medical countermeasure against BVD is the central risk factor distinguishing this outbreak from recent Ebola responses — during the 2018–2020 North Kivu outbreak that killed more than 2,000 people, the rVSV-ZEBOV-GP vaccine enabled effective ring vaccination. With no equivalent tool for BVD, responders must rely entirely on classical containment in a region — eastern DRC — characterized by active armed conflict, mass displacement, and historically high distrust of health authorities. The two unlinked Kampala cases are the sharpest immediate concern: if community transmission establishes itself in a city of nearly 4 million people with international airport connections, the containment calculus changes from outbreak management to potential pandemic risk. WHO's decision to stop short of "pandemic emergency" implies confidence that containment remains feasible — but that judgment will be reviewed rapidly as contact tracing data comes in.

## [cybersecurity] Disgruntled ex-Microsoft researcher releases BitLocker bypass "YellowKey" — encrypted Windows 11 drives readable with a USB stick and a keystroke

**Source:** https://www.theregister.com/security/2026/05/13/disgruntled-researcher-releases-two-more-microsoft-zero-days/5239758

**Lead:** A researcher known as "Nightmare-Eclipse," believed to be a former Microsoft employee acting in retaliation for a contract dispute, released proof-of-concept exploit code on May 13 showing that Windows 11's BitLocker encryption can be bypassed with physical access, a USB drive, and a CTRL-key sequence in the Windows Recovery Environment — with no patch available from Microsoft.

**Context:** The researcher, posting under the aliases "Nightmare-Eclipse" and "Chaotic Eclipse," has disclosed five Microsoft zero-days in 2026: BlueHammer (CVE-2026-32201, now patched after April Patch Tuesday), RedSun (Windows Defender privilege escalation, unpatched), UnDefend (Windows Defender denial-of-service, unpatched), and now YellowKey and GreenPlasma, both released May 13. The disclosures began after the researcher posted that "someone violated our agreement and left me homeless with nothing," and security reporters describe the campaign as insider retaliation. YellowKey targets Windows 11, Windows Server 2022, and Windows Server 2025 — not Windows 10: an attacker copies specially crafted "FsTx" files to a USB drive, boots the target machine into the Windows Recovery Environment (WinRE), and triggers an unrestricted shell by holding CTRL, bypassing BitLocker and granting full read/write access to the drive's contents. Security expert Rik Ferguson summarized the practical impact: "a stolen laptop stops being a hardware problem and becomes a breach notification." The concurrent GreenPlasma exploit escalates any Windows 11 or Server 2022/2025 process to SYSTEM-level privileges via the CTFMON framework, enabling credential harvesting, lateral movement, and ransomware deployment from a standard user account. The researcher has announced a "dead man's switch" — additional vulnerabilities held ready for release — suggesting further disclosures may follow before Microsoft can patch the outstanding flaws.

**Why it matters:** BitLocker is the primary disk-encryption layer protecting hundreds of millions of Windows-deployed endpoints — enterprise laptops, government workstations, regulated-sector devices — and the breach-notification threshold assumption it underpins ("if the device is encrypted, a theft is a hardware loss, not a reportable data breach") applies to a large fraction of current incident response and insurance determinations. YellowKey's physical-access requirement is a real constraint, but it is not a high bar: lost or stolen devices, insider access scenarios, service depot chains, and law enforcement seizures all represent situations where WinRE access is feasible. Available mitigations — enabling BitLocker PINs at boot and setting BIOS password locks — are not default configurations in most enterprise deployments, leaving a large effective attack surface until administrators remediate. The "dead man's switch" threat raises the possibility of further disclosures timed to maximize pressure on Microsoft, which has not yet publicly acknowledged the outstanding YellowKey or GreenPlasma flaws. The exploitation of what appears to be intimate insider knowledge of internal Windows components raises questions about how Microsoft manages privileged-access agreements and NDAs for departing employees with security-sensitive system knowledge.

## [tech law] FTC bans data broker Kochava from selling sensitive location data, requiring explicit consent and new compliance programs

**Source:** https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/05/ftc-ban-kochava-subsidiary-selling-sensitive-location-data-settle-charges-they-sold-location-data

**Lead:** The Federal Trade Commission announced on May 4, 2026, that it will ban Idaho-based data broker Kochava and its subsidiary Collective Data Solutions from selling precise location data from hundreds of millions of mobile devices without affirmative express consumer consent, settling a 2022 lawsuit over sales that exposed visits to health clinics, places of worship, and other sensitive locations.

**Context:** The FTC's enforcement action against Kochava was originally filed in August 2022, alleging that the company's data products allowed purchasers to trace individuals' movements to sensitive locations — reproductive health facilities, addiction treatment centers, houses of worship, military installations, and domestic violence shelters — without those individuals' knowledge or consent. Kochava and its subsidiary Collective Data Solutions (CDS) collected precise geolocation data from hundreds of millions of mobile device identifiers through relationships with app publishers and upstream data supply chains, packaging it into audience segments and raw data products sold to advertisers, insurers, financial institutions, and other buyers. Under the proposed settlement order, Kochava and CDS must obtain affirmative express consumer consent before any location data transaction; establish a "sensitive location data program" maintaining a comprehensive list of sensitive venue categories; implement supplier assessment protocols to verify that consent was obtained upstream in the data supply chain; allow consumers to request the identities of businesses to which their location data was sold; and submit incident reports to the FTC when third-party data suppliers violate contractual consent requirements. The order does not include a monetary penalty, which privacy advocates have flagged as a significant gap given the revenue Kochava generated from the contested products.

**Why it matters:** The Kochava settlement is the FTC's most detailed behavioral order yet in the data broker sector and establishes an operational compliance template — consent verification, sensitive-location screening, supplier auditing, consumer data-request rights — that will function as de facto industry standards until Congress passes comprehensive federal privacy legislation. The absence of a financial penalty is a material limitation: industry observers can note that non-compliance generated only structural obligations rather than fines that would alter the economic calculus of data brokerage, reducing deterrence for players with smaller profiles and less public exposure than Kochava. For the mobile advertising and data supply chain ecosystem, the order's supplier assessment requirement is novel: it pushes consent accountability upstream to original data collectors, not just the final seller, which could meaningfully increase compliance costs and renegotiate leverage for downstream data buyers. The FTC under Chairman Andrew Ferguson has publicly prioritized COPPA enforcement and sensitive-data protection, and the Kochava settlement's consent-first framework maps directly onto that enforcement direction. State attorneys general in California, Washington, and Illinois are treating this federal settlement as a template for their own enforcement under CCPA and analogous state privacy laws.

## [law] Hungary's Tisza party wins historic supermajority — constitutional scholar argues the Fidesz-era cardinal law system should now be abolished

**Source:** https://verfassungsblog.de/the-big-lie-of-two-thirds-majority/

**Lead:** Following Péter Magyar's Tisza party's unprecedented win of a two-thirds parliamentary majority in Hungary's 2026 election — the first non-Fidesz supermajority in 16 years — a constitutional law scholar argues the very system of cardinal laws that Fidesz weaponized to entrench its rule should be dismantled as a condition of genuine democratic transition.

**Context:** When Viktor Orbán's Fidesz party won its first two-thirds majority in 2010, it used the supermajority to restructure Hungary's constitutional order — including dramatically expanding the number of "cardinal laws," a legislative category that can only be passed or amended with a two-thirds parliamentary vote. Fidesz added more than 30 policy areas to the cardinal law category, including migration policy, asylum, family policy, the electoral system, and tax rates. The intended effect was constitutional lock-in: even if Fidesz later lost its supermajority, a successor government with only a bare parliamentary majority could not amend taxation or asylum policy without Fidesz consent. Beáta Bakó, a postdoctoral researcher at Charles University in Prague writing in Verfassungsblog on May 13, 2026, argues that the assumption underlying cardinal laws — that requiring a supermajority guarantees compromise between political actors — has been empirically falsified. Fidesz used its unilateral supermajority to pass cardinal laws without any cross-party compromise whatsoever, demonstrating that the mechanism's protective function depends entirely on the existence of divided supermajority politics. Now that Tisza has won its own two-thirds majority — the first time any non-Fidesz party has achieved this threshold since 2010 — the article argues Tisza should use that power to abolish or substantially narrow the cardinal law category precisely to prevent future authoritarian actors from weaponizing it again.

**Why it matters:** Hungary's political transition from Fidesz to Tisza is one of the most significant rule-of-law developments in the European Union in a decade, and the debate over cardinal laws goes to the core of what post-authoritarian constitutional repair looks like in practice. If Tisza abolishes or narrows the cardinal law category, it removes one of the most potent constitutional entrenchment tools Fidesz constructed — but also sets a precedent that supermajority governments can undo their predecessors' constitutional work with minimal procedural friction, which has its own risks for constitutional stability. Bakó's argument is essentially a claim that constitutional design must be evaluated against the realistic worst case, not the assumption of good-faith actors: a provision that functions as a constraint only when power is divided should not survive a transition government with the procedural power to remove it. The European Commission's Rule of Law Report has cited Hungary's cardinal law structure as a barrier to judicial independence reform, and its abolition could advance Hungary's compliance with outstanding EU Article 7 proceedings and unlock conditional EU fund releases. The Polish democratic transition — which has moved faster on judicial reform — is providing a comparative reference point that both the EU institutions and Hungarian constitutional lawyers are watching carefully.

## [law] India's Modi proposes expanding Lok Sabha from 543 to 850 seats — constitutional scholars warn delimitation could corrode the federal bargain between north and south

**Source:** https://verfassungsblog.de/federalism-against-democracy/

**Lead:** The Modi government's Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill, 2026, which would expand India's lower house from 543 to approximately 850 seats through a new population-based delimitation, is drawing constitutional scrutiny over whether the reapportionment would systematically reduce parliamentary representation for southern states that achieved population stabilization earlier than the more populous, BJP-dominated north.

**Context:** India's delimitation — the process of redrawing electoral constituency boundaries and reallocating parliamentary seats among states — has been frozen since 1976, when Indira Gandhi suspended automatic reapportionment to avoid penalizing states that had successfully reduced population growth. The current allocation of 543 Lok Sabha seats therefore reflects 1970s population figures, which significantly underrepresent the more populous northern and eastern states — Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, and Madhya Pradesh, all BJP strongholds — relative to their current populations. The Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill proposes to expand the Lok Sabha to approximately 850 seats and conduct a new delimitation exercise allocating seats proportional to current population. Sarath Pillai, writing in Verfassungsblog on May 12, 2026, argues that this reallocation would substantially reduce the parliamentary representation of southern states — Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, and Telangana — which have achieved replacement-level fertility and thus have smaller populations relative to their economic contribution. The constitutional issue extends beyond fairness: India's federal design gives states voice through Rajya Sabha representation and fiscal transfers, but the Lok Sabha is the dominant chamber, and a major compositional shift toward BJP-majority states would alter the legislative calculus on language policy, central-state fiscal relations, and constitutional amendment thresholds.

**Why it matters:** Delimitation at this scale is potentially the largest structural change to Indian electoral politics since independence, playing out against intense center-periphery tensions between the Hindi-belt north and the non-BJP south. Southern states — already vocal about receiving disproportionately low shares of central tax devolution relative to their economic output — would face the simultaneous prospect of losing both fiscal leverage and parliamentary seats. India's constitution has a "unitary tilt," as Pillai describes it: the central government retains emergency override powers not available in most federal systems, making the Lok Sabha's composition particularly consequential for the center-state power balance. The Modi government's current parliamentary strength means the 131st Amendment could be passed before state electorates have an opportunity to shift the center's composition, locking in structural changes with long-term implications. India's Supreme Court has previously invalidated constitutional amendments that destroy the "basic structure" of the constitution, and some constitutional scholars argue that a reapportionment systematically disadvantaging specific regional and linguistic communities could qualify as a basic-structure violation — setting up a potential judicial confrontation that would test the limits of India's constitutional entrenchment doctrine.
