## 1. Court turns down Apple's request to pause order holding it in contempt
**Source:** scotusblog.com | **Topic:** law
**Link:** https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/05/court-tuns-down-apples-request-to-pause-order-holding-it-in-contempt/

**Lead:** Justice Elena Kagan denied Apple's emergency request to stay a federal contempt finding over its App Store anti-steering rules, handing Epic Games a significant procedural victory while Apple's broader appeal continues.

**Context:** The dispute between Apple and Epic Games, maker of Fortnite, has moved through federal courts since 2020, when Epic challenged Apple's requirement that developers use its in-app purchase system and pay commissions of up to 30 percent. A federal judge's original order barred Apple from preventing developers from directing customers to alternative payment channels; Apple was later found in contempt after implementing measures—including steep new commissions on third-party purchases—that courts determined undermined the order's spirit. Apple asked the Supreme Court to temporarily halt the contempt ruling, arguing that a stay was needed "before Apple is forced to litigate its commission rate" under a flawed contempt designation while its broader appeal was pending before the Ninth Circuit. Justice Elena Kagan rejected the emergency application on May 6, 2026, less than an hour after Epic filed its opposition and before Apple could even file a reply—a speed legal observers interpreted as signaling the justices viewed the application as meritless on its face. Epic had argued that Apple's "willful contempt has successfully delayed the restoration of competition by more than two years, allowing it to reap billions of dollars" in fees courts had already found anti-competitive.

**Why it matters:** By refusing the stay, the Supreme Court leaves Apple exposed to the lower court's remedial requirements while its broader appeal proceeds, which could force Apple to restructure how it monetizes third-party app distribution across more than a billion active iOS devices. The summary denial—issued before Apple's deadline to file a reply—is legally unusual and suggests the Court saw no emergency warranting intervention, a signal that may shape how aggressively Apple's counsel pursues subsequent emergency applications in the underlying case. Apple has continued collecting billions in commission revenue from App Store transactions since the original injunction issued in 2021, which is precisely the ongoing harm that the contempt order was designed to remedy; every additional month of delay compounds that competitive distortion. The Ninth Circuit will now review the contempt merits, and the Supreme Court's hands-off posture suggests it will not intervene again unless the lower court's remedial order is found fundamentally flawed—setting a high bar for Apple's next appeal.

**Why it matters:** The two actions together illustrate the Court's current willingness to move at extraordinary speed on politically divisive questions while defying simple ideological characterization. The abortion pill stay was issued by Alito—the author of the Dobbs opinion—suggesting that even the Court's most consistent opponent of abortion rights is wary of being seen as eliminating access to a medication used in roughly two-thirds of US abortions.

**Lead:** The Supreme Court rejected Black voters' emergency request to undo the expedited finalization of its Louisiana redistricting ruling, locking in an accelerated timeline that forces the state legislature to redraw congressional maps under court supervision before the 2026 elections.

**Context:** On May 6, 2026, the Supreme Court summarily rejected an emergency application by Black voters defending Louisiana's 2024 congressional map, who had asked the Court to reverse its May 5 decision to immediately finalize its April 29 ruling in Louisiana v. Callais. The 6-3 underlying ruling held that Louisiana's revised congressional map violated the Equal Protection Clause by creating two majority-Black districts primarily on the basis of race without adequate justification under existing precedent. The immediate finalization bypassed the standard 32-day waiting period that normally allows losing parties to seek reconsideration before a judgment is sent to lower courts for enforcement, eliminating any further delay to the redistricting mandate. Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson, joined by Justices Sotomayor and Kagan, had written on May 5 that the expedited procedure was unprecedented in recent decades and "spawned chaos in the State of Louisiana"; Justices Alito, Thomas, and Gorsuch wrote a concurrence criticizing her dissent's "rhetorical excess." Republicans currently hold four of Louisiana's six House seats; analysts estimate the state could flip one or two additional seats depending on how redrawn lines are configured.

**Why it matters:** With the May 6 denial, all appellate options to delay the redistricting mandate are exhausted, and Louisiana's Republican-controlled legislature must redraw its six-seat congressional map in a matter of weeks under lower-court supervision—the fastest mandatory redistricting timetable in modern Supreme Court history. Former President Trump has publicly encouraged Alabama, Tennessee, and other southern states to pursue similar emergency redistricting following the ruling, with Republican analysts estimating potential House gains of up to 20 seats nationally if the Louisiana framework is replicated elsewhere. The procedural back-and-forth over the 32-day waiting rule has hardened what is becoming a structural fault line between the Court's conservative majority and its liberal justices over whether emergency procedures are being deployed to produce partisan electoral outcomes, a critique that will intensify as cases from Alabama and Georgia citing similar VRA arguments reach the Court. The decision also sets the framework for redistricting litigation heading into the 2030 census cycle, when the Court's revised Gingles standards from Louisiana v. Callais will govern every Section 2 challenge to newly drawn congressional and state legislative maps.

## 4. Google, Microsoft, and xAI will allow the US government to review their new AI models
**Source:** theverge.com | **Topic:** tech
**Link:** https://www.theverge.com/ai-artificial-intelligence/924017/google-microsoft-xai-government-review

**Lead:** Google, Microsoft, and xAI committed to giving the US government pre-release access to their new AI models for security evaluation by NIST's Center for AI Standards and Innovation, marking the first structured federal AI oversight commitments from major US labs.

**Context:** The National Institute of Standards and Technology announced on May 5, 2026, that Google, Microsoft, and xAI had agreed to submit new models to the Center for AI Standards and Innovation (CAISI)—housed within the Department of Commerce—for evaluation before public deployment. Microsoft said it would work with government scientists to test AI systems "in ways that probe unexpected behaviors," while CAISI disclosed it had already completed more than 40 pre-release evaluations, including on models not yet available to the public. The commitments were partly catalyzed by Anthropic's Mythos model, whose cybersecurity capabilities prompted the White House to weigh formal pre-release review procedures. AI policy adviser David Sacks, who had pushed internally for a mandatory review process, reportedly faced industry pushback and was effectively sidelined within the administration after that effort stalled, making the voluntary CAISI commitments a compromise outcome. Anthropic, OpenAI, and Meta are conspicuously absent from this round of agreements.

**Why it matters:** Pre-release government review of AI models represents a structurally significant shift from the voluntary, industry-led safety frameworks that have governed US AI policy since the 2023 White House Executive Order, which required only self-reporting of safety test results rather than independent government evaluation. If the CAISI process is formalized into regulation, federal agencies could gain authority to require model modifications or delay deployments based on safety findings—a form of pre-market gatekeeping that does not exist in US statute but partially exists under the EU AI Act for general-purpose AI systems above certain compute thresholds. CAISI's disclosure that it has already completed more than 40 evaluations implies that the institutional machinery is in place and that formal regulatory authority—not bureaucratic capacity—is the missing piece for a binding review regime. The absence of Anthropic, OpenAI, and Meta from the current commitments is conspicuous given that those three companies operate the most widely deployed frontier models in the United States; their exclusion either reflects strategic calculation or a disagreement over what government evaluators would be permitted to see and act on.

## 5. FTC bans data broker Kochava from selling sensitive location data
**Source:** therecord.media | **Topic:** tech_law
**Link:** https://therecord.media/ftc-bans-kochava-location-data-sales

**Lead:** The FTC settled its August 2022 lawsuit against data broker Kochava, permanently barring the company from selling consumers' precise geolocation data—including histories tied to health clinics and houses of worship—without explicit affirmative consent.

**Context:** Kochava, a data broker operating through its subsidiary Collective Data Solutions, built its commercial model on collecting and reselling highly granular personal data including precise GPS coordinates accurate to within 10 meters, mobile device identifiers, app usage histories, annual income estimates, and location histories showing visits to health care clinics, abortion providers, and houses of worship. The FTC filed suit in August 2022, alleging Kochava's practices constituted unfair and deceptive trade in violation of the FTC Act—a legal theory that does not require proof of deception, only that the practice causes substantial consumer harm that is not outweighed by countervailing benefits. The settlement, finalized in May 2026, requires Kochava to establish a sensitive location data catalog, implement a supplier assessment program verifying consumer consent before purchasing location data from third parties, adopt mandatory data deletion timelines, and create mechanisms allowing consumers to withdraw consent and identify which downstream parties have received their data.

**Why it matters:** The Kochava consent order is one of the most structurally comprehensive US data-broker enforcement actions to date, going beyond monetary penalties to mandate operational changes in how the company collects, stores, and monetizes personal information—a structural remedy model the FTC has been developing since Lina Khan's tenure. The agency's legal theory—that selling precise geolocation data without affirmative consent is inherently "unfair" even when technically legal—signals the FTC intends to use Section 5 unfairness authority aggressively against the location-data marketplace in the absence of comprehensive federal privacy legislation. Location data has acquired particular legal sensitivity in the post-Dobbs landscape, because records showing visits to abortion clinics or out-of-state reproductive health providers can be subpoenaed or purchased by state prosecutors investigating abortion law violations in the 14 states with near-total bans. Other operators in the precision-location market—including SafeGraph, Veraset, and dozens of advertising-technology data brokers—should read the Kochava order as a direct warning that the FTC will pursue structural relief rather than nominal fines for privacy-invasive commercial practices targeting sensitive consumer location data.

## 6. New CISA initiative aims for critical infrastructure to operate offline during cyberattacks
**Source:** therecord.media | **Topic:** tech_law
**Link:** https://therecord.media/cisa-initiative-aims-for-critical-infrastructure-to-operate-during-cyberattacks

**Lead:** CISA launched CI Fortify, a new initiative requiring US critical infrastructure operators to develop plans for isolating systems and maintaining services without internet connectivity during active cyberattacks, explicitly targeting Chinese Volt Typhoon and Russian state-actor threats.

**Context:** The Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency unveiled CI Fortify on May 6, 2026, describing it as "timely, actionable guidance" for critical infrastructure organizations facing sustained adversarial campaigns. The initiative responds directly to two named state-actor threats: China's Volt Typhoon group, which US officials say has pre-positioned malware in power grids, water systems, and communications networks for potential activation during a Taiwan-related military conflict, and Russian cyberattacks that have struck Polish operational technology networks in 2025-2026. CISA Acting Director Nick Andersen said CI Fortify requires organizations to move beyond perimeter defense to develop detailed emergency operational plans for running services while entirely disconnected from the internet and third-party networks—including segmented operational technology systems, isolated backup infrastructure, and rapid restoration capabilities that function without telecommunications connectivity. CISA has begun conducting targeted assessments tailored to individual sectors and organizations, though the agency declined to disclose which entities are participating or how many assessments are currently underway.

**Why it matters:** CI Fortify represents a doctrinal shift from "defend and detect" to "operate through attack"—an explicit government acknowledgment that adversarial access to US critical infrastructure is likely already pre-positioned and may not be eliminable before a crisis, making operational resilience during active compromise more strategically important than prevention alone. The Volt Typhoon context is especially significant because that campaign's documented purpose is strategic positioning rather than immediate disruption: US officials assess it is designed to activate across multiple infrastructure sectors simultaneously if military conflict over Taiwan escalates, creating compounding cascading failures at exactly the moment US military logistics would be most dependent on civilian networks. CISA currently cannot compel private critical-infrastructure operators to comply with CI Fortify requirements, and implementation will depend entirely on voluntary participation unless Congress passes mandatory cybersecurity standards legislation analogous to the Transportation Security Administration's emergency pipeline security rules issued after the 2021 Colonial Pipeline attack. Organizations with legacy operational technology—utilities, water treatment systems, and manufacturing—face the steepest compliance burden because OT isolation requires physical engineering interventions that cannot be achieved through software updates and may require costly equipment replacement.

## 7. Hackers compromise Daemon Tools in global supply-chain attack, researchers say
**Source:** therecord.media | **Topic:** tech_law
**Link:** https://therecord.media/hackers-compromise-daemon-tools-global-supply-chain-attack

**Lead:** Suspected Chinese-linked attackers embedded backdoors in official Daemon Tools Lite installers distributed through the software's own website beginning around April 8, 2026, compromising thousands of machines across more than 100 countries before Disc Soft patched the breach within 12 hours of notification.

**Context:** Attackers compromised the official distribution channel for Daemon Tools Lite, a widely used Windows disk-imaging utility produced by Latvian developer Disc Soft, inserting backdoors into installer versions 12.5.0.2421 through 12.5.0.2434. The malicious code embedded itself in core components that executed automatically at system startup, providing persistent access across all affected machines. Kaspersky researchers, who discovered and disclosed the attack, identified Chinese-language elements in the malware but stopped short of attributing the campaign to a specific Chinese threat group. Most of the thousands of infected systems across more than 100 countries—including Russia, Brazil, Turkey, Spain, Germany, and China—received only a basic system-profiling payload for reconnaissance and victim triage; a select few organizations in government, science, manufacturing, and retail sectors in Russia, Belarus, and Thailand received a more sophisticated backdoor delivering the Quic RAT implant. Disc Soft addressed the compromise within 12 hours of notification and released patched versions, recommending all users update immediately.

**Why it matters:** Software supply-chain attacks through official distribution channels are particularly corrosive to security because they exploit the reasonable trust users place in downloading software from a vendor's own website—precisely the behavior that security training recommends. The Daemon Tools compromise joins a pattern of high-profile supply-chain attacks (SolarWinds in 2020, 3CX in 2023, XZ Utils in 2024) that demonstrate how single-point-of-trust distribution mechanisms can achieve nation-state-scale initial access without requiring any exploitable mistake from victims. The targeting pattern—broad initial infection globally, with sophisticated payloads reserved for government and research institutions in Russia and Belarus—is consistent with Chinese intelligence collection against states with ambiguous positions in the Russia-China relationship, particularly as Moscow's war in Ukraine has strained its economic dependence on Beijing. Any organization that installed Daemon Tools Lite between April 8 and approximately April 20, 2026, should treat those systems as potentially compromised and review logs for indicators of contact with known Quic RAT command-and-control infrastructure, as the basic-payload stage is designed specifically to identify high-value targets for escalated access.

## 10. Meta will use AI to analyze height and bone structure to identify if users are underage
**Source:** techcrunch.com | **Topic:** tech_law
**Link:** https://techcrunch.com/2026/05/05/meta-will-use-ai-to-analyze-height-and-bone-structure-to-identify-if-users-are-underage/

**Lead:** Meta announced it will deploy AI visual-analysis tools on Facebook and Instagram that scan photos and videos for physical markers of youth—including height and bone structure—to identify and remove accounts belonging to users under 13, raising novel questions about biometric privacy law.

**Context:** Meta announced on May 5, 2026, that it is deploying AI-powered visual analysis across Facebook and Instagram to detect users under 13 by scanning uploaded images and videos for physical signals including face size, height relative to surrounding objects, and bone structure. The company stressed that the system is "not facial recognition" and does not identify specific individuals, but rather extracts biometric-adjacent signals to estimate a user's probable age—a distinction it is treating as legally significant under state biometric privacy frameworks. If the system flags an account as likely belonging to someone under 13, Meta deactivates it and requires age verification before restoring access. The rollout builds on Meta's Teen Account initiative, which since 2024 has automatically enrolled hundreds of millions of users identified as under 18 in restricted platform experiences with parental controls, even when those accounts listed adult birthdays. The announcement came weeks after a New Mexico jury ordered Meta to pay $375 million in civil penalties for misleading consumers about children's safety on its platforms.

**Why it matters:** Meta's bone-structure scanning creates a novel regulatory question: whether AI inference of biological characteristics from images—height estimation, skeletal-structure analysis—qualifies as biometric data processing under state laws that do not require precise identity matching, such as Illinois's Biometric Information Privacy Act, Texas's Capture or Use of Biometric Identifier Act, or Washington's My Health My Data Act. Courts in Illinois have held that BIPA applies to probabilistic inference from physical characteristics in some contexts, which could expose Meta to per-violation statutory damages across millions of flagged user accounts if regulators or plaintiffs pursue that theory aggressively. The $375 million New Mexico verdict demonstrates that US courts are prepared to impose nine-figure damages for children's platform safety failures, creating strong incentive for the rest of the social-media industry to adopt similar AI-detection measures or face comparable litigation exposure from the dozens of state attorneys general who have active children's safety investigations. The EU's Digital Services Act additionally imposes strict necessity and proportionality obligations on Very Large Online Platforms processing potential child data, requirements that a probabilistic AI approach generating false positives against adult users may struggle to satisfy when regulators at the Irish Data Protection Commission—Meta's EU lead regulator—conduct their inevitable review.
