## [ai] Jury dismisses all of Elon Musk's claims against OpenAI and Sam Altman in under two hours

**Source:** https://www.pbs.org/newshour/nation/jury-sides-with-openai-saying-elon-musks-lawsuit-was-not-filed-on-time

**Lead:** A nine-member federal jury in Oakland unanimously dismissed all of Elon Musk's claims against OpenAI and CEO Sam Altman on May 18, deliberating for under two hours and finding that Musk's lawsuit — which alleged OpenAI betrayed its nonprofit founding mission — was filed outside the statute of limitations.

**Context:** The trial, which began April 27 before Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers of the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California, centered on Musk's claim that Altman and co-founder Greg Brockman had secretly steered OpenAI from its nonprofit mission of beneficial AI toward profit-making, while Musk — who co-founded OpenAI in 2015 and invested $38 million in its early years — was excluded from the transition. Musk sought damages payable to OpenAI's charitable arm, removal of Altman from the board, and an unwinding of the company's for-profit restructuring, which positions OpenAI for an eventual IPO at its current $852 billion valuation. OpenAI's lawyers countered at trial that no permanent nonprofit commitment ever existed and that Musk filed suit to seize control of the fast-growing AI developer, with OpenAI's attorney quipping, "Mr. Musk may have the Midas touch in some areas, but not in AI." Sam Altman testified that Musk had demanded "complete control" over the proposed for-profit arm in 2017 and discussed passing control to his children if he could not run it himself. Judge Rogers accepted the advisory jury verdict as the court's own judgment, dismissing all claims including a parallel claim against Microsoft for aiding OpenAI through $13 billion in investment commitments between 2019 and 2023.

**Why it matters:** The statute-of-limitations dismissal means the jury never reached the substantive question of whether OpenAI's nonprofit-to-for-profit conversion breached a charitable trust — a legal question that would have set significant precedent for how courts scrutinize governance transitions at AI research organizations. Judge Rogers signaled an appeal faces an uphill climb: "There's a substantial amount of evidence to support the jury's finding," she said, noting that reversing a factual limitations determination on appeal is especially difficult. The verdict clears the last major legal obstacle to OpenAI's restructuring and IPO path, reinforcing the model — increasingly adopted across the AI sector — of converting nonprofit research entities into profit-seeking corporations without judicial review of whether such transitions breach the founding charitable purpose. Musk's legal team reserved appeal rights but has not announced a timeline; xAI, Musk's competing AI company, continues to develop its Grok series, and the trial may have damaged his reputational position in AI policy circles regardless of the legal outcome.

## [law] Supreme Court grants cert in Crowther v. Board of Regents to resolve whether Title IX lets employees sue schools for sex discrimination

**Source:** https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/05/court-agrees-to-hear-case-on-ability-of-employees-to-bring-certain-suits-for-sex-discrimination-/

**Lead:** The Supreme Court agreed to hear Crowther v. Board of Regents of the University System of Georgia (No. 25-183), taking up a circuit split over whether employees — not just students — of federally funded educational institutions can bring private sex-discrimination suits under Title IX of the Education Amendments of 1972.

**Context:** The case was brought by an art professor and women's basketball coach at Georgia public universities who alleged sex discrimination by their institutions. The Eleventh Circuit dismissed the claims, concluding that Title IX's prohibition of sex discrimination in "education program[s] or activity receiving Federal financial assistance" indicates the statute protects students, not employees, and thus does not create a private right of action for school staff. This holding conflicts with rulings in other circuits that have permitted employee Title IX claims, leaving the scope of protection uncertain for teachers, coaches, administrators, and other educational staff in the states covered by the Eleventh Circuit — Alabama, Florida, and Georgia. Title IX has historically been enforced primarily in student-facing contexts involving sexual harassment, assault, and gender equity in athletics, but its statutory text does not expressly exclude employees from its protections. The Court separately declined to hear Doe v. X Corp., a lawsuit against X (formerly Twitter) regarding child pornography reporting obligations on the platform.

**Why it matters:** The Court's ruling will determine whether employees of approximately 18,000 federally funded educational institutions can bring Title IX claims in addition to — or instead of — Title VII claims, which carry different procedural requirements, damages caps, and administrative prerequisites. If the Eleventh Circuit's restrictive reading prevails nationwide, affected employees will be confined to Title VII's framework, potentially losing access to Title IX's distinct remedial structure and the ability to challenge institutional culture directly under the education-focused statute. If the Court reverses, it would open a significant new litigation pathway that could increase exposure for colleges and K-12 districts, and would likely prompt Congress to revisit the legislative history of Title IX to clarify congressional intent. The case also fits into the Roberts Court's ongoing reassessment of implied private rights of action under spending-clause statutes — a doctrinal area with broad implications beyond Title IX — and will be argued in the Court's fall 2026 term.

## [law] Supreme Court refuses to reinstate Virginia's Democratic redistricting map, locking the state into an existing map for the 2026 midterms

**Source:** https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/05/justices-decline-to-reinstate-virginia-map/

**Lead:** The Supreme Court on May 15 declined, in an unsigned order, to reinstate a new Virginia congressional redistricting map that Virginia Democrats argued would strongly favor their party, ensuring that the state's 2026 midterm elections will proceed under the currently blocked map that Republicans contend is more favorable to them.

**Context:** Virginia Democrats and Attorney General Jay Jones sought emergency Supreme Court intervention after a lower court blocked the new congressional map from taking effect ahead of the 2026 midterm cycle. The new map would have significantly altered the competitive landscape for Virginia's 11 House seats, and Democrats characterized its blockage as an electoral injustice. Governor Abigail Spanberger called the outcome a "nullification of an election" and accused the justices of disregarding millions of voters; Jones described it as "yet another profoundly troubling example of the continued national attack on voting rights." The Court's order provides no reasoning, which is standard practice for emergency applications, leaving the precise legal basis for the denial unclear. The decision comes in the immediate aftermath of the Court's April 29 ruling in Louisiana v. Callais, which struck down a majority-Black congressional district as an unconstitutional racial gerrymander and which the Court subsequently moved to immediately finalize — bypassing the normal 32-day waiting period — to allow Louisiana to redraw its map in time for 2026 elections.

**Why it matters:** The Virginia denial, combined with the Louisiana ruling, produces an asymmetric redistricting landscape: maps drawn to protect minority voter representation under the Voting Rights Act were struck down in Louisiana, while a Democratic remedy map was blocked in Virginia, both outcomes tending to preserve configurations that benefit Republican incumbents. With House control potentially turning on a handful of seats nationally, the practical electoral impact of these redistricting orders may prove decisive in 2026. The lack of reasoning in the Virginia order makes it difficult to predict how the Court would rule on similar emergency redistricting applications in other states — Pennsylvania, Michigan, and North Carolina all have contested map litigation currently in various stages. For the VRA's future, the combination of the Louisiana merits decision and the Virginia procedural denial suggests the Court is not inclined to serve as a backstop for minority-representation or Democratic-advantaging maps, leaving the path for future VRA enforcement through Section 2 expensive, slow, and electorally uncertain.

**Lead:** Grafana Labs, which serves more than 7,000 enterprise customers, refused to pay an undisclosed ransom after the criminal group CoinbaseCartel used a stolen GitHub API token to exfiltrate the company's complete source codebase, though no customer data was compromised in the breach.

**Context:** CoinbaseCartel, a data theft group that emerged in September 2025 as an offshoot of the Scattered Lapsus$ Hunters (SLSH) cybercriminal collective, publicly claimed the breach on a Friday, announcing it had obtained Grafana's complete codebase via a compromised GitHub access token. Grafana issued a statement Saturday night confirming the breach, stating the company had invalidated the stolen credentials and implemented additional security measures while investigating the credential leak's root cause. The group has targeted more than 100 companies since September using stolen credentials and social engineering — rather than traditional ransomware encryption — a theft-and-extortion model that avoids the operational complexity of ransomware deployment while providing equivalent financial leverage. Grafana's open-core observability platform is embedded in cloud monitoring infrastructure across major enterprises; the company's 7,000+ customer base spans sectors from financial services to healthcare and critical infrastructure. The company cited longstanding FBI guidance that paying ransoms provides no guarantee of non-disclosure and actively incentivizes further attacks as the basis for its refusal.

**Why it matters:** Source code exposure creates secondary risk that customer data theft does not: bad actors with Grafana's proprietary codebase can identify zero-day vulnerabilities in Grafana deployments across thousands of enterprise environments, potentially enabling exploitation at scale months or years before any patch cycle can catch up, without any disclosure obligation to the affected enterprises. Grafana's decision to refuse payment — and to publicly commit to a post-incident review — is notable in an infrastructure software sector with a mixed record on ransom non-payment, where the fear of customer-impacting downstream exploitation creates pressure to pay even when FBI guidance counsels against it. The breach method — GitHub API token compromise via social engineering — is increasingly the attack vector of choice for sophisticated threat actors, reflecting a broader shift from brute-force network intrusion to targeted manipulation of developer toolchains, CI/CD pipelines, and code repository credentials. Organizations that have Grafana deeply integrated in their monitoring stack should treat this as a prompt to audit third-party access token permissions and implement token rotation policies while Grafana completes its root-cause investigation.

## [privacy] OpenAI rolls out ChatGPT financial account integration via Plaid and Intuit; cybersecurity experts warn of account takeover risk

**Source:** https://therecord.media/experts-warn-of-privacy-cyer-risks-ai-finance

**Lead:** OpenAI is rolling out a ChatGPT feature allowing users to connect financial accounts at 12,000+ institutions — including Bank of America, Charles Schwab, and American Express — through Plaid and Intuit integrations, prompting warnings from cybersecurity experts that the consolidation creates a high-value target for account takeover with no fiduciary obligations or regulatory oversight equivalent to licensed financial advisors.

**Context:** The feature, positioned as a personal finance assistant, would give ChatGPT read access to account balances, transaction histories, investment portfolios, and spending patterns across all linked institutions simultaneously. Integration partners include Plaid — the dominant bank-to-app connector embedded in thousands of fintech applications — and Intuit, which handles tax preparation, accounting, and personal finance for millions of U.S. consumers. Ridhi Shetty of the Center for Democracy and Technology noted that financial data reveals "deeply personal details about a person's life, habits, vulnerabilities, and relationships," and raised concerns that OpenAI's terms of service leave ambiguous whether the data could be used for advertising or commercial targeting beyond the stated financial advice function. Diana Kelley, CISO at Noma Security, identified the consolidated financial picture as an unusually rich target: "A single account takeover could expose a detailed net worth and spending map" spanning balances, credit, investments, and debts across multiple institutions. Ram Varadarajan, CEO of Acalvio, described the single-platform concentration as creating a "high-value target for account takeover attacks" unlike the distributed risk profile of users managing accounts individually across separate apps.

**Why it matters:** The regulatory gap is striking — licensed financial advisors are fiduciaries subject to SEC and FINRA oversight with enforceable duties of care, but ChatGPT providing financial analysis faces none of those requirements. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau has authority over data aggregators like Plaid under Section 1033 of Dodd-Frank, but enforcement of those rules in AI-integrated contexts is untested and the CFPB's current enforcement posture is constrained. In Europe, the EU's AI Act and GDPR create significant friction for this model — financial data qualifies as sensitive personal data requiring explicit consent and purpose limitation — but U.S. users currently have far weaker protections. If a major breach or unauthorized data use materializes, the likely legal consequence is class action litigation testing whether OpenAI's terms adequately disclose the scope of financial data processing and whether the company assumed implied fiduciary obligations. The broader pattern — AI assistants gaining access to email, calendars, health records, and now financial accounts — is moving consumer AI from an advice tool to a comprehensive personal data consolidator operating largely outside the regulatory frameworks designed to govern each individual data category.

## [tech law] AI chatbots and suicide prevention: why blunt liability rules may eliminate the mental health resource marginalized users rely on most

**Source:** https://www.techdirt.com/2026/05/18/we-need-a-more-serious-discussion-about-suicide-and-ai-chatbots/

**Lead:** A Techdirt analysis by Jess Miers argues that legislative proposals imposing strict liability on AI chatbots for suicide-adjacent interactions misread the evidence on chatbot harm and benefit, and risk driving platforms away from mental health engagement entirely — depriving marginalized communities who distrust traditional crisis resources of their most accessible support option.

**Context:** The piece responds to a John Oliver Last Week Tonight segment characterizing AI chatbots as exploiting vulnerable users and driving suicide harm, arguing the critique oversimplifies a complex evidence picture. A Brown University study found that one in eight young people now use AI chatbots for mental health purposes, and over 90% of those respondents described their interactions as helpful. Thirty Replika users reportedly credited the chatbot with saving their lives; OpenAI has introduced a "trusted contact" feature allowing users to designate emergency contacts as a voluntary safeguard. Current legislative proposals include California SB243 — which would require chatbots to break character in mental health-adjacent conversations — and expanded liability frameworks triggered when users in distress have negative outcomes. The Adam Raine case, where a user reportedly bypassed ChatGPT safety protections before dying by suicide and which has been cited in Congressional hearings, illustrates the difficulty of drawing causal lines in chatbot-adjacent harm; Miers argues it reflects the limits of blunt restrictions rather than their necessity. Joseph Weizenbaum observed in 1966 that people share intimate thoughts with machines more readily than with humans — a dynamic that has proved durable and that researchers argue produces genuine therapeutic benefit.

**Why it matters:** Liability rules that mandate character-breaking, mandatory reporting, or police-contact triggers create an impossible set of tradeoffs: each intervention that reduces one type of risk increases another, because the populations most likely to rely on AI for mental health support — LGBTQ+ youth, communities of color with deep distrust of emergency services, rural users with no local therapist access — are also the populations most likely to disengage when chatbot interactions trigger institutional responses. Unlike defamation or product safety liability, where platforms can limit exposure by removing content, AI chatbot liability rules will shape conversation behavior in real time and cannot easily be calibrated to individual user risk profiles. The FTC, the FCC, and multiple state attorneys general are pursuing different regulatory frameworks simultaneously with no federal preemption, creating substantial uncertainty for AI developers trying to build responsible mental health features without assuming unlimited liability. The most likely near-term effect of punitive liability — OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google deploying blanket restrictions on mental health topic engagement — may produce worse outcomes for the most vulnerable users than the unregulated status quo, a dynamic that legislators focused on visible harm cases tend not to foreground.

**Context:** The WHO declared the PHEIC on May 17, activating international response mechanisms for an outbreak caused by the Bundibugyo strain of Ebola — one of the four orthoebolaviruses that cause disease in humans, and historically less lethal than the Zaire strain responsible for the catastrophic 2014–2016 West Africa outbreak. The DRC health minister warned of a "very high" lethality rate as the confirmed death toll hit 80 on May 16 before climbing past 100 by May 18; 246 suspected cases alongside only 8 confirmed laboratory cases reflect the severe diagnostic capacity constraints in Ituri Province, where armed conflict has restricted health worker access. Three of the six Americans faced what the CDC classified as high-risk exposure, and one is experiencing symptoms consistent with Ebola disease; the U.S. government is attempting to arrange medical evacuation to a facility where the individuals can be safely quarantined and tested. No Ebola cases from this outbreak have been confirmed in the United States, and the CDC assessed the risk to the American public as low. The outbreak's location in Ituri Province is particularly challenging: the region has experienced armed conflict and multiple Ebola outbreaks since 2018, complicating contact tracing, vaccination campaigns, and safe burial practices.

**Why it matters:** The PHEIC declaration activates international funding and coordination mechanisms including emergency WHO contingency reserves and donor-country response commitments, but the conflict environment in Ituri Province will constrain what rapid-response measures can practically achieve — the same factors that repeatedly extended earlier DRC outbreaks. Six American exposures, including a symptomatic individual, will likely accelerate CDC deployment of a dedicated response team and may trigger a domestic U.S. policy conversation about travel health advisories and medical evacuation protocols. The Bundibugyo strain's historically lower case fatality rate — approximately 25–36%, compared to the Zaire strain's 40–90% — is meaningful context for risk assessment, though it provides limited reassurance when the outbreak is active in an area where surveillance and treatment capacity are severely limited. From a global health governance perspective, this PHEIC will serve as an early test of whether the post-COVID reforms to international health response infrastructure — including WHO's Pandemic Accord negotiations and reformed emergency procedures — produce faster international coordination than the delayed responses that characterized both the 2014–2016 Ebola outbreak and the COVID-19 pandemic's early weeks.
