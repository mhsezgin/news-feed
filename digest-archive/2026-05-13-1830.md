## [law] South Carolina Supreme Court unanimously overturns Alex Murdaugh's murder convictions, orders new trial

**Source:** BBC | **Link:** https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cedpvnq7wn1o

**LEAD:** The South Carolina Supreme Court ruled 5-0 on Wednesday that disgraced attorney Alex Murdaugh did not receive a fair trial because court clerk Becky Hill improperly communicated with jurors about his credibility, ordering a new trial for the murders of his wife and son.

**CONTEXT:** Alex Murdaugh, the once-prominent South Carolina attorney convicted in March 2023 of murdering his wife Maggie and younger son Paul at the family hunting property in June 2021, had his double-murder convictions vacated by the state's highest court. The court found that trial clerk Becky Hill had made improper communications to jurors suggesting that Murdaugh's testimony could not be trusted — a finding the justices called a violation of the defendant's right to a fair trial by an impartial jury: Hill had "placed her fingers on the scales of justice." The court also ruled that the trial judge erred in allowing extensive evidence of Murdaugh's financial crimes — including allegations he stole approximately $12 million from clients over years — to be introduced in the murder trial, finding the prejudice outweighed the probative value. Murdaugh, 58, is not free: he is serving a 40-year federal sentence and a concurrent 27-year state sentence for the financial crimes, to which he pleaded guilty separately, and will remain incarcerated regardless of the murder retrial's outcome. Prosecutors announced they intend to retry him on the murder charges, though no date has been set.

**WHY IT MATTERS:** The reversal is significant on two legal grounds, each of which has implications beyond this case. The clerk-misconduct holding is relatively rare in American jurisprudence — courts generally presume jurors follow instructions — and the unanimity of the South Carolina Supreme Court's ruling signals the conduct was serious enough to overcome that presumption; it will likely prompt procedural reforms in how court staff interact with deliberating juries. The ruling on financial-crimes evidence is also significant: courts often admit "motive" evidence from a defendant's other wrongdoing, and the South Carolina court's willingness to find that evidence improperly tilted the trial may constrain future prosecutorial tactics in high-profile white-collar-linked criminal trials. For the retrial, prosecutors face the challenge of winning a conviction without evidence that proved so inflammatory the high court excluded it; Murdaugh's guilt or innocence on the murders remains a live legal question despite his other guilty pleas.

## [cybersecurity] UK to overhaul 1990 Computer Misuse Act with new statutory defenses for security researchers

**Source:** The Record | **Link:** https://therecord.media/uk-moves-to-shield-security-researchers-cybercrime

**LEAD:** The British government announced plans to reform the Computer Misuse Act 1990 for the first time, adding statutory defenses for good-faith security researchers whose vulnerability research and penetration testing work has faced legal uncertainty under the 36-year-old law.

**CONTEXT:** The Computer Misuse Act, passed in 1990 when the internet was barely a commercial reality, makes unauthorized access to computer systems a criminal offense — a broad prohibition that has long created legal ambiguity for cybersecurity professionals whose legitimate defensive work involves probing systems for weaknesses. The British government's proposed reforms, unveiled in briefing documents alongside the King's Speech marking a new parliamentary session, would introduce statutory defenses for good-faith cybersecurity activities including authorized penetration testing, vulnerability research, and defensive threat intelligence gathering. The CyberUp Campaign, which has long lobbied for reform, had argued that security professionals "cannot be expected to defend the country with one hand tied behind their backs." The reforms would also introduce new "Cyber Crime Risk Orders" enabling courts to impose proactive restrictions on individuals who pose ongoing cyber threats — a shift toward preventive disruption rather than purely reactive prosecution. Draft legislation has not yet been published; the reform is expected to proceed through Parliament in the second half of 2026.

**WHY IT MATTERS:** This reform matters because the legal ambiguity in the existing CMA has had a chilling effect on the UK's security research community: researchers who discover vulnerabilities in British infrastructure or companies have sometimes avoided reporting them for fear of prosecution under a law that does not cleanly distinguish "unauthorized access for research purposes" from criminal hacking. The US Computer Fraud and Abuse Act has the same structural problem, and the UK reform will be watched closely by American researchers and advocacy groups as a model. The addition of Cyber Crime Risk Orders is also significant: it gives UK law enforcement a civil-law tool analogous to the injunctions used in gang-suppression law, enabling restrictions on cyber threat actors before they commit further offenses — potentially a useful tool against the growing population of known cybercriminals whose actions fall below the threshold for extradition or prosecution. UK cybersecurity firms and academic researchers are the immediate beneficiaries; the broader beneficiary is the UK's ability to attract and retain security talent that might otherwise avoid a jurisdiction where their work carries residual criminal exposure.

## [tech law] EU Commission president calls for minimum age law for social media as Digital Fairness Act takes shape

**Source:** The Record | **Link:** https://therecord.media/european-commission-head-pushes-restriction-teen-social-media

**LEAD:** European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen declared that discussions about minimum-age requirements for social media "can no longer be ignored," signaling that a legislative proposal could emerge by summer 2026 as part of the Digital Fairness Act, which will go beyond the DSA to target addictive design features.

**CONTEXT:** Von der Leyen announced that an expert panel would deliver recommendations within weeks and that a legal proposal could emerge by summer 2026, though the European Parliament must ultimately pass any legislation. The move reflects a significant acceleration of EU platform regulation: the Digital Services Act, which entered full force in February 2024, already requires platforms to protect minors from harmful content and conduct risk assessments, but it does not set a minimum age and relies on platforms' own enforcement. The forthcoming Digital Fairness Act would target what regulators describe as "attention-capturing mechanisms, complex terms, and subscription traps" — design patterns the Commission believes are deliberately deployed to maximize engagement at the expense of user wellbeing. Multiple EU member states are already moving independently: Spain, Greece, Norway, France, Denmark, Turkey, and the Netherlands are all considering or implementing age verification protocols. Separately, the Commission is investigating Meta for potential DSA violations involving insufficient protections for minors on Instagram and Facebook, and a separate probe is examining xAI's Grok nudification capabilities.

**WHY IT MATTERS:** A Commission-level age restriction law would be the EU's most aggressive intervention into social media platform design to date, going beyond content moderation to regulate the fundamental architecture of recommendation and engagement systems. The legal instrument will likely require age verification at the platform level — a technically and privacy-controversial mechanism, since effective age verification requires collecting identity data that itself creates privacy risks. Privacy experts including the IAPP's Isabelle Roccia have noted that Commission-level action is important precisely to prevent fragmentation: if seven member states implement seven different age verification regimes, the resulting compliance burden for platforms could paradoxically harm the single market. For tech companies, the combination of the DSA's existing obligations, the forthcoming DFA, and active investigations into Meta and xAI signals that the EU is moving from "platforms must assess risks" to "platforms must demonstrate specific design outcomes" — a regulatory posture that will require significant product changes, not just policy updates.

## [ai] Meta launches end-to-end encrypted 'Incognito Chat' for AI on WhatsApp with no server logs

**Source:** TechCrunch | **Link:** https://techcrunch.com/2026/05/13/whatsapp-adds-an-incognito-mode-in-meta-ai-chats/

**LEAD:** Meta CEO Mark Zuckerberg announced Incognito Chat for Meta AI on WhatsApp, claiming it is "the first major AI product where there is no log of your conversations stored on servers," with all AI inference running inside a Trusted Execution Environment that prevents even Meta from reading the messages.

**CONTEXT:** Meta's Incognito Chat is designed to differentiate itself from every previous AI "incognito" implementation, which typically means conversations are not saved to a user's history but remain visible to the platform for safety and compliance purposes. Under Meta's Private Processing system, AI inference — the computational step where the model generates a response — occurs inside a Trusted Execution Environment (TEE), a hardware-enforced secure enclave where the code runs in isolation and the data is inaccessible even to Meta's own infrastructure. Messages in transit are end-to-end encrypted (as all WhatsApp messages already are), and once the AI session ends, conversation data is deleted from the TEE without any persistent log. Zuckerberg characterized it as offering "complete privacy" and the company published a technical description of the TEE architecture on about.fb.com alongside the announcement. The feature is rolling out on WhatsApp and the Meta AI app "over the coming months."

**WHY IT MATTERS:** If Meta's technical claims hold under independent scrutiny, Incognito Chat represents a genuine architectural departure from how every major AI assistant currently operates: all other systems — ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, Copilot — retain server-side logs by default, even when offering "incognito" modes that simply exclude conversations from history displays. The use of TEEs for AI inference has been theoretically proposed but not previously deployed at consumer scale by a major AI provider. The privacy and regulatory implications are significant: under GDPR, data that is never stored never needs to be retained, audited, or produced in response to law enforcement requests — which means Incognito Chat could become attractive to users in regulated industries (healthcare, legal, financial) and in jurisdictions with aggressive surveillance laws. The credibility of Meta's privacy claims will depend on independent security audits of the TEE implementation; TEEs have previously been shown to have side-channel vulnerabilities, and Zuckerberg's announcement pre-dates any such audit.

## [law] SCOTUS to consider whether California can force religious preschools to post notices about other faiths

**Source:** SCOTUSblog | **Link:** https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/05/can-the-state-force-religious-preschools-to-promote-other-religions-/

**LEAD:** Three California churches have asked the Supreme Court to hear Foothills Christian Ministries v. Johnson, challenging a state law requiring licensed childcare centers — including religious preschools — to post notices informing parents that children are free to attend religious services of any faith, on Free Exercise and compelled-speech grounds.

**CONTEXT:** California's childcare licensing statute requires all licensed childcare centers to post signs and distribute written notices telling parents that children are "free to attend religious services or activities of his/her choice" and are entitled to visits from spiritual advisors of their own choice. Three churches operating preschools under California licensing — Foothills Christian Ministries and two co-petitioners — argue that requiring them to post this notice violates the Free Exercise Clause (by compelling them to facilitate access to other religions) and the Free Speech Clause (by compelling them to advertise religious alternatives to their own faith). The Ninth Circuit upheld the law, finding it neutral and generally applicable, and thus subject only to rational-basis review. The cert petition in case number 25-802 argues that the law fails even rational-basis scrutiny and that, under Fulton v. City of Philadelphia (2021), government licensing conditions that discriminate against religious exercise trigger strict scrutiny. California countered that the notices are factual commercial disclosures within the state's broad regulatory authority over childcare.

**WHY IT MATTERS:** If the Supreme Court grants cert, this case will sit at the intersection of two of the Roberts Court's most active doctrinal domains: compelled speech (where the Court expanded protection in 303 Creative LLC v. Elenis in 2023) and religious liberty (where Fulton, Espinoza, and Carson v. Makin have steadily expanded Free Exercise rights against government licensing conditions). A ruling in favor of the churches would significantly limit states' ability to condition professional licenses on speech that regulated entities find religiously objectionable — with obvious implications for healthcare providers, counselors, pharmacists, and others whose licenses carry disclosure or referral requirements that conflict with religious doctrine.

**LEAD:** Microsoft has already patched more than 500 vulnerabilities in the first five months of 2026 — including 173 in April and 137 in May — with its internal AI system MDASH autonomously discovering 16 vulnerabilities in this month's patches, four rated critical, without human researchers identifying them first.

**CONTEXT:** Microsoft's Security Response Center VP Tom Gallagher acknowledged that "Microsoft engineers and the wider security community alike are increasingly using AI to examine software more carefully" than was previously possible, explaining the acceleration in patch volume. The company revealed MDASH (Microsoft's internal AI vulnerability discovery system) as the engine behind the autonomous finds: when tested retrospectively against five years of known vulnerabilities in specific code components, MDASH recovered 96% of vulnerabilities in one component and 100% in another — a recovery rate that would be impossible for human-only review at the speed of modern software development. The surge is industry-wide: Apple addressed 52 vulnerabilities discovered using Anthropic's Project Glasswing AI system, Oracle shifted to monthly critical patches after AI tooling revealed previously undetected flaws in its database software, and Google released 127 Chrome fixes in a single day driven partly by automated analysis. **WHY IT MATTERS:** The AI-driven acceleration in vulnerability discovery creates a fundamental tension in cybersecurity: more vulnerabilities are being found and patched, which is good, but the rate of discovery now outpaces most organizations' ability to deploy patches before attackers find the same vulnerabilities independently. Security teams that were already struggling to keep up with monthly patch cycles now face the prospect of emergency out-of-band patches multiple times per month, and enterprise environments with complex change-management requirements cannot patch at the speed AI discovery demands. The MDASH results — 96-100% retrospective recovery of known historical flaws — also imply that a substantial backlog of unpatched vulnerabilities in legacy Microsoft code may be systematically surfaced in coming months, meaning the current surge may be a preview of years of accumulated technical debt being rapidly disclosed.

## [law] The government's Fourth Amendment double standard: race may justify a stop but must be invisible to the stopped

**Source:** SCOTUSblog | **Link:** https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/05/the-governments-fourth-amendment-double-standard/

**LEAD:** In United States v. Carter, a D.C. case involving a "firearm interdiction" stop of Black men, the federal government argues courts must ignore a person's race when determining whether they felt free to leave a police encounter — even as the government simultaneously argues in other cases that race can support reasonable suspicion for a stop.

**CONTEXT:** Professor Daniel Harawa's SCOTUSblog analysis identifies a structural contradiction in the federal government's Fourth Amendment positions across pending cases. In *United States v. Carter*, officers conducted a "firearm interdiction" stop of a group of Black men in Washington, D.C.; when a lower court considered the defendants' race as part of whether a reasonable person in their position would have felt free to leave — the constitutional test for a seizure — the government objected and sought a rule prohibiting courts from factoring race into that analysis. Yet in *Noem v. Vasquez-Perdomo* and related immigration enforcement cases, the government has argued explicitly that "race and ethnicity can be a factor supporting reasonable suspicion in appropriate circumstances." Harawa calls this "selective colorblindness": the government wants race to inform the officer's side of the Fourth Amendment equation — giving it evidentiary weight to justify stops — while demanding that race be invisible on the citizen's side, preventing courts from considering whether the racial context of the encounter made the citizen reasonably feel coerced.

**WHY IT MATTERS:** The legal question — whether race can be considered in determining whether a seizure occurred — directly affects the Fourth Amendment rights of millions of people who experience race-contingent policing, and a Supreme Court ruling adopting the government's position would constitutionalize selective colorblindness in a way that would be extraordinarily difficult to reverse legislatively. Empirical research on policing consistently documents that race shapes the police encounter in both directions: officers are more likely to initiate stops of Black and Latino individuals, and those individuals are more likely to feel that compliance is mandatory rather than voluntary. Denying courts the ability to account for race in assessing reasonable person standards effectively asks courts to pretend away documented social reality. The broader doctrinal significance is that this is precisely the kind of argument — "race cannot be considered by courts even when it shapes lived experience" — that conservative majorities on the Roberts Court have sometimes found persuasive, making it important to track how the Carter cert petition develops.
