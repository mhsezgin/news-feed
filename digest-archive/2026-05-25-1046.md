## [global politics] Russia GPS-jammed RAF jet carrying UK defence secretary

**Source:** BBC News — <https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/clyp0jrxv7ro>

**LEAD:** Russia electronically jammed the GPS systems of an RAF transport aircraft carrying UK Defence Secretary John Healey over the Baltic Sea, in what British officials described as a deliberate and dangerous act of electronic warfare against a NATO ally.

**CONTEXT:** GPS jamming and spoofing in the Baltic region has been documented since at least 2022, with Finnish and Estonian aviation authorities logging thousands of interference events affecting civil and military aviation. What distinguishes this incident is the confirmed identity of the passenger — a sitting cabinet minister on official travel — and the UK government's decision to publicly attribute the jamming to Russian military electronic warfare assets. The RAF aircraft was reportedly able to navigate using backup inertial systems, so no safety emergency arose, but the incident illustrates the routine nature of Russian electromagnetic aggression against NATO members in the region. Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania, Finland, and Poland have all raised this issue in NATO councils and called for countermeasures, including hardened civilian avionics standards and spectrum monitoring infrastructure.

**WHY IT MATTERS:** The public attribution by a NATO member government of a deliberate EW act targeting a ministerial aircraft marks an escalation in the information war around Russian hybrid tactics. It will add pressure on NATO to formalise a collective response doctrine for electromagnetic warfare, an area where alliance policy has lagged behind Russian operational practice. For cybersecurity and national-security law practitioners, the incident sits at the frontier of the laws of armed conflict as applied to peacetime hybrid operations — GPS jamming in international airspace is a legally grey act that does not clearly trigger Article 5, yet causes real harm.

**WHY IT MATTERS:** The S-1 is the first comprehensive look at SpaceX's financials for a company that has operated with unusual opacity for its scale. Investors will now debate whether the Starlink cash engine justifies a premium multiple that prices in Starship optionality, or whether the widening losses and Musk governance risk cap the valuation. ## [global politics] Ebola outbreak declared in DRC as cases spread

**Source:** NPR — <https://www.npr.org/2026/05/24/nx-s1-5833095/drc-ebola-africa>

**LEAD:** The World Health Organization and Democratic Republic of Congo authorities have declared a new Ebola outbreak in the country's conflict-wracked east, with initial cases identified in North Kivu province — a region where past outbreaks have proven exceptionally difficult to contain.

**CONTEXT:** The DRC has experienced more Ebola outbreaks than any other country, including the catastrophic 2018–2020 North Kivu outbreak that killed over 2,200 people and unfolded amid active armed conflict involving dozens of militia groups. The current outbreak was identified through genomic sequencing of samples from patients presenting with haemorrhagic fever symptoms in a remote area with limited road access. MSF and WHO rapid-response teams have been deployed, and ring-vaccination with the rVSV-ZEBOV vaccine (Ervebo) is under way for confirmed contacts. The complicating factor, as in 2018, is that North Kivu remains an active conflict zone — rebel groups including M23 (backed by Rwanda) control significant territory, restricting movement of health workers and contact-tracing teams. Air access to affected communities is the primary logistics channel, and fuel and security costs constrain response capacity.

**WHY IT MATTERS:** Even a contained outbreak in North Kivu commands global attention because of the region's history and the potential for cross-border spread into Uganda, Rwanda, and Burundi — all of which have porous borders and significant population movement. From a global health governance perspective, the outbreak tests whether the post-COVID reforms to WHO's Health Emergency Preparedness framework translate into faster, better-resourced field response. For global-politics watchers, any deterioration will put pressure on the fragile DRC-Rwanda diplomatic process and could complicate the M23 ceasefire talks.

## [cybersecurity] Belarus-linked GhostWriter campaign targets Ukraine with OysterFresh malware

**Source:** The Record — <https://therecord.media/oysterfresh-belarus-linked-campaign-targets-ukraine>

**LEAD:** Researchers have documented a sophisticated Belarus-linked cyber-espionage campaign — attributed to the GhostWriter threat actor — deploying a previously undocumented implant called OysterFresh against Ukrainian government agencies, media organisations, and civil-society groups.

**CONTEXT:** GhostWriter (also tracked as UNC1151) has been active since at least 2017 and is assessed with high confidence to operate at the direction of Belarusian state intelligence (GRU-Belarus or KGB alignment, depending on the analyst). The group is known for influence operations — fabricating documents attributed to NATO officials — but has increasingly paired information operations with technical intrusion campaigns since 2022. OysterFresh is a modular credential-harvesting implant delivered via spear-phishing emails with weaponised Office documents; it establishes persistence through scheduled tasks and communicates over HTTPS to actor-controlled infrastructure hosted on compromised European hosting providers. The campaign's targeting — Ukrainian MFA-adjacent organisations and journalists covering the war — suggests intelligence collection rather than destructive intent, though the infrastructure overlaps with historically disruptive GhostWriter operations.

**WHY IT MATTERS:** The campaign is significant for several reasons: it demonstrates continued investment by a NATO-border state in offensive cyber capabilities despite international isolation; it targets civil society alongside government, widening the threat surface for organisations that often lack enterprise-grade defences; and it provides new IOCs (indicators of compromise) that threat intelligence teams at NATO member governments should incorporate into their detection stacks. For cybersecurity law practitioners, the operation sits in the contested space of state-sponsored espionage that does not clearly cross the threshold of armed attack under international law but causes real harm to sovereignty and information integrity.

## [law] More Than a Trade Route: The Legal Stakes of the Strait of Hormuz

**Source:** Verfassungsblog — <https://verfassungsblog.de/more-than-a-trade-route-hormuz/>  
*(brief: full article body only partially accessible)*

**LEAD:** As US-Iran nuclear diplomacy stalls and Gulf tensions simmer, a new Verfassungsblog analysis examines the layered legal regime governing the Strait of Hormuz — arguing that its status under international law is both more complex and more fragile than the "freedom of navigation" framing typically used in Western policy discourse.

**CONTEXT:** The Strait of Hormuz is the world's most critical oil chokepoint, with roughly 20% of global petroleum and 25% of LNG passing through it daily. Under UNCLOS Part III, straits used for international navigation are subject to a right of "transit passage" — a stronger right than innocent passage, which Iran has historically contested. Iran is not a party to UNCLOS, relying instead on customary international law and bilateral agreements, and has periodically asserted the right to close the strait in conditions of armed conflict or as an economic counter-pressure measure. The Verfassungsblog piece situates the current diplomatic moment within this legal framework: any Iranian move to impede transit passage would trigger a complex calculus involving the laws of naval blockade, the UN Charter's prohibition on the threat or use of force, and the specific treaty obligations of states whose vessels transit the strait. The analysis draws on the 1988 Oil Platforms case (Iran v. United States, ICJ) and the 2010 MV Louisa case for precedent on the limits of self-help in maritime disputes.

**WHY IT MATTERS:** For international lawyers and policy practitioners, the piece is a timely reminder that the Strait of Hormuz is not simply a geography problem but a legal one — and that the applicable rules are contested in ways that matter enormously if diplomatic talks break down. A closure or interdiction scenario would immediately raise questions about the legality of naval escorts, mine-clearing operations, and retaliatory sanctions under both international humanitarian law and the UN sanctions framework. For readers tracking the Iran negotiations, understanding the legal stakes clarifies why both sides are playing for time and why incremental agreements (partial sanctions relief, enrichment caps) are more legally manageable than comprehensive settlement.
