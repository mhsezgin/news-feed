## 3. [cybersecurity] Dutch intelligence warns China has achieved parity with the US in offensive cyber capabilities

**Source:** [therecord.media](https://therecord.media/china-cyber-capabilities-match-us-dutch-intel-says)

**Lead:** The Netherlands' Defence Intelligence and Security Service (MIVD) declared that China now "probably stands on equal footing" with the United States in offensive cyber capabilities, the first time a NATO-allied intelligence agency has explicitly assessed Chinese cyber parity.

**Context:** The MIVD report found that Chinese-linked groups doubled their zero-day exploit use in 2025, making China the most prolific state-sponsored user of previously unknown vulnerabilities globally. In 2025, Chinese operatives gained router access at smaller Dutch ISPs as part of the Salt Typhoon campaign. The PLA's 2024 restructuring — dissolving the Strategic Support Force and establishing a standalone Cyberspace Force — enabled enhanced operational flexibility. The MIVD noted that detection and mitigation against Chinese operations are "often inadequate" and that only a small fraction of attacks against Dutch interests are ever discovered. China actively targets Dutch semiconductor, quantum computing, and aerospace sectors tied directly to Dutch ASML export controls.

**Why it matters:** An explicit "parity" assessment from a NATO-allied agency carries weight that US intelligence assessments cannot. The doubling of Chinese zero-day use is alarming because zero-days leave defenders no patch, no warning, and no remediation path. The PLA Cyberspace Force institutionalization means China has doctrinal cyber capacity comparable to US Cyber Command. For EU tech companies, the targeting of semiconductor and quantum sectors signals direct economic espionage tied to export control disputes. The assessment will accelerate European NIS2 compliance, push NATO to update its cyber deterrence posture, and justify expanded European intelligence sharing on Chinese threats.

## 4. [cybersecurity] TridentLocker ransomware hits Sedgwick Government Solutions, stealing data tied to DHS, ICE, CBP and CISA

**Source:** [therecord.media](https://therecord.media/sedgwick-cyber-incident-ransomware)

**Lead:** The TridentLocker ransomware gang claimed a breach at Sedgwick Government Solutions — a federal contractor serving DHS, ICE, CBP, and CISA — stealing 3.4 gigabytes of data and marking the newly emerged group's second known victim after Belgium's postal service.

**Context:** TridentLocker, a ransomware-as-a-service operation that emerged in November 2025, claimed the attack on New Year's Eve 2025; Sedgwick confirmed it in May 2026. The subsidiary provides claims and risk management services to DHS, ICE, CBP, USCIS, the Department of Labor, and CISA itself. Sedgwick stated the affected file transfer system was isolated from broader operations. Law enforcement and customers were notified and external cybersecurity experts engaged. TridentLocker's rapid escalation from debut to US federal contractor breach within weeks signals a sophisticated, well-resourced operation.

**Why it matters:** The breach of a CISA-serving contractor crystallizes a systemic vulnerability: federal contractors handling sensitive government data are not subject to the same cybersecurity standards as agencies themselves. Claims data tied to DHS and immigration agencies may include PII on asylum seekers and border incidents with life-altering consequences if disclosed. A 3.4GB exfiltration is small but ransomware groups retain data for follow-on extortion; full impact may not be known for months. This breach will likely be cited in congressional oversight and could accelerate efforts to extend FedRAMP-like requirements to contractors handling federal agency data — a gap that has existed since FedRAMP's 2011 creation.

## 6. [tech law] China's NPC reviews cybersecurity law amendments with fines up to ¥10 million and personal supervisor liability

**Source:** [therecord.media](https://therecord.media/china-cybersecurity-law-update-penalties-companies-executives)

**Lead:** China's National People's Congress is reviewing draft amendments to its Cybersecurity Law that would impose fines up to 10 million yuan on critical infrastructure operators whose incidents affect sensitive data and create personal financial liability for direct supervisors.

**Context:** The NPC Standing Committee received the draft amendment for review this week. Under the proposal, critical information infrastructure operators in transport, energy, and finance face maximum ¥10 million ($1.4M) fines for severe incidents. Selling uncertified cybersecurity products triggers fines of three times illegal gains; using non-certified products in critical sectors triggers ten times procurement costs. Storage of critical information on international cloud servers independently establishes liability. Individual supervisors face personal fines up to ¥100,000 ($14,000). Experts characterize the amendment as blending network defense with information sovereignty requirements.

**Why it matters:** The uncertified-product provisions effectively mandate domestically certified — typically domestically produced — cybersecurity technology in China's critical sectors, accelerating displacement of Western security vendors. Multinationals using global cloud providers face a concrete choice: compliance or operational efficiency. The personal liability provision mirrors EU NIS2 and US CIRCIA requirements, suggesting global convergence on individual accountability for cybersecurity failures. Data localization triggers will further fragment enterprise cloud architectures in China, reducing market share for AWS, Azure, and Google Cloud. If enacted, this is the most significant expansion of Chinese cybersecurity enforcement since the original 2017 law.

EU-specific disclosure obligations under NIS2 and the Cyber Resilience Act will point to ENISA's database, creating a dual-track disclosure burden for companies selling in both US and EU markets. The fragility of CVE's funding inadvertently highlights a governance failure: critical global cybersecurity infrastructure has been running on annual appropriations with no stable institutional home.
