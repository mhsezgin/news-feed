## 1. Too Dangerous to Deploy: Anthropic's Mythos and What Comes Next

**Source:** [Just Security](https://www.justsecurity.org/138011/too-dangerous-anthropic-mythos/)
**Topic:** tech

**Lead:** Anthropic's Mythos Preview — a frontier AI model that can autonomously exploit software vulnerabilities within hours of discovery — is the first AI system deemed too dangerous for public release, spurring the Trump administration to reverse course on AI oversight.

**Context:** On April 7, 2026, Anthropic announced Mythos Preview, a frontier AI model with hacking capabilities so potent the company declared it "too dangerous" for unrestricted public release. Mythos can autonomously identify and exploit previously unknown software vulnerabilities "potentially within minutes or hours of discovery" — capabilities that "surpass all but the most skilled human" operators, according to Anthropic. The company reported the model had already found thousands of high-severity vulnerabilities, including some in every major operating system and web browser. Rather than a standard commercial launch, Anthropic restricted access to approximately 50 industry partners — including Amazon, Apple, Cisco, Google, Microsoft, and NVIDIA — through Project Glasswing, a defensive cybersecurity initiative backed by $100 million in usage credits and $4 million in direct donations to non-profit security groups. The Pentagon had designated Anthropic a "supply chain risk" in March 2026, but Mythos's capabilities prompted Dario Amodei to meet White House officials; Trump told CNBC afterward that a defense deal was "possible." The Commerce Department's Center for AI Standards and Innovation subsequently announced expanded pre-deployment testing agreements with Google DeepMind, Microsoft, and xAI.

**Why it matters:** Mythos marks the first instance of a major AI lab voluntarily restricting a commercial model's release on capability-safety grounds rather than product readiness — a precedent the AI governance community has long advocated but never secured through voluntary commitments alone. The model's autonomous vulnerability exploitation compresses the patch-response window from the current weeks-to-months standard to potentially hours, rendering current patch cadence fatally inadequate for under-resourced institutions like hospitals, schools, and utilities. The Trump administration's shift from opposition to Biden-era AI oversight toward active model-testing frameworks signals that national security realities can override ideological hostility to regulation — but the resulting framework remains voluntary and fragmented, covering only companies that signed CAISI agreements. European allies were excluded from Glasswing's 50 founding partners, intensifying transatlantic tensions over AI access and defensive coordination. The key question: whether Congress codifies any pre-deployment evaluation requirement, or whether Mythos remains a one-time exception that the next generation of AI companies is free to ignore.

## 2. What we know about Iran's response to the latest US ceasefire proposal

**Source:** [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/5/8/what-we-know-about-irans-response-to-the-latest-us-ceasefire-proposal)
**Topic:** global_politics

**Lead:** Iran submitted a 14-point counterproposal to Pakistani mediators on May 10 demanding a complete end to the war on all fronts, rejecting the US requirement to halt uranium enrichment for twelve years and insisting on UN Security Council guarantees against future attacks.

**Context:** The United States' 14-point proposal demanded that Iran cease uranium enrichment for at least 12 years, surrender approximately 440 kg of uranium enriched to 60 percent, agree not to develop nuclear weapons, and reopen the Strait of Hormuz — in exchange for gradual sanctions relief, the release of billions in frozen Iranian assets, and withdrawal of the US naval blockade within 30 days. Iran's counterproposal, transmitted through Pakistan as the sole mediating channel, rejects the nuclear enrichment demand as non-negotiable and calls for UN Security Council guarantees against future attacks, complete sanctions lifting, war reparations, and a new maritime protocol for the Strait of Hormuz. Iranian lawmaker Ebrahim Rezaei called the US proposal "more of an American wish-list than a reality," while Parliament Speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf publicly mocked the negotiations on social media. Despite ongoing diplomatic exchanges, both sides traded fire in the Strait of Hormuz on May 8, and a drone struck a vessel off Qatar on May 10 as Iran warned the US against attacking its ships.

**Why it matters:** The core division is structural, not cosmetic: Washington wants to denuclearize first and end hostilities second; Tehran wants to stop the war on all fronts — including Lebanon — before any nuclear discussion. Pakistan's role as sole intermediary is itself a vulnerability: no European power, UN body, or Gulf state has been granted the same diplomatic channel, meaning any breakdown in Pakistani neutrality could collapse the talks entirely. The Strait of Hormuz closure continues to suppress global oil export volumes and disrupt supply chains across Asia and Europe. President Trump's declared "red line" on enrichment means any deal permitting continued Iranian enrichment would require Trump to accept a public retreat from his stated position. The Lebanon question is the hidden complication: Iran's counterproposal makes comprehensive war termination conditional on resolving Hezbollah's ongoing conflict, which Washington has treated as a separate track — but Tehran refuses to separate them.

## 3. But First, Implementation (Hungary Constitutional Repair)

**Source:** [Verfassungsblog](https://verfassungsblog.de/but-first-implementation/)
**Topic:** global_politics

**Lead:** Hungary's new Prime Minister Péter Magyar, whose Tisza party won a constitutional supermajority with 53% of the vote on May 9, demanded that President Tamás Sulyok and Chief Justice András Varga resign by May 31 or face removal, launching Hungary's most consequential attempt at constitutional repair since Orbán's 2010 consolidation.

**Context:** Hungary's 2026 parliamentary election saw Magyar's Tisza party secure 53.1 percent of the vote, translating into a constitutional supermajority — enough seats to amend the constitution without coalition partners. Magyar was sworn in as prime minister in early May 2026, ending Viktor Orbán's rule that began with a similar supermajority in 2010 and progressively restructured Hungary's judiciary, prosecutorial service, and public broadcasting to entrench Fidesz authority. In his inaugural speech to the National Assembly, Magyar demanded President Tamás Sulyok resign no later than May 31, and separately called on Chief Justice András Varga to do the same, calling both "puppets" of Orbán. Verfassungsblog's analysis notes a central paradox: Tisza holds enough power to fix the constitution, but reversing Orbán's changes while respecting European rule-of-law norms requires Magyar not to use the same majoritarian tools Orbán deployed. Hungary's Constitutional Court, Prosecutor General's office, and State Audit Office remain staffed by Fidesz-era appointees with fixed terms.

**Why it matters:** Hungary's case is the sharpest live test of whether a post-autocratic government can dismantle illiberal institutions without replicating the constitutional shortcuts that built them — the same challenge Poland faced after its 2023 election. If Magyar removes Sulyok and Varga unilaterally, he risks creating the same type of executive-over-judiciary precedent that EU institutions condemned Orbán for. If he waits for voluntary resignations, Fidesz-appointed officials retain enough institutional leverage to block his reform agenda. The EU's rule-of-law conditionality framework, which froze approximately €30 billion in cohesion funds to Hungary during the Orbán era, is now watching whether Magyar's government satisfies European Court of Justice standards in process as well as outcome. The May 31 deadline is the first concrete test.

## 4. The Dialectic of Viewpoint Discrimination (Germany/Israel Speech Law)

**Source:** [Verfassungsblog](https://verfassungsblog.de/the-dialectic-of-viewpoint-discrimination/)
**Topic:** law

**Lead:** On the anniversary of Germany's World War II surrender, the Bundesrat began debate on a Hesse-sponsored bill that would criminalize publicly denying Israel's right to exist with up to five years in prison, reigniting a fundamental constitutional debate about whether Germany's historical obligations can justify viewpoint-based speech restrictions.

**Context:** The proposed legislation would create a new criminal offence targeting anyone who "publicly or in an assembly denies the right of the State of Israel to exist or calls for the elimination of the State of Israel in a manner capable of encouraging antisemitic acts of violence or arbitrary measures." Hesse frames it as closing a legal loophole not covered by Holocaust denial laws, with penalties up to five years. The top administrative court in North Rhine-Westphalia ruled in November 2025 that questioning Israel's right to exist does not constitute an existing criminal offence. A virtually identical Hesse proposal failed two years earlier after leading legal scholars raised constitutional objections. The first Bundesrat reading on May 8, the anniversary of Germany's unconditional surrender, was deliberately timed to reinforce the legislation's framing.

**Why it matters:** The bill tests whether Germany's Basic Law Article 5 protections for free expression — which permit restrictions only through "general laws" that cannot be viewpoint-based — can accommodate a statute that explicitly criminalizes one political position. Holocaust denial laws passed the "general law" test because they target a narrow, historically specific deception; "denial of Israel's right to exist" is far more politically contested. The earlier Hesse bill failed because leading constitutional scholars argued it constituted viewpoint discrimination — the state criminalizing one side of a political dispute. If enacted, the law will almost certainly reach the Federal Constitutional Court. The European Court of Human Rights has struck down analogous laws in other Council of Europe jurisdictions under Article 10, meaning this statute would likely generate litigation spanning both national and European courts simultaneously.

## 6. Trump Uses DOJ to Try to Escape E. Jean Carroll Verdict

**Source:** [Techdirt](https://www.techdirt.com/2026/05/08/once-again-trump-looks-to-get-out-of-paying-e-jean-carroll-by-having-the-doj-substitute-in-for-himself/)
**Topic:** law

**Lead:** The Trump Justice Department filed a Supreme Court motion invoking the Westfall Act to substitute the federal government as defendant in the E. Jean Carroll defamation case — a maneuver three courts have already rejected — which would extinguish Carroll's $88.3 million verdict if the Court accepts it.

**Context:** Trump faces $88.3 million in combined judgments from two Carroll jury verdicts. The Westfall Act allows the federal government to substitute itself as defendant when a government employee has acted within official duties; if substituted, the case would be dismissed. Three separate courts rejected this argument: the Attorney General explicitly declined Westfall certification in 2023, courts ruled that waiver belongs to the United States as an entity; the 2025 attempt came post-trial and post-verdict, violating procedural timing requirements; and courts found "denying you raped someone is not part of the president's official job." A Second Circuit judge described the litigation as "deeply burdensome" while refusing en banc review for the third time. The matter now reaches the Supreme Court following its 2024 Trump v. United States presidential immunity ruling.

**Why it matters:** The 2024 Trump v. United States immunity decision created the legal environment in which this renewed Westfall argument becomes more plausible — the Court has demonstrated willingness to expand the scope of official presidential conduct. If the Court accepts the substitution, it would allow any president to escape personal civil liability by retrospectively invoking government immunity, extinguishing jury verdicts already rendered. The DOJ's willingness to re-argue a question courts have ruled waived suggests the government is treating successive administrations as separate legal actors who can relitigate finalized procedural decisions. Carroll would be left with an uncollectible verdict despite prevailing twice before juries. The case is now a direct test of whether the Court's 2024 immunity expansion extends into civil defamation law.

## 7. What I learned attending all those Supreme Court oral arguments this term

**Source:** [SCOTUSblog](https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/05/what-i-learned-attending-all-those-supreme-court-oral-arguments-this-term/)
**Topic:** law

**Lead:** SCOTUSblog's Amy Howe attended nearly every oral argument in the 2025-26 term and found a court under visible internal strain, with sessions running nearly double their scheduled length, justices speaking simultaneously in "face-offs," and public inter-justice conflicts spilling from courtroom into speeches and social media.

**Context:** Oral arguments in the 2025-26 term have nearly doubled their scheduled duration; Chief Justice Roberts has indicated he may revise the format this summer. Multiple justices now regularly speak simultaneously — Howe calls these "face-offs" — with junior justices expected by unwritten protocol to yield to senior colleagues. Attending in person revealed dynamics invisible through livestream audio: Justice Barrett physically waved off Justice Jackson's attempted interruption, and President Trump's attendance at the birthright citizenship argument — the first sitting president present — visibly changed the courtroom's tone. Women comprised only 30 percent of arguing lawyers in 2025-26, barely improved from 28 percent in 2016-17. Justice Alito expressed concern that arguments "can go on and on and on," and in April's session only one non-government lawyer argued before the Court for the first time among non-government counsel.

**Why it matters:** The practical effect of longer arguments is not more information — justices have already read full briefing — but heightened fatigue and diminished engagement during second arguments, a dynamic Howe documents explicitly. The public inter-justice conflicts from April 2026 — Sotomayor on Kavanaugh, Jackson on shadow-docket orders, Thomas's public response — are reflected in the in-person dynamics: a bench that visibly argues with itself and projects divisions that once stayed in the conference room. The Supreme Court bar's concentration — with nearly all non-government arguing slots held by repeat advocates — raises access-to-justice concerns. A format revision this summer could significantly alter oral argument as a legal institution, affecting not only the day-to-day courtroom experience but also the institution's signaling about how it weighs briefs versus live advocacy.

## 8. Musk v. Altman (AI Governance Analysis)

**Source:** [Verfassungsblog](https://verfassungsblog.de/musk-v-altman/)
**Topic:** tech_law

**Lead:** The Musk v. Altman trial, in which Elon Musk seeks Sam Altman's removal and demands OpenAI revert to its nonprofit structure, raises fundamental questions about whether charitable mission statements embedded in AI company charters create enforceable legal obligations that override subsequent commercial restructuring.

**Context:** The trial centers on OpenAI's conversion from a nonprofit to a capped-profit company and plans for full for-profit restructuring, which Musk alleges abandons the founding mission to develop AI "for the benefit of humanity." Evidence shows OpenAI had fallen from second to near-last among major AI companies on the 2025 Stanford Foundation Model Transparency Index; it announced advertising plans for ChatGPT in January 2026 and disbanded its alignment team in February 2026 despite repeated lawsuits. Shivon Zilis testified that Musk had attempted to recruit Altman to Tesla's board before their falling-out. The Verfassungsblog analysis by Teodora Groza argues the trial highlights a gap in European AI regulation: the EU AI Act sets technical safety requirements but creates no enforceable governance standards for AI organizations. Groza calls for mandatory internal safety boards with independent experts, regulator-appointed observers, and golden shares giving public authorities a direct governance stake.

**Why it matters:** The trial is the first judicial examination of whether a charitable mission embedded in a tech company's founding documents creates legally binding constraints overriding subsequent commercial restructuring — relevant to every AI company that has made public-benefit representations to regulators or investors. A ruling for Musk could affect every US benefit corporation and AI lab that has made comparable safety or mission representations. From the EU's perspective, the trial illustrates why the AI Act's technical risk classification is structurally insufficient: it can regulate what an AI system does technically, but not who decides whether, when, and at what safety threshold that system is deployed commercially. Groza's call for mandatory governance instruments goes well beyond anything in current EU or US law and is unlikely to reach implementing regulations before 2027. The case to watch after the verdict: whether any state attorney general intervenes to enforce charitable trust obligations against OpenAI's restructuring.
