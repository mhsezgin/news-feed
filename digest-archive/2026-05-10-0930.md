## 3. Russia Hacked Routers to Steal Microsoft Office Tokens

**Source:** krebsonsecurity.com | **Topic:** tech_law  
**Link:** https://krebsonsecurity.com/2026/04/russia-hacked-routers-to-steal-microsoft-office-tokens/

**Lead:** Russia's GRU-linked Forest Blizzard hacked more than 18,000 routers at peak activity in December 2025, modifying DNS settings without installing malware to intercept Microsoft Office authentication tokens from government agencies worldwide — defeating multi-factor authentication by capturing session tokens after users had already authenticated.

**Context:** Forest Blizzard, the GRU military intelligence unit also known as APT28 or Fancy Bear, exploited known vulnerabilities in consumer-grade MikroTik and TP-Link routers to silently redirect DNS traffic through attacker-controlled servers — all without installing persistent malware on the compromised devices. The campaign targeted more than 200 organizations and 5,000 consumer devices, with peak activity in December 2025 affecting over 18,000 routers; primary victims included government ministries of foreign affairs, law enforcement agencies, and third-party email providers. By hijacking DNS settings, attackers could perform man-in-the-middle interception of OAuth authentication tokens even from users who had completed multi-factor authentication — effectively defeating MFA by capturing the session token rather than the password. Security researchers noted the approach was deliberately "old-school": no malware on endpoints meant traditional endpoint detection and response tools were blind to the attack. The FCC announced on March 23, 2026, that it would cease certifying consumer-grade routers manufactured outside the United States, citing this class of national security threat.

**Why it matters:** The campaign reveals a fundamental gap in enterprise and government security: perimeter devices like consumer and SOHO routers are rarely monitored by security operations centers, rarely receive firmware updates, and are rarely included in threat detection frameworks — yet they sit directly in the path of all network traffic. By operating at the router layer, Forest Blizzard defeated MFA — the most commonly recommended organizational security control — without cracking any authentication system, simply by capturing tokens after successful authentication. This is a tactical evolution that shifts the attack surface from credential theft to session token interception, requiring different defensive responses including certificate pinning, encrypted DNS (DoH/DoT), and network-layer anomaly detection. The FCC's decision to restrict non-US router certification is the first major regulatory response and is likely to face legal challenge from foreign manufacturers, but the campaign provides the clearest evidentiary basis yet for treating home routers as national security infrastructure. The 18,000-router peak infection count suggests the campaign infected routers well beyond the primary government targets, using compromised households as unwitting relay infrastructure — a supply-chain-style attack that amplifies reach without requiring additional compromises.

**Lead:** In an unusual cluster of public conflicts across three weeks in April 2026, Supreme Court justices criticized each other's reasoning and character in speeches and writings, reflecting deep institutional tensions over emergency shadow-docket orders and the Trump administration's aggressive use of executive power.

**Context:** Three separate incidents within an eight-day window in April 2026 crystallized growing public dysfunction at the Supreme Court. Justice Sonia Sotomayor criticized Justice Brett Kavanaugh's solo concurrence in Noem v. Perdomo — an immigration enforcement case — during a University of Kansas Law School appearance on April 7, suggesting Kavanaugh lacked working-class empathy; she later issued a formal apology calling her remarks "inappropriate" and "hurtful." Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson, in a Yale Law School lecture on April 13, characterized approximately two dozen emergency docket orders favoring Trump administration positions as "scratch-paper musings" that "can seem oblivious and thus ring hollow," directly targeting conservative colleagues' reasoning. Justice Clarence Thomas responded on April 15 by lamenting that he had joined a court that no longer "dealt with differences as friends," and criticizing colleagues who recast themselves as "institutionalists" or "thoughtful moderates." Justice Elena Kagan separately warned that the emergency shadow docket was "never" designed to be used to circumvent established precedent such as Humphrey's Executor v. United States (1935).

**Why it matters:** Public inter-justice disagreements of this intensity are historically rare and reflect genuine institutional stress, not mere judicial temperament. The structural root cause is the emergency shadow docket: it allows the court to issue consequential orders without full briefing or reasoned opinions, which strips dissenting justices of their normal vehicle for public disagreement — a written dissent — and forces them into speeches and media appearances instead. The volume of Trump administration emergency applications has made this a routine dynamic, with major executive-branch actions on firing agency heads, immigration enforcement, and foreign affairs being governed by shadow-docket orders that provide almost no precedent for lower courts to apply. Sotomayor's apology is itself significant: she felt the institutional cost of being seen as attacking a colleague personally rather than legally, suggesting that even the most vocal critics recognize the reputational risk. The practical legal consequence is a court whose most important emergency decisions lack the reasoned analysis that would make them useful legal authority; what to watch is whether the court uses Noem v. Perdomo or a successor case to establish clearer emergency docket standards — the only structural fix available short of congressional action.

## 5. Justices Poised to Protect Generics Manufacturers in Skinny-Label Patent Case (Hikma v. Amarin)

**Source:** scotusblog.com | **Topic:** law  
**Link:** https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/04/justices-poised-to-protect-generics-manufacturers-from-liability-for-decisions-of-pharmacists-about/

**Lead:** The Supreme Court heard oral arguments in Hikma Pharmaceuticals USA v. Amarin Pharma on April 29 and appears ready to rule for the generic drug maker in a case that will determine whether generics companies face induced-infringement liability for pharmacist dispensing decisions — a ruling that could preserve or gut the 1984 skinny-label system that keeps generic drugs affordable.

**Context:** Hikma Pharmaceuticals USA v. Amarin Pharma, argued April 29, 2026, asks the Supreme Court whether a generic drug manufacturer can be held liable for induced patent infringement when pharmacists dispense its drug for off-label uses covered by the brand-name drug's patent — uses deliberately omitted from the generic's "skinny label" under federal law. The dispute centers on Vascepa, a cardiovascular fish-oil medication made by Amarin, and Hikma's generic equivalent. Amarin argues that Hikma's marketing of its product as a "generic version" of Vascepa — combined with publicly available data about Vascepa's patented heart-disease use — amounts to inducing physicians and pharmacists to infringe its patent. The Hatch-Waxman Act of 1984 explicitly created the skinny-label mechanism to allow generic entry for non-patented uses of a drug while a brand manufacturer retains patents on other uses; Amarin's theory would effectively nullify that mechanism by treating commercial context as inducement. The Federal Circuit ruled against Hikma in 2025, and the Supreme Court granted certiorari on January 16, 2026 — its first major patent case since 2023.

**Why it matters:** A ruling for Hikma would preserve the skinny-label pathway that keeps the generic drug market functioning: generics companies must be able to enter markets for non-patented uses without fear that pharmacists' off-label dispensing will expose them to patent liability they have no ability to prevent. A ruling for Amarin would functionally block generic entry for any drug with an important patented use — dramatically extending effective market exclusivity and reversing exactly the outcome Hatch-Waxman was designed to prevent. The stakes are enormous: the FDA estimates generics account for over 90% of all U.S. prescriptions filled, and brand manufacturers have used induced-infringement theories to block generics in cancer, cardiovascular, and neurological drug markets. The Federal Circuit's 2025 ruling against Hikma was already disrupting the generics industry by making it practically impossible to launch with skinny labels; a Supreme Court reversal would restore the pre-2025 status quo and create a clearer liability standard for the entire $150 billion generic pharmaceutical market. Justices across the ideological spectrum expressed skepticism of imposing liability for third-party conduct — a signal that the ruling is likely to be decisive rather than narrowly tailored.

## 6. German Federal Court Rejects Climate Cases Against BMW and Mercedes-Benz

**Source:** verfassungsblog.de | **Topic:** law  
**Link:** https://verfassungsblog.de/the-bmw-and-mercedes-climate-cases/

**Lead:** Germany's Federal Court of Justice dismissed two landmark climate cases against BMW and Mercedes-Benz on March 23, rejecting demands that the automakers stop selling combustion-engine vehicles after 2030 and drawing a constitutional line between judicial and legislative authority over climate policy.

**Context:** The German Bundesgerichtshof (BGH) dismissed suits brought by three private citizens who are also directors of the environmental advocacy group Deutsche Umwelthilfe (DUH), who sought a court-ordered ban on BMW's and Mercedes-Benz's global marketing of passenger cars with internal combustion engines after October 2030. The BGH's dismissal rested on three distinct legal grounds. First, the court held that potential climate harms from greenhouse gas emissions could not be attributed to the defendant automakers under established German attribution principles: liability as an indirect "disturber" requires breach of specific duties of conduct and a normative delineation of spheres of responsibility — criteria that general emissions contributions do not satisfy. Second, the court rejected the "personal emissions budget" theory: Germany has a national carbon budget, but no individual company has ever been assigned a share of that budget, making it legally meaningless to claim BMW or Mercedes "exceeded" an allowance they never held. Third, the court held that deciding when combustion engines should be phased out is a political question belonging to elected legislators, not judges. Deutsche Umwelthilfe announced it is reviewing the ruling before deciding whether to pursue the Federal Constitutional Court.

**Why it matters:** The BGH ruling is among the most significant judicial statements on corporate climate liability in Europe, establishing doctrinal barriers that will constrain similar litigation across German-law jurisdictions and inform courts in France, Austria, and Switzerland where comparable cases are pending. The attribution holding directly rejects the legal theory that underpins cases like Milieudefensie v. Shell — the 2021 Dutch ruling requiring Shell to cut emissions by 45% — suggesting that if the same theory were applied to German automakers in a German court, it would fail. The political-question doctrine applied here is narrower than its U.S. equivalent but performs the same function: shielding legislative prerogatives from judicial override on contested policy questions. That said, the DUH's potential appeal to the Federal Constitutional Court (Bundesverfassungsgericht) is the key variable: Germany's constitutional court issued a landmark 2021 climate ruling finding that inadequate climate protection can violate constitutional rights, and whether that duty extends to individual corporations remains unresolved. If the Bundesverfassungsgericht takes the case, it could produce a ruling that either entrenches the BGH's limits or breaks new constitutional ground on corporate climate duty.

## 10. LLMs Corrupt Your Documents When You Delegate

**Source:** arxiv.org | **Topic:** tech  
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.15597

**Lead:** A new DELEGATE-52 benchmark study of 19 frontier LLMs finds that Gemini, Claude, and GPT models corrupt an average of 25% of document content by the end of long agentic workflows, introducing silent errors that compound over time across 52 professional domains including coding, medical records, and music notation.

**Context:** The paper introduces DELEGATE-52, a benchmark covering 52 professional document domains, and evaluates 19 frontier LLMs including Gemini 3.1 Pro, Claude 4.6 Opus, and GPT 5.4 on their ability to faithfully execute delegated document editing tasks without corrupting content. The key finding is that even top-performing models averaged 25% document content corruption by the end of long workflows. The failure mode is "sparse but severe": models do not make frequent small mistakes but instead introduce occasional large errors — particularly difficult for users to detect because the surrounding content remains correct, and passive sampling of a document is unlikely to encounter the error. Larger documents, longer interaction chains, and the presence of distractor files all worsen performance. Notably, the study found that agentic tool use — giving models access to code execution, file systems, and browser capabilities — does not improve document integrity despite conventional assumptions that tool-augmented agents are more reliable. The research defines the core problem as a "trust deficit": delegation requires the expectation that the LLM will faithfully execute the task without editorial substitution, and silent corruption violates that expectation in ways passive review cannot catch.

**Why it matters:** Agentic AI workflows — where LLMs receive multi-step document editing, summarization, and analysis tasks — are the central enterprise AI use case in 2026, and legal, medical, financial, and scientific documents modified through AI pipelines that silently corrupt 25% of content would generate significant liability for organizations relying on those outputs. The specific finding that tool use does not help is important for product and safety assumptions: many AI system designers assumed that code execution or retrieval capabilities would make outputs more accurate, but corruption appears upstream of tool access in the model's text-generation behavior. The "sparse but severe" error pattern is particularly insidious from a quality-control standpoint: random sampling of an AI-edited document has low probability of encountering a sparse, severe mistake, while comprehensive review eliminates the efficiency rationale for AI delegation in the first place. For the AI safety research community, this maps onto sycophancy and instruction-following alignment challenges — models likely corrupt documents by making editorial substitutions they believe are improvements rather than deliberately diverging, suggesting the fix involves training on faithful reproduction objectives rather than helpfulness maximization. Organizations deploying AI for document workflows should implement automated diff-checking at every step and treat AI-edited documents as requiring human verification proportional to the stakes of the document type.
