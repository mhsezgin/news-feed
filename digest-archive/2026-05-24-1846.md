## [law] SCOTUS conferences Dershowitz v. CNN defamation petition, opening first formal vote on whether to revisit or abolish New York Times v. Sullivan after 62 years

**Source:** [scotusblog.com](https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/05/more-opinions-on-the-way/)


**The Supreme Court placed Dershowitz v. CNN on its conference list for a first cert vote on May 24, 2026, giving the justices a formal opportunity to decide whether to hear a case that explicitly asks the Court to abolish or substantially restrict the 62-year-old actual-malice defamation standard established in New York Times v. Sullivan.**


During Donald Trump's first Senate impeachment trial in January 2020, Harvard Law professor emeritus Alan Dershowitz argued on the Senate floor that a president cannot be impeached for conduct he genuinely believed was in the national interest. CNN on-air commentators subsequently characterized Dershowitz as arguing that a president could commit bribery or extortion without impeachment consequences -- a claim Dershowitz immediately and vigorously denied. Even the district court reviewing the defamation case found Dershowitz had 'said nothing of the kind' and one judge found CNN 'simply lied about what Dershowitz had said.' Nevertheless, both the district court and the First Circuit dismissed the defamation suit because Dershowitz failed to satisfy the actual-malice standard -- knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard for the truth -- established in New York Times Co. v. Sullivan (1964). Dershowitz's cert petition presents two questions: whether systematic omission of qualifying language from a recorded statement can constitute proof of actual malice, and whether the Sullivan standard 'should be discarded altogether or at least as to private citizens who are public figures.' The Supreme Court unusually ordered CNN to respond to the petition months ago -- a step taken in only a fraction of filed petitions -- and the case was scheduled for conference after CNN filed its brief in opposition and Dershowitz's team filed a reply brief. CNN argued that Florida law independently adopts the same actual-malice standard, meaning Dershowitz would lose on state grounds regardless of what happens to Sullivan.


**Why it matters:** New York Times v. Sullivan is the constitutional foundation of American defamation law for public figures and officials; overruling or materially restricting it would expose news organizations to defamation suits they currently win at the motion-to-dismiss stage based on plaintiffs' inability to prove subjective awareness of falsity. Justices Thomas and Gorsuch have each written separately in recent years questioning whether Sullivan was correctly decided as an original constitutional matter, providing a ready intellectual framework for reconsidering the precedent. If the Court grants cert -- even before reaching the merits -- media organizations, insurers, and editorial lawyers will immediately reassess risk across pending and future publications, creating predictable chilling effects on coverage of public figures. CNN's state-law fallback argument means Dershowitz may lose even if Sullivan is narrowed federally, but that does not protect the broader media industry from the signal a cert grant would send to future litigants and lower courts. The case arrives during a sustained White House campaign against press credibility and congressional proposals to reform defamation law, making the political stakes unusually charged: a ruling restricting Sullivan would be the most consequential shift in press-protection law since the decision itself was handed down.

## [law] Empirical analysis of SCOTUS oral arguments finds contested cases generate 28% more transcript pages and 46% more skeptical questioning -- a reliable real-time outcome signal

**Source:** [scotusblog.com](https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/05/what-oral-argument-behavior-reveals-about-supreme-court-unanimity-and-division/)


**A new empirical analysis of six October 2025 Term Supreme Court cases finds that contested decisions (5-4 or 6-3) average 93.7 transcript pages per argument versus 73 for unanimous cases, with 200 justice turns versus 137 and 46% more skeptical or probing exchanges -- a pattern consistent enough to serve as a meaningful predictive signal during argument.**


Researcher Adam Feldman analyzed six October 2025 Term cases divided into contested (5-4 or 6-3) and unanimous outcome groups. Contested cases averaged 93.7 transcript pages per argument, 200.3 justice turns, 35.0 skeptical turns, and 26.3 foundational probing questions. Unanimous cases averaged 73 pages, 136.7 justice turns, 24.0 skeptical turns, and 18.7 foundational probes per argument. Feldman specifically found that future dissenters typically ask more questions than eventual majority opinion authors -- consistent with the behavior of justices who need to surface and probe unfavorable facts to build a minority position. Justice Clarence Thomas was a notable exception to all patterns: he asked the fewest questions overall while authoring two majority opinions during the period studied, using what Feldman describes as 'exploratory or doctrinal' questioning rather than adversarial challenges when he did engage. In unanimous cases, justices' questions often functioned as road-mapping exercises -- helping advocates structure arguments that the Court would later rely on in its opinion -- rather than genuine probing of contested legal issues. Feldman's analysis describes the behavioral differences as reflecting justices 'working through disagreement' in real time during contested cases rather than merely eliciting clarification.


**Why it matters:** The research challenges the longstanding conventional wisdom that oral argument is unpredictable judicial theater and instead offers a behavioral framework for predicting case divisions in real time. For advocates preparing arguments in the October 2025 Term's remaining blockbusters -- birthright citizenship (Trump v. Barbara), presidential authority to fire independent-agency members without cause (Trump v. Slaughter, implicating the FTC and SEC), and consolidated transgender sports ban cases -- high volumes of skeptical questioning directed at particular justices would be a meaningful signal of where the divided Court is heading. If the empirical pattern is robust across additional terms, it has practical implications for how law firms and appellate strategists allocate preparation resources: contested cases demand advocates who can withstand sustained adversarial cross-examination, while unanimous cases reward advocates who can help the Court construct a workable doctrinal framework. The Thomas exception -- lowest questioning volume but highest majority-opinion authorship rate in the studied period -- raises a broader question about whether restrained questioning reflects a distinct leadership approach that will become more influential as Thomas approaches senior status on the Court.
