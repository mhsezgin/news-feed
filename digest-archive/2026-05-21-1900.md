## [law] Supreme Court rules 8-1 against cruise lines in Cuban confiscation case, exposing them to $400M+ in Helms-Burton Act damages

**Source:** [scotusblog.com](https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/05/court-rules-against-cruise-lines-in-cuban-confiscation-case/)

**Lead:** The Supreme Court ruled 8-1 in Havana Docks Corp. v. Royal Caribbean that four major cruise lines incurred Helms-Burton Act liability by transporting nearly one million passengers through Cuba's confiscated Havana cruise terminal between 2016 and 2019.

### Context
Havana Docks Corporation held the rights to operate Havana's cruise port terminal until the Cuban government confiscated the property in 1960. The 1996 Helms-Burton Act allows U.S. nationals to sue anyone "trafficking in property confiscated by the Cuban Government," but presidents continuously suspended the lawsuit provision from 1996 until 2019, when President Trump declined renewal. Between 2016 and 2019, four cruise lines — Royal Caribbean, Norwegian, Carnival, and MSC — transported nearly one million passengers through the docks, generating substantial revenue. A Miami federal district court awarded Havana Docks over $400 million in damages; the Eleventh Circuit reversed 2-1. In an opinion by Justice Clarence Thomas joined by seven colleagues, the Court held that "confiscated property" encompasses both the physical docks and all prior interests in them, and that any use of such tainted property triggers liability to those holding those interests. Justices Sotomayor and Kavanaugh wrote separately to flag concerns about unlimited recovery possibilities. Justice Elena Kagan was the sole dissenter, arguing that Havana Docks' expired leasehold interest should not qualify for recovery.

### Why it matters
The ruling significantly expands potential liability for businesses that have conducted commercial operations using formerly private Cuban assets. With hundreds of billions of dollars in confiscated Cuban property potentially qualifying for claims, the decision could ultimately affect cruise operators, hotel chains, energy companies, and any enterprise that has used assets seized since 1959. The 8-1 majority signals that Helms-Burton trafficking claims are to be read broadly — a major win for Cuban exile claimants and a significant compliance warning for global companies with current or historical Cuba operations. The case returns to lower courts for the cruise lines to raise remaining defenses, including damages-calculation arguments that will determine whether the $400 million award is ultimately reinstated. For legal and compliance officers, the ruling creates new due-diligence obligations before conducting Cuba-related commercial activity and may affect operations in other jurisdictions with comparable confiscation histories.

## [law] Supreme Court dismisses death-row IQ dispute 5-4, leaving Alabama inmate's Eighth Amendment intellectual disability protection intact

**Source:** [scotusblog.com](https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/05/court-sidesteps-death-row-iq-dispute/)

**Lead:** The Supreme Court dismissed Hamm v. Smith 5-4 as improvidently granted, leaving intact the Eleventh Circuit ruling that Alabama death-row inmate Joseph Smith is intellectually disabled under Atkins v. Virginia and cannot be executed.

### Context
Joseph Smith has been on Alabama's death row for over 20 years following his 1997 conviction for the robbery and murder of Durk Van Dam. Across multiple proceedings, Smith obtained five IQ test scores ranging from 72 to 78 — a borderline range at the center of a circuit dispute over how courts must handle multiple IQ results in Atkins claims. The 2001 Atkins v. Virginia ruling bars execution of intellectually disabled persons under the Eighth Amendment, but lower courts have disagreed on whether to average scores or credit the lowest reading. Alabama argued courts must consider the cumulative weight of all scores; the Eleventh Circuit ruled against Alabama and found Smith intellectually disabled. The Supreme Court granted certiorari to resolve the question but dismissed the case as "improvidently granted" — meaning at least five justices concluded it should not have been accepted — leaving the Eleventh Circuit ruling in Smith's favor undisturbed. Justice Sotomayor filed a 22-page concurrence explaining the dismissal; Justice Alito dissented in 24 pages joined by Thomas, Roberts, and Gorsuch, warning the dismissal will produce inconsistent outcomes across circuits.

### Why it matters
The dismissal delivers an immediate practical victory for Smith, whose execution had been pending resolution, and represents a significant moment in the ongoing tension between the conservative majority and liberal bloc on capital punishment. Alito's pointed dissent predicts that future borderline-IQ cases will be decided inconsistently across circuits and signals that the right-wing justices view current Atkins doctrine as too permissive — meaning the issue is likely to return to the Court in a future case. For capital defense attorneys, the ruling preserves a favorable framework for establishing intellectual disability through IQ score ranges without requiring a single definitive test result. The dismissal also reflects the Court's broader pattern of accepting high-profile constitutional questions and then retreating before issuing a ruling. Courts in the Fifth, Sixth, and Eleventh Circuits handling similar borderline-IQ cases will now navigate conflicting precedents without Supreme Court guidance.

## [law] NY Court of Appeals holds Justice for Injured Workers Act retroactive, blocking use of WCB rulings to defeat construction injury suits

**Source:** [nycourts.gov](https://www.nycourts.gov/ctapps/decisions/2026/may26/03217opn26.pdf)

**Lead:** New York's Court of Appeals held in Garcia v. Monadnock Construction (2026 NY Slip Op 03217) that the Justice for Injured Workers Act applies retroactively to all pending cases, barring defendants from using prior Workers' Compensation Board determinations as collateral estoppel to dismiss personal injury claims.

### Context
Plaintiff Waldy Quinones Garcia sustained neck and back injuries in a construction site accident on August 6, 2020, filing both a civil personal injury lawsuit and a workers' compensation claim. A Workers' Compensation Board panel denied his claim in October 2021, finding no causal relationship to the accident. The construction defendants moved for summary judgment, seeking to use that WCB ruling as collateral estoppel to foreclose Garcia's civil suit. The Justice for Injured Workers Act (JIWA), enacted as L 2022, ch 835 and effective December 30, 2022, created Workers' Compensation Law sections 11(2) and 118-a, explicitly barring courts from giving collateral estoppel effect to WCB findings in civil personal injury actions. Lower courts split on whether JIWA reached accidents and WCB proceedings predating the statute's enactment. The Court of Appeals resolved the split, holding JIWA applies retroactively to all pending cases, and ruled the defendants' collateral estoppel defense was "palpably insufficient and devoid of merit" — requiring denial of their summary judgment motion.

### Why it matters
This ruling is significant for New York personal injury practice, especially construction injury cases under Labor Law sections 200, 240, and 241. Before JIWA, employers and insurers routinely obtained favorable WCB causation rulings in administrative proceedings where injured workers often had minimal representation, then used those findings to defeat civil lawsuits, effectively letting an administrative system determine tort liability. JIWA was designed to sever that pathway, but its retroactive reach had been contested across hundreds of pending cases. By holding JIWA fully retroactive, the Court of Appeals has revived injury claims defendants sought to extinguish and restored injured workers' right to have causation decided by a civil jury. For defense counsel and insurers, the ruling eliminates a reliable early-exit summary judgment tool; for plaintiffs' attorneys, it reopens previously dismissed cases. Any dismissals on collateral estoppel grounds issued between JIWA's December 2022 enactment and today may be subject to reinstatement motions, potentially generating a significant wave of renewed litigation across New York state courts.

## [global politics] Paris appeals court convicts Air France and Airbus of manslaughter in the 2009 AF447 crash, reversing acquittal in France's worst aviation disaster

**Source:** [aljazeera.com](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/5/21/french-court-finds-airbus-air-france-guilty-of-manslaughter-in-2009-crash)

**Lead:** The Paris Court of Appeal found Air France and Airbus solely and entirely responsible for the June 2009 crash of flight AF447 that killed all 228 aboard, reversing a 2023 acquittal and imposing the maximum corporate manslaughter fine of 225,000 euros per passenger.

### Context
Flight AF447 departed Rio de Janeiro on June 1, 2009, bound for Paris with 216 passengers and 12 crew. Over the mid-Atlantic, a storm caused the aircraft's pitot tubes — sensors measuring airspeed — to ice over; receiving conflicting speed readings, pilots responded by climbing, causing the plane to stall and plunge into the ocean. Recovery of the black boxes required two years of deep-sea salvage. A Paris criminal court acquitted both companies in 2023, accepting the defense that pilot error was the proximate cause. Prosecutors appealed, and the Paris Court of Appeal reversed on May 21, 2026, finding both companies fully responsible. The court found Air France failed to provide adequate high-altitude stall recovery training — a deficiency the carrier acknowledged — and that Airbus had not addressed known pitot tube icing defects with sufficient urgency despite earlier safety alerts. The maximum corporate manslaughter penalty is 225,000 euros per victim; prosecutors described the total fines as largely symbolic given the companies' scale. Airbus announced it will appeal to the Court of Cassation, France's highest criminal court.

### Why it matters
The verdict carries legal significance on multiple levels. It reestablishes that aviation manufacturers and carriers can face criminal liability for systemic failures when they knowingly allow defects and training gaps to persist. France's successful appellate reversal of a corporate criminal acquittal is rare and sets a precedent for future major-disaster prosecutions. For global airworthiness regulators, the finding that Airbus bore criminal responsibility for pitot tube deficiencies — even after EASA and FAA mandated replacements post-crash — raises questions about whether post-hoc regulatory compliance is sufficient to defeat criminal liability when the manufacturer knew of the risk before the accident. Families of the 228 victims have pursued accountability for 17 years; the ruling delivers criminal recognition that no civil settlement could provide. Airbus's Court of Cassation challenge will focus on legal and procedural questions rather than factual findings, meaning the core liability determination is likely to stand regardless of outcome.

## [cybersecurity] European law enforcement dismantles First VPN, ransomware-enabling anonymizer featured in 'almost every major' Europol cybercrime case

**Source:** [therecord.media](https://therecord.media/europe-dismantles-first-vpn)

**Lead:** French and Dutch authorities with Europol seized 33 servers and obtained First VPN's complete user database on May 19-20, shutting down a service that marketed itself on Russian cybercrime forums and appeared in nearly every major cybercrime investigation Europol supported in recent years.

### Context
First VPN was a commercial anonymizing service that explicitly marketed itself on Russian-speaking cybercrime forums as uncooperative with law enforcement and operating outside any legal jurisdiction, making it a standard tool for ransomware operators, fraud rings, data thieves, and money-laundering networks. Europol described its presence as pervasive, appearing in "almost every major cybercrime investigation" the agency supported in recent years. France led the May 19-20 operation, supported by the Netherlands; 33 servers were dismantled across multiple jurisdictions. Ukrainian authorities questioned the service's administrator at France's formal request, continuing EU-Ukraine law enforcement cooperation despite the ongoing war. Europol's European Cybercrime Centre coordinated the operation. Investigators obtained First VPN's complete user database — a data trove that Europol's head of cybercrime Edvardas Sileris said would "open new cases globally" by generating investigative leads across multiple criminal enterprises simultaneously.

### Why it matters
The First VPN takedown dismantled shared criminal infrastructure rather than a single actor, simultaneously disrupting ransomware groups, fraud networks, and data theft operations with no direct connection to each other. The user database acquisition is the most consequential element: law enforcement now holds a map of who used the service and when, enabling targeted follow-on arrests across jurisdictions. This mirrors the cascade prosecutions after EncroChat (2020) and Anom (2021), both of which generated waves of secondary cases from the data obtained. The operation also demonstrates continued EU-Ukraine cooperation in cybercrime enforcement, legally significant because it creates evidentiary chains that may support future extradition or prosecution of Russia-based criminal actors. For the ransomware ecosystem, losing a trusted high-reliability anonymizing layer creates short-term operational disruption and long-term exposure risk from the database now in law enforcement hands — particularly for operators who used the service with identifiable payment methods or consistent patterns correlatable with known attacks.

### Context
The May 2026 Beijing summit produced formal technology commitments with major security implications. On navigation, Russia and China agreed to coordinate GLONASS and BeiDou satellite systems, improve interoperability, and collaborate on radio frequency allocation, satellite orbits, and IoT integration — directly upgrading Russia's precision targeting capabilities with higher-accuracy Chinese systems independent of US GPS. On AI, China proposed a global AI cooperation organization; both nations jointly opposed using AI "as a geopolitical tool" for technological dominance, framing their programs as defensive responses to Western pressure. ### Why it matters

GLONASS-BeiDou interoperability directly enhances Russia's military precision guidance in Ukraine and future conflicts, providing Chinese-calibrated accuracy that Western sanctions and GPS jamming were intended to deny. The internet sovereignty framework is equally consequential over the longer term: both nations are investing heavily in Global South digital infrastructure, and their coordinated promotion of sovereignty-based governance through shared technical standards and capacity-building has the potential to entrench government-controlled internet architectures in dozens of developing countries before democratic alternatives can gain traction. For US and European export control strategists, the joint open-source commitment signals the coalition is explicitly working to reduce the leverage that technology restrictions provide — accelerating the trajectory toward a bifurcated global technology ecosystem from theoretical to operational. Western intelligence agencies have consistently identified this China-Russia technology alignment as among their highest-priority long-term threat concerns.

## [tech law] UK cybercrime law reform would protect only 300 of Britain's 70,000 security researchers, experts warn

**Source:** [therecord.media](https://therecord.media/uk-plans-for-cybercrime-law-reform-limited-protections)

**Lead:** The UK government's long-awaited amendment to the Computer Misuse Act 1990 would restrict its statutory defense to roughly 300 accredited professionals — 0.4 percent of the country's 70,000-strong cybersecurity workforce — while barring them from verifying whether a discovered vulnerability is genuine.

### Context
Security Minister Dan Jarvis announced plans to amend the Computer Misuse Act 1990 following years of industry pressure. The proposed statutory defense is narrow in ways experts say make it functionally useless. Protection covers only scanning internet-facing systems; researchers must stop immediately upon discovering a vulnerability — before confirming whether it is real or assessing its severity — the precise step most system owners require before taking remediation action. The defense is restricted to British nationals holding UK Cyber Security Council accreditation; accredited individuals must conduct tests personally without delegating to junior staff or automated tools. Approximately 300 people hold the required certification against a cybersecurity workforce of 70,000 — 0.4 percent of the sector. Cyber policy consultant Jen Ellis described "a misalignment between expectations and reality" and called the proposal "much narrower" than industry sought. Agentic AI scanning tools, now widely deployed across the sector, remain in a legal grey area, and the accreditation model excludes bug bounty hunters, academic researchers, hobbyists, and professionals at smaller firms.

### Why it matters
The UK's reform places Britain below Germany, France, the Netherlands, Belgium, and the United States — all of which have broader frameworks that do not require government certification and do not bar researchers from confirming vulnerabilities are real. The practical effect may be to worsen the UK's national security posture: if legitimate researchers cannot legally test and disclose vulnerabilities, those flaws remain unpatched and exploitable by foreign state actors and criminal groups. For UK-based technology companies, the discrepancy between UK and European legal protections means security teams performing industry-standard practices in UK offices may be committing an offence that would be lawful in Paris or Amsterdam. The failure to address agentic AI tools creates a chilling effect on automated vulnerability scanning precisely when such tools are becoming indispensable — an ironic outcome from a reform ostensibly designed to strengthen the UK's cybersecurity ecosystem. Prior UK Computer Misuse Act reform attempts have stalled for years under successive governments, and the industry's disappointment with this proposal makes another multi-year delay likely.
