## [law] Supreme Court unanimously holds federal courts retain jurisdiction to confirm arbitration awards in cases already pending before them

**Source:** https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/05/justices-validate-authority-of-federal-courts-to-confirm-arbitration-awards-at-least-in-cases-al/

**Lead:** The Supreme Court unanimously held in Jules v. Andre Balazs Properties that federal courts have jurisdiction to confirm arbitration awards under the FAA when the underlying case was originally filed in federal court, clarifying the post-Badgerow landscape for employment and commercial arbitration enforcement.

**Context:** Jules, an employee, brought a federal lawsuit against Andre Balazs Properties — a luxury hotel group — and after the parties arbitrated their dispute, Jules moved in the same federal case to confirm the arbitration award under the Federal Arbitration Act. Balazs Properties argued that the 2022 Supreme Court decision in *Badgerow v. Walters* stripped federal courts of jurisdiction over such motions unless independent federal jurisdiction existed for the FAA motion itself, not merely for the underlying lawsuit. Justice Sonia Sotomayor, writing for a unanimous Court, rejected that reading: when a case is already pending in federal court with proper jurisdictional footing, that jurisdiction "continues while the parties arbitrated" and covers a subsequent motion to confirm the award filed within the same docket. The Court drew a precise line — the *Badgerow* rule applies only to freestanding FAA motions filed as standalone cases, not to confirmation motions appended to live federal proceedings. The ruling resolves a circuit-level disagreement that had created unpredictable outcomes for parties seeking to enforce awards from arbitrations that originated in federal employment or commercial litigation.

**Why it matters:** Arbitration is the primary dispute-resolution mechanism for tens of millions of employment contracts in the United States, and confirmation of awards in federal court is the essential enforcement step when a losing party refuses to comply voluntarily. If *Badgerow* had been read broadly, parties who arbitrated disputes arising from federal claims — discrimination suits, wage-and-hour cases, securities disputes — would have needed to file separate confirmation actions in state court or establish independent federal jurisdiction from scratch, adding cost, delay, and jurisdictional risk to a process that arbitration clauses are specifically designed to streamline. The unanimous ruling preserves the practical enforceability of arbitration for parties who chose federal court as their original forum. It also implicitly validates the common practice of federal courts retaining jurisdiction over cases sent to arbitration pending the award, ensuring that return to the federal docket for confirmation is a smooth procedural step rather than a new jurisdictional obstacle. Future litigation will focus on clarifying the precise boundary between "pending" cases that benefit from the Jules rule and truly freestanding motions governed by Badgerow — a distinction courts will need to develop in cases where the original federal case was closed before confirmation was sought.

## [law] Supreme Court denies Virginia Democrats' request to reinstate congressional map struck down by state court on procedural grounds

**Source:** https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/05/court-denies-virginias-request-to-reinstate-congressional-map-that-would-benefit-democrats/

**Lead:** The Supreme Court denied without comment Virginia Democrats' emergency application to block a state court ruling that invalidated a voter-approved constitutional amendment enabling a new congressional map, effectively allowing the existing map to remain in place for the 2026 elections.

**Context:** In the wake of the Supreme Court's April 2026 Voting Rights Act ruling in Louisiana v. Callais — which made it dramatically harder for plaintiffs to challenge congressional maps as racially dilutive — Virginia Democrats moved quickly to create an alternative path. The state legislature referred a constitutional amendment to a special election ballot that, if passed, would authorize drawing a new congressional map outside the normal redistricting cycle. Voters narrowly approved the amendment in April. However, the Virginia Supreme Court struck the amendment down on state procedural grounds, finding that the legislature had referred the amendment to the ballot after early voting had already begun in the 2025 general election, violating the state constitution's requirement that amendments be approved in two separate legislative sessions separated by an election. Virginia Democrats and Attorney General Jay Jones asked the U.S. Supreme Court to stay that ruling, arguing it overthrew a democratic outcome and raised federal constitutional questions. Republicans countered that this was a purely state-law procedural matter. The U.S. Supreme Court issued a brief unsigned order denying the application, with no public dissents. Governor Abigail Spanberger had already indicated the state would not use the map for 2026.

**Why it matters:** The denial cements the Virginia Supreme Court's procedural ruling and removes one potential workaround Democrats had been exploring to contest the post-Callais redistricting landscape. The broader context is consequential: the Supreme Court's VRA ruling last month effectively allows Republican-controlled state legislatures in Arkansas, Louisiana, Mississippi, Missouri, and South Carolina to redraw maps with reduced exposure to Section 2 challenges — and Virginia Democrats' attempt to legislatively counteract that dynamic via constitutional amendment has now been shut down by both state and federal courts. The denial without noted dissent suggests the full Court viewed this as a state-law procedural question unworthy of federal intervention, not a statement on the merits of the underlying redistricting dispute. What to watch: whether Virginia Democrats attempt another constitutional amendment route before the 2028 cycle, and whether parallel litigation in other states produces different procedural outcomes.

## [cybersecurity] CISA orders federal agencies to patch CVSS 10.0 Cisco SD-WAN authentication bypass by Sunday after Five Eyes warned of active exploitation

**Source:** https://therecord.media/cisa-orders-all-federal-agencies-to-patch-cisco-sd-wan-bug

**Lead:** CISA issued a binding operational directive requiring all federal civilian agencies to patch CVE-2026-20182, a maximum-severity authentication bypass in Cisco Catalyst SD-WAN controllers that grants unauthenticated attackers full administrative access, by Sunday May 18 — one of the tightest federal patch deadlines in recent memory.

**Context:** CVE-2026-20182 is an authentication bypass vulnerability in the Cisco Catalyst SD-WAN Controller with a maximum CVSS score of 10.0 — the highest possible rating. The flaw allows an unauthenticated remote attacker to bypass authentication entirely and obtain full administrative privileges on the device, effectively letting an adversary present themselves as a trusted network component and take control of the entire SD-WAN fabric managed by that controller. The vulnerability was discovered by Rapid7 incident responders in March 2026 while investigating a related earlier bug. Exploitation in the wild was confirmed, and the Five Eyes intelligence alliance — the US, UK, Canada, Australia, and New Zealand — had already issued coordinated warnings in February about active exploitation of related Cisco network device vulnerabilities as part of a campaign linked to nation-state actors. CISA's binding operational directive gives federal civilian executive branch agencies until May 18, 2026 — roughly 72 hours from publication — to apply Cisco's patch, reflecting the severity of ongoing exploitation and the strategic value of SD-WAN infrastructure to government networks.

**Why it matters:** SD-WAN controllers are the nerve centers of software-defined wide-area networks, which the federal government and large enterprises use to manage connectivity across dozens or hundreds of branch sites. A compromise of the controller is a compromise of the entire managed network: an attacker with administrative access can redirect traffic, insert malicious routes, monitor all communications, and maintain persistent access that survives device reboots and configuration changes. The 72-hour patch window is among the most aggressive CISA has ever imposed, signaling that active exploitation is occurring or imminent at a scale that makes the normal 21-day or 15-day BOD windows dangerously long. The Five Eyes attribution to nation-state actors in February — intelligence pointing to China-linked threat groups as the primary exploiters of related Cisco flaws — means this is not opportunistic ransomware but targeted espionage infrastructure. For private-sector organizations that also rely on Cisco Catalyst SD-WAN, the federal BOD creates de facto pressure to patch on the same timeline.

**Context:** The European Commission's enforcement posture for 2026 focuses on two newly opened major investigations under the DMA and DSA. The first targets Meta's alleged prevention of competing AI providers from accessing WhatsApp — implicating the DMA's interoperability obligations, which require gatekeepers to open messaging platforms to third-party providers. **Why it matters:** The two new AI-focused investigations represent the Commission's first direct application of DMA and DSA provisions to AI model training and AI-adjacent gatekeeping behavior, setting precedents that will shape how EU digital law applies to the AI infrastructure layer rather than just traditional platform conduct. The Meta WhatsApp investigation is particularly significant because the DMA's interoperability mandate has never been tested against a platform's AI ecosystem: if Meta is found to be excluding AI competitors from WhatsApp's APIs to favor its own Meta AI, the fine exposure is up to 10% of global annual revenue — roughly $13 billion at 2025 revenue levels. The Google AI training investigation could compel the company to disclose what content it uses for model training under EU law, creating a transparency obligation that does not currently exist in the US or UK. **Context:** On February 24, 1996, two Cessna aircraft operated by Brothers to the Rescue — a Cuban-American exile group that flew over the Florida Straits searching for Cuban rafters — were shot down by Cuban MiG-29 fighter jets, killing four people including US citizens Carlos Costa, Armando Alejandre Jr., Mario de la Peña, and Pablo Morales. **Why it matters:** Indicting a living former head of state on federal criminal charges for an act of state-sponsored violence nearly 30 years ago would be virtually unprecedented in US legal history. ## [tech] California Protect Our Games Act clears Appropriations Committee 11-2, advancing bill that would require publishers to preserve playable online games at shutdown

**Source:** https://www.gamingonlinux.com/2026/05/california-protect-our-games-act-backed-by-stop-killing-games-passes-key-hurdle/

**Lead:** California's AB 1921, the Protect Our Games Act, passed the Assembly Appropriations Committee 11-2 on May 14 and heads to the full Assembly floor, marking the first time US legislation specifically targeting the practice of publishers remotely shutting down purchased games has cleared a legislative committee.

**Context:** Assembly Bill 1921, introduced by Democratic Assemblymember Chris Ward and backed by the international Stop Killing Games consumer advocacy campaign, would require any game publisher to give purchasers 60 days' written notice before shutting down online servers that render a previously sold game unplayable. Upon reaching the shutdown date, the publisher must provide one of three remedies: a playable offline or standalone version of the game, a patch or update that preserves core functionality without central server dependency, or a full refund. The bill applies to digital games sold for purchase in California on or after January 1, 2027, exempting free-to-play titles. The Assembly Appropriations Committee voted 11-2 in favor on May 14, 2026, after the bill survived the Privacy and Consumer Protection Committee and the Judiciary Committee. The Entertainment Software Association — representing Microsoft, Sony, EA, Ubisoft, Capcom, and Epic Games — opposed the bill in committee and is expected to intensify lobbying as the floor vote approaches.

**Why it matters:** The "games as a service" model has made server-dependent online games the dominant format in the industry, and publishers routinely shut down servers for titles that are no longer commercially viable, rendering games that consumers paid $40-$70 for entirely unplayable. The Protect Our Games Act would impose a new consumer protection standard on this practice that does not currently exist under federal or state law. Because California is the largest US consumer market, a law passed here would functionally operate as a national standard: publishers would face the choice of either building preservation-compatible architectures for all products or maintaining separate California-compliant SKUs. The ESA's opposition reflects an industry-wide concern that the bill sets a precedent for mandating ongoing service obligations after commercial decisions to discontinue products — a principle that could extend beyond gaming to other digital services. The bill still requires a full Assembly floor vote, Senate passage, and Governor Gavin Newsom's signature before becoming law.
