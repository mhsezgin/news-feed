## [law] SCOTUS rules unanimously that pension actuaries may use current interest rates when calculating employer withdrawal liability

**Source:** [SCOTUSblog](https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/05/justices-agree-that-actuaries-can-use-up-to-date-assumptions-in-assessing-costs-of-leaving-a-mul/)

**Lead:** The Supreme Court unanimously held in M&K Employee Solutions v. IAM National Pension Fund that ERISA actuaries calculating an employer's multiemployer pension withdrawal liability may use current interest-rate assumptions rather than rates locked to the prior plan-year measurement date.

**Context:** The case arose when M&K Employee Solutions withdrew from the IAM National Pension Fund in 2018, triggering a withdrawal liability calculation under the Multiemployer Pension Plan Amendments Act. The fund's actuary applied a 6.5% interest rate adopted in January 2018 — after the December 31, 2017 measurement date — rather than the 7.5% rate in effect at year-end. That single percentage-point difference increased M&K's withdrawal liability by $4.36 million, roughly tripling its expected exit bill. M&K sued, arguing ERISA's statutory scheme required actuaries to freeze all assumptions as of the measurement date. Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson, writing for a unanimous Court, drew a critical distinction: factual plan data must be fixed at the measurement date, but actuarial assumptions are "predictive judgments" and calculation tools — not historical facts about the plan — and therefore can legitimately reflect current economic conditions selected after the measurement date. The ruling resolves a circuit split that had produced inconsistent withdrawal liability calculations across jurisdictions, particularly in New York federal courts.

**Why it matters:** Employers considering exit from troubled multiemployer pension plans must now account for the reality that actuaries have full discretion to apply whichever interest-rate assumptions are in effect when withdrawal liability is actually calculated — not when the withdrawal occurred. This gives pension funds a structural advantage: by delaying calculations during periods when interest rates are falling, actuaries can significantly inflate withdrawal liability bills, because lower discount rates increase the present value of projected future pension obligations. The ruling is likely to accelerate employer departures from multiemployer plans in sectors with struggling funds — construction, trucking, entertainment — since exit costs become harder to predict and potentially far higher than anticipated at the time of withdrawal. The Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation, which insures multiemployer plans covering approximately 10 million American workers, will need to assess whether the flexibility this ruling permits creates perverse incentives for fund administrators to time calculations strategically. Congress may revisit the Multiemployer Pension Reform Act's withdrawal liability framework in response, particularly as several large funds remain on track for insolvency within the decade.

## [law] Legislation introduced to require Supreme Court to explain shadow-docket emergency orders within 7 days, as Justice Jackson warns of eroding legitimacy

**Source:** [SCOTUSblog](https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/05/shadow-docket-reform/)

**Lead:** Representative Jamie Raskin introduced legislation requiring the Supreme Court to publicly justify all emergency shadow-docket orders within seven days, as Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson warned that unexplained judicial interventions are eroding public confidence in the judiciary at a politically sensitive moment.

**Context:** The shadow docket refers to the Supreme Court's practice of deciding high-stakes emergency applications — on abortion access, immigration enforcement, voting rules, and environmental regulation — through unsigned, often one-sentence orders issued without full briefing or oral argument. Raskin's bill would mandate that the Court publish written legal justification within seven days whenever it acts on emergency applications, creating a transparency window that currently does not exist. Justice Jackson became the Court's most vocal internal critic of the practice after the Court shortened the standard 32-day waiting period before finalizing its Voting Rights Act ruling this term — a deviation from established practice that Jackson said occurs "only two or three times in roughly 25 years." Jackson has proposed two structural remedies: establishing a genuine-urgency threshold before the Court intervenes in lower-court cases, and reordering the factors the Court applies when weighing emergency relief. She argued publicly that "courts don't lose authority when they explain themselves — that's where their legitimacy comes from."

**Why it matters:** The shadow docket has become the primary mechanism through which the current Court's emergency interventions effectively set national policy — often in ways that are difficult for lower courts to apply as precedent and difficult for litigants to challenge. Raskin's bill, if enacted, would be the most significant structural constraint on the Court's emergency powers since Congress reformed the certiorari process in 1925; even without passing, it raises the political cost of unexplained orders. For litigants in high-stakes federal cases — reproductive rights plaintiffs, immigration detainees, environmental regulators — the shadow docket is frequently the decisive forum, making transparency reform more consequential than any single merits ruling. Jackson's public criticism is unusually direct for a sitting justice and signals a level of institutional concern that is rare in the Court's modern history. The reform push has bipartisan resonance: conservative litigants challenging Biden-era regulations were themselves disadvantaged by shadow-docket orders that paused rules without explanation, suggesting the critique transcends partisan interest. Future justices who face a transparency obligation will be incentivized to be more selective about granting emergency relief, potentially shifting the Court's role in ongoing political controversies.

## [cybersecurity] CISA contractor exposed agency AWS keys and CI/CD credentials on public GitHub for months; senators demand classified briefing

**Source:** [Krebs on Security](https://krebsonsecurity.com/2026/05/lawmakers-demand-answers-as-cisa-tries-to-contain-data-leak/)

**Lead:** A CISA contractor called Nightwing deliberately disabled GitHub's built-in credential-protection systems and published AWS GovCloud keys, CI/CD pipeline tokens, and RSA private keys for CISA systems to a public repository that remained accessible from at least November 2025 until mid-May 2026, with the exposed AWS keys remaining valid for two more days after CISA was notified.

**Context:** GitGuardian researcher Guillaume Valadon discovered a public GitHub repository named "Private-CISA" containing roughly 844 MB of data — 498 MB in the active working tree and the remainder in Git history — including AWS workspace credentials, Firefox password stores, RSA private keys granting access to CISA-IT code repositories, Kubernetes configuration files, and plaintext passwords for at least a dozen internal systems. Brian Krebs reported the exposure on May 18, 2026, after which the account was taken offline; however, the AWS GovCloud keys were not revoked until two days after CISA received the notification. The repository was created on November 13, 2025, meaning credentials may have been discoverable via basic GitHub search for over six months. CISA stated "there is no indication that any sensitive data was compromised," but the agency has not disclosed which credentials were active during the exposure window, who may have accessed the repository, or why continuous monitoring systems failed to detect the publication. Government contractor Nightwing, which had administrative access to CISA's code development platform, is identified as the account operator.

**Why it matters:** This is a profound operational security failure at the federal agency whose central mission is preventing credential exposures across the US government — and it occurred during a period of one-third workforce reductions, budget cuts of hundreds of millions of dollars, and cancellation of key programs including election security initiatives under the current administration. Senator Maggie Hassan submitted 12 specific questions and demanded a classified briefing from Acting Director Nick Andersen by June 5, warning that "adversaries — like China, Russia, and Iran — seek to gain access to and persistence on federal networks." AWS GovCloud keys provide access to classified federal infrastructure; six months of potential exposure gives sophisticated threat actors time to map CISA's internal architecture, identify targets, and establish persistence that would survive a simple credential rotation. The incident exposes a gap in contractor oversight: Nightwing apparently had sufficient privileges to deliberately bypass security controls, raising systemic questions about how agencies vet and monitor contractor actions in sensitive code environments. Congress is likely to respond with legislation mandating minimum security-certification requirements for contractors with administrative access to agency development platforms, potentially as part of the next DHS appropriations cycle.

## [cybersecurity] GitHub's 3,800 internal repos stolen in Nx Console supply chain attack tracing back to TanStack breach

**Source:** [BleepingComputer](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/github-links-repo-breach-to-tanstack-npm-supply-chain-attack/)

**Lead:** GitHub confirmed that approximately 3,800 internal repositories were exfiltrated after a poisoned version of the Nx Console VS Code extension — active on the Visual Studio Marketplace for only 18 minutes on May 18, 2026 — harvested developer credentials as part of a cascading supply chain attack by threat group TeamPCP that also compromised OpenAI, Mistral AI, and Grafana Labs.

**Context:** The attack originated when TeamPCP compromised a Narwhal Technologies developer's machine via the TanStack npm supply chain attack that had circulated through the npm ecosystem in early May 2026. Exploiting that foothold, TeamPCP published Nx Console version 18.95.0 — a trojanized build of the popular developer tool with 2.2 million VS Code installs — between 12:30 and 12:48 PM UTC on May 18. The malicious extension "looked and behaved like normal Nx Console, but on startup it silently ran a single shell command that downloaded and executed a hidden package," according to OX Security researcher Nir Zadok. The payload harvested credentials from 1Password vaults, Anthropic Claude Code configurations, npm registry tokens, GitHub CLI tokens, and AWS accounts. GitHub CISO Alexis Wales subsequently confirmed exfiltration of approximately 3,800 internal repositories; Wales stated the attack showed no evidence of impact to customer-owned repositories, enterprises, or organizations. TeamPCP demanded at least $50,000 for the stolen source code.

**Why it matters:** An 18-minute window was sufficient to steal source code from one of the world's largest software development platforms, illustrating that the VS Code Marketplace is the highest-value supply chain attack surface in modern software development: extensions run in developer environments with access to secrets managers, cloud credential stores, and source control tokens — the full set of keys to any organization's infrastructure. The cascading nature of the attack — TanStack breach → Nx Console developer compromise → GitHub credential theft → 3,800-repo exfiltration, with parallel victims including OpenAI and Mistral — demonstrates that supply chain compromises now self-replicate without requiring the attacker to break into each target independently. GitHub's own internal codebase breach raises questions about the integrity of GitHub Actions workflows, security advisory pipelines, and internal tooling trusted by millions of developers globally. The EU's Cyber Resilience Act, which enters force in 2027, will require mandatory code-signing and vulnerability disclosure for software components — requirements that, if already in place, would have made the TanStack-to-Nx chain significantly harder to execute. The attack should accelerate enterprise adoption of just-in-time credential provisioning and developer machine isolation, practices that limit the blast radius of a single compromised endpoint.

## [tech law] EU's AI Act Digital Omnibus deal extends high-risk AI deadlines 16 months, bans AI-generated non-consensual intimate imagery from December 2026

**Source:** [InsidePrivacy](https://www.insideprivacy.com/artificial-intelligence/eu-ai-act-update-timeline-relief-targeted-simplification-and-new-prohibitions/)

**Lead:** EU negotiators reached a provisional Digital Omnibus agreement on May 7, 2026, pushing the main Annex III high-risk AI compliance deadline back 16 months to December 2027, adding new prohibitions on AI-generated non-consensual intimate imagery effective December 2026, and granting the EU AI Office exclusive enforcement jurisdiction over AI systems built on general-purpose AI models.

**Context:** The package grants significant timeline relief: Annex III high-risk AI obligations (covering AI in healthcare, employment, credit, education, and critical infrastructure) shift from August 2, 2026 to December 2, 2027, a 16-month extension, while Annex I obligations move from August 2027 to August 2028. The timeline extensions respond to industry evidence that mandatory third-party conformity assessments cannot be completed at scale under the original calendar. On prohibitions, the agreement adds a new Article 4a banning AI systems that generate non-consensual intimate imagery (NCII) or child sexual abuse material, effective December 2, 2026 — a rare substantive expansion of the law's prohibited-use categories. Enforcement jurisdiction over AI systems that integrate general-purpose AI models — a category covering most commercial deployments of OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, and Meta models in European products — is concentrated exclusively in the EU AI Office, eliminating the overlapping national-regulator authority that had paralyzed enforcement. Violations of the new Article 25 information-sharing obligations between GPAI providers and downstream deployers now carry fines of up to 3% of worldwide annual turnover or €15 million. Formal adoption is expected in June 2026.

**Why it matters:** The timeline extensions provide meaningful compliance runway for AI developers in regulated sectors, but they also reset the enforcement calendar for patient-safety advocates, employment-discrimination litigants, and credit-access researchers who were expecting legally enforceable protections by mid-2026. The December 2026 NCII prohibition is the AI Act's first expansion of its prohibited-use list since finalization and will be immediately tested: providers who build image-generation systems without adequate safeguards face liability even for unintentional NCII generation, while deployers face liability only for intentional misuse — a split that will drive litigation over where design negligence ends and user abuse begins. Centralizing GPAI enforcement in the AI Office resolves years of jurisdictional ambiguity but concentrates enormous market power at a single institution: the AI Office alone will now assess pre-market conformity for the world's most commercially significant AI systems deployed in Europe. The Cyber Resilience Act and AI Act together are creating a novel European software liability framework where both security and AI-safety failures carry quantified corporate consequences — a structural shift that US-headquartered developers must build into product roadmaps regardless of how US domestic AI regulation evolves. With formal adoption in June and Official Journal publication anticipated in July, the December 2026 NCII ban is weeks away from being legally binding.

## [tech law] Supreme Court's pending Chatrie ruling could set the broadest Fourth Amendment digital-privacy limits since Carpenter, covering geofence and reverse-keyword warrants

**Source:** [The Record](https://therecord.media/why-supreme-court-chatrie-case-could-reshape-privacy)

**Lead:** The Supreme Court's pending ruling in Chatrie v. United States — the first major digital Fourth Amendment case since Carpenter in 2018 — will determine whether law enforcement can compel Google to expose location data on every person within 150 meters of a crime scene without individualized suspicion, with potential implications for reverse-keyword searches and all cloud-stored personal data.

**Context:** Okello Chatrie was identified and convicted of robbing a Virginia credit union after police obtained a geofence warrant directing Google to disclose location records for all 19 accounts tied to devices within 150 meters of the bank during a 30-minute window — a search covering thousands of innocent users' data to identify one suspect. At oral argument in April 2026, liberal justices expressed alarm about searches lacking individualized suspicion across millions of user accounts, while conservative justices invoked the third-party doctrine of Smith v. Maryland (1979), arguing users voluntarily expose location data to Google when they accept its privacy policies. The Court last addressed digital privacy in Carpenter v. United States (2018), holding that seven days of historical cell-site location data required a warrant — but the justices have not yet defined whether Carpenter's reasoning extends to shorter timeframes, different data types, or location data stored by commercial cloud services rather than cell carriers. Petitioner's counsel argued that "anonymity is quite illusory" because location patterns reveal identity without ever needing a name, and that Google updates location records every two minutes within three meters of accuracy.

**Why it matters:** If the Court upholds geofence warrants, law enforcement in all 50 states will have clear authority to query Google, Apple, Microsoft, and Meta's location databases for everyone present near any crime scene, without naming a suspect in advance — a practice Google received roughly 60,000 requests per year to support as of 2024. Privacy advocates warn the ruling could also authorize "reverse-keyword searches" directing tech companies to identify every user who searched a particular term, and broad cloud sweeps of photos, emails, and documents stored across consumer services. A ruling against geofence warrants would force law enforcement to develop alternative investigative techniques, likely triggering legislation codifying the ruling's limits and defining what warrant particularity requirements apply to bulk cloud data requests. The case has attracted nearly 30 amicus briefs from tech companies, civil liberties organizations, and law enforcement groups — an unusually wide field that signals the ruling will reshape digital investigative practice for a generation. The decision is expected before the Court's term ends in late June 2026, making it one of the most consequential pending privacy rulings of the decade.

**Context:** The Republican People's Party (CHP) is Turkey's oldest secular party and currently polls roughly neck-and-neck with President Erdoğan's AKP — the first credible opposition parity in over two decades. The court's ruling centered on allegations that Özel secured delegates' loyalty at the 2023 congress through job promises and other kickbacks when he defeated Kılıçdaroğlu; the CHP denies all charges and characterizes the case as politically motivated. **Why it matters:** A court voiding the internal election of a legally registered opposition party and installing leadership of its choice represents a direct assault on the organizational autonomy that multiparty democracy requires; if sustained, it eliminates the CHP's ability to choose its own leadership free of judicial interference aligned with the ruling government. The 6% Borsa Istanbul drop and government bond decline reflect investor concern that political instability will deepen under a manufactured leadership crisis, especially at a time when Turkey's central bank is relying on institutional credibility to manage persistent inflation. The CHP's vow to "resist" the ruling raises the prospect of street protests that could further destabilize the country ahead of the 2028 presidential election, which Erdoğan is constitutionally barred from contesting unless he engineers a constitutional referendum or calls early elections. For the European Union — which periodically conditions Turkey's EU candidate status on democratic governance standards — the ruling provides fresh grounds for those arguing that accession talks should be formally suspended. The imprisonment of İmamoğlu already produced a market shock earlier in 2025; a second leadership destabilization suggests the government is systematically neutralizing opposition capacity ahead of the next electoral cycle regardless of international reaction.

## [global politics] WHO raises Ebola risk to 'very high' in DR Congo — 177 suspected deaths, no approved vaccine for Bundibugyo strain

**Source:** [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/5/22/who-raises-ebola-public-health-risk-to-very-high-in-dr-congo)

**Lead:** The WHO upgraded the Ebola risk in eastern DR Congo to "very high" at the national level on May 22, 2026, five days after declaring a Public Health Emergency of International Concern, as nearly 750 suspected cases and 177 suspected deaths dwarf the 82 confirmed cases — a gap that officials warn indicates an outbreak far larger than detection systems are capturing, with no licensed vaccine or treatment for the Bundibugyo strain.

**Context:** The current outbreak involves the Bundibugyo strain of Ebola, a less common and less studied variant compared to the Zaire strain that drove the 2014–2016 West Africa epidemic (11,000 deaths) and the 2018–2020 DRC outbreak that was controlled using ring vaccination. Unlike for Zaire Ebola, no licensed vaccine or specific treatment exists for Bundibugyo virus disease, which carries an estimated case fatality rate of 25–30%, eliminating the primary containment strategy used in recent outbreaks. The outbreak is centered in Ituri province in eastern DRC — an area experiencing active armed conflict, mass displacement, and deep community mistrust — conditions that severely impede contact tracing, safe burial protocols, and treatment-center operations. WHO's May 17 PHEIC declaration followed confirmed cases in Kampala, Uganda, among travelers from DRC, and sporadic cases in Germany, the Czech Republic, and the Netherlands. Provincial authorities in Ituri imposed funeral restrictions and gathering limits of 50 people, but residents in Rwampara burned a treatment center amid misinformation-fueled unrest, and a high positivity rate among initial samples — 8 positive among 13 collected — suggests widespread undetected transmission.

**Why it matters:** The Bundibugyo variant's lack of approved medical countermeasures transforms what would otherwise be a containable outbreak into a race between traditional epidemiological tools — isolation, contact tracing, safe burials — and a highly lethal virus spreading through one of the world's most operationally difficult environments. The WHO Regional Director's unusual public warning that "it would be a big mistake to underestimate" the Bundibugyo strain signals that health authorities believe the confirmed case count is a severe underestimate, and that the outbreak's true scale may require a response on the order of the 2018–2020 effort that ultimately involved over $1 billion in international funding. Spread to Uganda and three European countries via air travel demonstrates that Ituri's porous borders and ongoing displacement make geographic containment unreliable; the international community cannot wait for DRC to control the outbreak domestically before preparing port-of-entry screening and healthcare surge capacity. Emergency Bundibugyo-specific vaccine candidates exist in research pipelines, but regulatory timelines make approval within the current outbreak window unlikely without coordinated emergency use authorization from multiple national agencies — a process that required months even during COVID. For international health governance, the Bundibugyo outbreak is the first major test of whether accelerated countermeasure development pipelines and early-warning systems have been meaningfully improved since post-COVID reforms.
