## [global politics] US strikes Iran again as ceasefire collapses over uranium verification dispute and IRGC Hormuz repositioning

**Source:** [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/5/26/us-strikes-iran-again-what-we-know-and-is-the-ceasefire-over)

**US Central Command conducted fresh strikes on Iranian military positions on May 26, 2026, after Iran resumed uranium enrichment activities and IRGC naval forces repositioned near Strait of Hormuz chokepoints, signaling a collapse of the ceasefire framework under negotiation since late April.**

CENTCOM announced the strikes in an early morning statement, describing targeted strikes on Iranian air defense radar installations and IRGC naval command infrastructure at Bandar Abbas and Qeshm Island. The Pentagon characterized the strikes as a response to "material violations of ceasefire terms" by Iran, specifically identifying Iran's resumption of uranium enrichment to 60% purity at the Fordow facility—detected by IAEA monitoring equipment that Iran had allowed to remain operational during the ceasefire—and the repositioning of IRGC fast boats and mine-laying vessels to positions within six nautical miles of major Hormuz shipping lanes. Iranian state media reported "light to moderate damage" at targeted facilities and announced the IRGC was at maximum readiness. The underlying negotiating failure, according to Al Jazeera's reconstruction from officials on both sides, was Iran's refusal to accept an international verification mechanism for disposing of its highly enriched uranium stockpile before receiving sanctions relief—a condition the US viewed as essential and Iran viewed as an unacceptable sovereignty intrusion. Qatar and Pakistan, who had served as mediators, both called for an immediate return to negotiations.

**Why it matters:** The resumption of hostilities after a period during which a deal appeared imminent is significantly more dangerous than the original outbreak of conflict, because both sides had made public commitments about the contours of a settlement that now constitute failed expectations. Iran's domestic political dynamics—in which hardliners and the IRGC had already framed any deal as capitulation—mean that a second ceasefire will be substantially harder to negotiate: each escalation hardens the domestic political cost of compromise. The strikes on Bandar Abbas and Qeshm Island are particularly consequential because those facilities are operationally linked to the IRGC's mining and fast-attack capability in the Strait, and any attrition of that capability creates pressure on the IRGC to use remaining assets before further strikes, increasing the near-term closure risk. For global energy markets, the collapse of ceasefire negotiations removes the forward-looking discount on oil prices that had developed over the past week, and the combination of IRGC repositioning and US strikes creates a realistic short-term scenario for full Strait closure—the most severe energy shock in modern history given the 20% of global oil trade at risk.

## [tech law] Texas AG sues Meta and WhatsApp over encrypted messaging and minor age-verification obligations under SCOPE Act

**Source:** [Texas Tribune](https://www.texastribune.org/2026/05/21/texas-whatsapp-meta-privacy-encryption-lawsuit/)

**Texas AG Ken Paxton sued Meta and WhatsApp on May 21, alleging that end-to-end encryption is not a defense to WhatsApp's failure to implement age verification for minors under Texas's SCOPE Act—a legal theory that, if successful, would require either a backdoor or a fundamental architectural change to WhatsApp.**

The complaint, filed in the Western District of Texas, names three causes of action: violation of the Texas Securing Children Online Through Parental Empowerment Act (SCOPE Act, effective January 2025) for failure to implement verifiable age gates and parental controls for minor users; violation of the Texas Data Privacy and Security Act for collecting and processing biometric identifiers through voice messages and profile photos without adequate consent and privacy notice; and violation of the Texas Harmful Application Law for designing WhatsApp's interface in ways alleged to maximize engagement among minor users without age-appropriate restrictions. The central legal theory targets WhatsApp's end-to-end encryption: Paxton argues that Meta designed WhatsApp to make parental monitoring technically impossible and that this architectural choice—rather than constituting a First Amendment or technical necessity defense—reflects a willful decision to prioritize feature adoption over legally required child-safety obligations. The complaint echoes the structure of Texas's 2024 biometric data suit against Meta, which settled for $1.4 billion. WhatsApp has approximately 2 billion users globally and an estimated 40 million users in the United States; Texas alleges a substantial fraction are minors under 18 for whom SCOPE Act requirements apply.

**Why it matters:** The legal theory targeting end-to-end encryption as a statutory violation is the most significant development in the US encryption policy debate since the FBI's 2016 San Bernardino litigation: if Texas courts accept that encryption architecture can itself violate state consumer protection laws, the precedent could cascade across the 15+ states that have enacted SCOPE Act-equivalent child safety legislation, creating potentially divergent compliance requirements across the largest messaging platforms. WhatsApp's constitutional defense will rely heavily on Section 230 immunity, First Amendment protection for encryption as a form of mathematical expression, and the Communications Decency Act—but Section 230 has increasingly been narrowed in child-safety litigation, and several circuit courts have held that design choices can lose Section 230 protection when the design itself facilitates illegal activity. For the broader encryption debate, a Texas win would validate the policy position that age verification mandates effectively require platform architectural changes incompatible with strong end-to-end encryption—a conclusion that privacy advocates and cryptographers have argued since KOSA and its state equivalents were first proposed, and that supporters of those laws have consistently disputed.

## [law] Germany amends Grundgesetz Article 87a to authorize Bundeswehr drone shootdowns, resolving constitutional constraint on domestic military force

**Source:** [Verfassungsblog](https://verfassungsblog.de/drohnenabwehr-grundgesetz-verteidigung/)

**Germany's Bundestag amended Grundgesetz Article 87a with a two-thirds supermajority on May 25, authorizing Bundeswehr drone shootdowns over German territory without prior cabinet approval—removing a constitutional constraint that had prevented Germany from operationalizing its air defense capabilities for domestic protection against the Russian drone threat.**

Article 87a of the Grundgesetz had since 1956 restricted the deployment of armed Bundeswehr forces in German territory to defense against external attack, with the Federal Constitutional Court having held in 2006 that the provision prohibits shootdowns of hijacked civilian aircraft—a ruling that cast doubt on whether armed defensive responses to drone incursions were constitutionally permissible. The amendment passed May 25 adds explicit language authorizing the Bundeswehr to engage and destroy unmanned aerial systems operated in a manner determined to pose an imminent threat to the security of German territory, infrastructure, or population, without requiring prior authorization beyond the standing operational rules of engagement issued by the Federal Defense Ministry under Bundeswehr command authority. The Bundesrat voted simultaneously to ratify the amendment, meeting the constitutional requirement for a concurrent two-thirds majority in both chambers. Verfassungsblog's analysis by constitutional law scholar Christoph Möllers argues that while the amendment resolves the immediate operational question, it creates three constitutional hard cases: the definition of "hostile" for dual-use civilian drones that can be commandeered remotely; the allocation of use-of-force authority between Bundeswehr and Länder police in ambiguous incidents below the armed-attack threshold; and whether the amendment's scope covers autonomous drone-counterdrone systems that engage without individual human authorization in each instance.

**Why it matters:** Germany's constitutional constraint on domestic military force use was a deliberate post-WWII design choice reflecting deep societal concern about militarism, and amending it—even for the narrow purpose of drone defense—represents a significant normative shift in Germany's constitutional culture, made possible only by Russia's 2022 invasion fundamentally reordering German security politics. The operational significance is immediate: German air defense units equipped with the SkyNex and Skyranger systems had been legally unable to engage hostile drones over German territory, including near critical infrastructure such as the Rhine-Ruhr energy grid and Baltic Sea gas pipeline infrastructure that Russia-linked drone reconnaissance has reportedly targeted. For NATO, Germany's constitutional amendment completes an institutional shift: Germany now has both the legal authority and, following its 100 billion euro defense spending package, increasingly the hardware to provide domestic territorial air defense without requiring US or allied forces to fill the legal gap. The constitutional debate about "hostile" drones—particularly the hybrid-warfare scenario where nominally civilian commercial drones are remotely reprogrammed for reconnaissance or attack—is the central unresolved question that will be litigated at the Federal Constitutional Court within years as the first Bundeswehr drone shootdowns occur and affected parties challenge the legal basis.
