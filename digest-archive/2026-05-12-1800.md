## [ai] Sam Altman takes the stand in Musk v. OpenAI trial

**Link:** https://www.theverge.com/ai-artificial-intelligence/916975/altman-takes-stand-elon-musk-openai-trial

**Lead:** OpenAI CEO Sam Altman took the stand in a California federal jury trial on May 12, testifying that Elon Musk demotivated key researchers by ranking their accomplishments and that morale at the company rose when Musk departed the board in 2018.

**Context:** The trial pits Musk against OpenAI, the AI nonprofit he co-founded and helped fund before departing its board in 2018. Musk filed suit in 2024, alleging OpenAI and its leaders — primarily Altman and president Greg Brockman — breached their charitable trust when the organization shifted from a pure nonprofit mission to its current structure, which includes a capped-profit arm now valued at $852 billion. Musk is seeking a court order requiring OpenAI to revert to full nonprofit status, the removal of Altman and Brockman from the board, and more than $130 billion returned to the nonprofit arm. Altman testified that Musk required him to rank researchers' accomplishments, which "demotivated" key team members. In what Altman called a "particularly hair-raising moment," other co-founders asked Musk what would happen to his proposed majority control of OpenAI if he died; Musk reportedly responded that control might pass to his children. Altman added that no donor besides Musk has ever complained that OpenAI was deviating from its mission, and that the morale boost after Musk's departure was significant. Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella testified the prior week that Musk never raised concerns with him about Microsoft's investment in OpenAI.

**Why it matters:** This is one of the most consequential legal proceedings in the AI industry's short history, touching foundational questions about what duties a nonprofit AI organization owes to its stated mission and whether a profit-oriented structure can constitute a breach of charitable trust. A ruling for Musk could threaten OpenAI's ability to raise private capital and its ongoing $40 billion SoftBank funding round, and could create precedent affecting other mission-driven AI labs that have adopted hybrid nonprofit/profit structures. The trial is also producing the public record of how OpenAI actually governed itself during the critical early years — including what role Musk played, how management decisions were made, and how the November 2023 Sam Altman ouster came about. Mira Murati's deposition, released earlier in the trial, described that board decision as a "Hail Mary." Musk is expected to take the stand in the coming days, and OpenAI's conversion to a public benefit corporation is proceeding in parallel — meaning the trial's outcome could reshape the legal framework under which the most valuable AI company in the world operates.

## [ai/law] Parents sue OpenAI after ChatGPT allegedly recommended a fatal drug combination

**Link:** https://www.theverge.com/ai-artificial-intelligence/928691/openai-chatgpt-wrongful-death-overdose

**Lead:** The parents of UC Merced student Sam Nelson, who died in May 2025, have sued OpenAI and CEO Sam Altman in California state court, alleging ChatGPT actively recommended a fatal kratom-Xanax-Benadryl drug combination on the day of their son's overdose.

**Context:** Leila Turner-Scott and Angus Scott filed the wrongful death lawsuit in California state court on May 12, 2026, naming OpenAI and CEO Sam Altman as defendants. Their son Sam Nelson, 19, was a psychology student at UC Merced who died from an accidental overdose in May 2025. According to the complaint, Nelson consulted ChatGPT on the day of his death about drug combinations; the chatbot allegedly told him it was safe to combine kratom (an herbal supplement sold in pills and drinks) with Xanax, a prescription benzodiazepine widely used for anxiety. The chatbot then allegedly suggested he could add Benadryl (diphenhydramine) to intensify the effect he sought. Kratom and benzodiazepines can both cause respiratory depression, and their combination is documented to be potentially fatal. OpenAI stated in response that "ChatGPT is not a substitute for medical or mental health care," that it has "continued to strengthen how it responds in sensitive and acute situations," and that the chatbot encouraged Sam to seek professional help on multiple occasions.

**Why it matters:** This lawsuit is among the most significant AI product liability cases filed to date, directly testing whether AI companies can be held liable in tort for the advice their models give to individual users. OpenAI's primary legal defenses will likely center on products liability preemption, whether Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act extends to AI-generated outputs, and whether the chatbot's recommendations constitute actionable negligence. If California courts allow the case to survive a motion to dismiss, it will pressure the entire AI industry to implement more aggressive refusal behaviors and disclaimers on drug-related queries. The case runs parallel to the Musk v. OpenAI trial and OpenAI's ongoing public benefit corporation conversion. Anthropic, Google, and Meta each face similar latent exposure given that their models handle millions of sensitive health queries daily. A successful claim in California — which has among the most plaintiff-friendly tort environments — could establish a template for litigation in other states and jurisdictions.

## [tech law] EU Commission preliminarily finds TikTok's addictive design breaches the DSA

**Link:** https://www.cnbc.com/2026/05/12/tiktok-instagram-social-media-addictive-eu-crack-down.html

**Lead:** The European Commission issued a preliminary finding that TikTok's algorithmic features — endless scrolling, autoplay, and push notifications — violate the Digital Services Act's child-protection provisions, with EU Commission President von der Leyen signaling parallel proceedings against Meta's Instagram and Facebook, and against X.

**Context:** Von der Leyen announced Tuesday that the Commission has issued a preliminary finding under DSA Article 28 that TikTok's design constitutes "addictive design" systematically harming minors, citing links to sleep deprivation, depression, anxiety, self-harm, cyberbullying, grooming, and exploitation. The preliminary finding is not a final decision: TikTok has the right to respond before the Commission reaches a conclusion. Under the DSA, fines for violations by VLOPs can reach 6% of global annual revenue — hundreds of millions of euros for ByteDance. Von der Leyen simultaneously indicated parallel proceedings against Meta for Instagram and Facebook (failing to enforce their own 13-year age minimum) and against X for similar design practices. The anticipated "Digital Fairness Act," specifically prohibiting addictive and harmful design in consumer apps, is expected to be proposed later in 2026.

**Why it matters:** This is the most aggressive use of DSA child-protection provisions since the law applied to VLOPs in 2023, and the first proceeding explicitly targeting algorithmic system design rather than content moderation failures — extending the law's reach from what is on a platform to how the platform is engineered. A final finding would establish the first binding EU legal definition of "addictive design," which regulators in the UK, Australia, and US states would likely use as a reference standard. For Meta, the exposure is arguably greater than TikTok's given its larger EU user base and the simpler legal theory around age minimum enforcement failures. The proceedings raise definitional questions the DSA does not fully resolve — where is the line between engagement optimization and addiction? — that will need to be answered for the first time in binding regulatory decisions.

## [cybersecurity] Instructure pays undisclosed ransom after second Canvas breach; Congress investigates

**Link:** https://therecord.media/instructure-pays-ransom-canvas-incident-congress-investigation

**Lead:** Instructure, maker of the Canvas LMS used by thousands of US universities and K-12 schools, confirmed it paid an undisclosed ransom to the ShinyHunters hacking group on May 12 after suffering a second intrusion just five days after the initial breach, as Congress demanded an accountability briefing before May 21.

**Context:** The Canvas attack began May 1, 2026, when ShinyHunters claimed to have exfiltrated data from approximately 9,000 customers, including names, email addresses, student IDs, and messages between students and professors. Instructure declared the incident contained on May 2. A second intrusion on May 7 — just five days later — defaced the platform with a ransom demand and blocked millions of students from accessing course materials, forcing universities nationwide to reschedule or cancel exams. On May 12, ShinyHunters threatened to release all stolen data publicly; Instructure confirmed payment under an agreement that included digital confirmation of data destruction. Rep. Andrew Garbarino of the House Homeland Security Committee demanded a briefing before May 21, citing "serious questions about incident response capabilities."

**Why it matters:** The Canvas incident illustrates the acute risk created by LMS market concentration — a single vendor compromise can simultaneously affect millions of students at thousands of institutions. Paying the ransom contradicts FBI and CISA guidance, and the "digital confirmation of data destruction" has no enforcement mechanism. Congressional interest could accelerate proposed cybersecurity requirements for ed-tech vendors, where existing federal law (FERPA) imposes no breach notification timeline. The two-intrusion-in-five-days pattern indicates a serious incident response failure. FERPA-protected student records cannot be "changed" if compromised, making harm potentially long-lasting.

## [law] James Comey indicted for Instagram seashell post under "true threats" doctrine

**Link:** https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/05/true-threats-james-comey-and-the-supreme-court-an-explainer-/

**Lead:** Former FBI Director James Comey was indicted in May 2026 for an Instagram photo showing seashells arranged as "86 47," which federal prosecutors allege is a veiled threat to kill President Trump — a case that directly tests the Supreme Court's 2023 Counterman v. Colorado standard for true threats under the First Amendment.

**Context:** The DOJ unsealed an indictment charging Comey with making a threatening communication against a federal official after he posted an Instagram photo of seashells arranged as "86 47" on a North Carolina beach. The government alleges "86" is mob slang for "kill," making the sequence a threat against President Trump (the 47th president). Comey deleted the post, saying it "never occurred" to him the arrangement carried violent meaning. In Counterman v. Colorado (2023), the Supreme Court held that prosecutors must show the defendant was at least reckless about whether the communication constituted a threat — meaning conscious disregard of a substantial risk that others would interpret it as threatening. The prosecution must prove Comey knew there was a substantial risk the post would be read as a threat and posted it anyway.

**Why it matters:** The Comey indictment is one of the highest-profile tests of the Counterman standard since 2023 and a novel use of federal threat statutes against symbolic social media imagery. If the prosecution succeeds, it will create precedent allowing symbolic posts — beach photos, memes, arrangements of objects — to be prosecuted as federal criminal threats, raising serious chilling-effect concerns. If it fails, it strengthens the argument that ambiguous symbolism cannot satisfy Counterman's recklessness standard without direct evidence of subjective intent. The case carries an unavoidable political dimension: Comey was the FBI director Trump fired in 2017, making the prosecution appear to many as political retaliation. The Supreme Court may ultimately need to clarify how Counterman applies to non-verbal symbolic communication.

## [law] Federal prosecutors charge Dali ship operator in Baltimore bridge collapse

**Link:** https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cnvpm67ner6o

**Lead:** Federal prosecutors unsealed a criminal indictment on May 12 charging Synergy Marine Group — the Singaporean operator of the container ship Dali — and its Indian technical superintendent with conspiracy, obstruction, and misconduct resulting in death for the March 2024 collapse of Baltimore's Francis Scott Key Bridge, which killed six construction workers.

**Context:** The indictment, filed April 8 and unsealed May 12, 2026, charges Synergy Marine Pte Ltd. (Singapore) and Synergy Maritime Pte Ltd. (Chennai, India), along with technical superintendent Radhakrishnan Karthik Nair, 47. Charges include conspiracy, willfully failing to report a known hazardous condition to the Coast Guard, obstruction of a federal agency proceeding, and false statements. Prosecutors allege the defendants forged safety certifications, falsely claimed the vessel was seaworthy, and lied to federal investigators. The DOJ contends the March 26, 2024 disaster was preventable had Synergy Marine properly maintained the Dali's generators: the ship suffered a catastrophic electrical failure causing loss of propulsion and steering before striking the bridge.

**Why it matters:** This is one of the rare cases where a foreign shipping company faces federal criminal prosecution in the US for a maritime disaster on American waters. The forgery and obstruction allegations add a separate evidentiary track beyond simple negligence; if safety certifications were deliberately falsified, the case could reshape accountability standards for maritime classification societies and flag states. Civil litigation by the families of the six workers killed proceeds separately and gains substantial evidentiary leverage from the criminal indictment. The case will likely accelerate proposed legislation on maritime infrastructure protection that Maryland congressional delegation members have been seeking since 2024.

## [tech law] EU member states exported surveillance tech to human rights violators despite 2021 reforms

**Link:** https://therecord.media/eu-countries-export-surveillance-tech-to-countries-with-human-rights-issues

**Lead:** A Human Rights Watch report released May 12 found that Bulgaria, Poland, Finland, Denmark, Estonia, and the Czech Republic have exported surveillance technology to over two dozen countries with documented human rights violations, exposing fundamental failures in the EU's 2021 export-control reforms that the European Commission has failed to enforce.

**Context:** The report, compiled from trade documents obtained via freedom of information requests, identified Bulgaria as the most prolific exporter — selling surveillance technology to over 20 countries including the UAE and Azerbaijan. Poland exported phone-monitoring systems to Rwanda, where the government has used similar technology against dissidents. France, Greece, Spain, Germany, and Italy also export but refused to provide trade records. The 2021 EU export-control expansion required exporters to assess human rights records of recipient nations, but the Commission has "failed to effectively police member states' surveillance tech sales." The Commission plans to evaluate the framework in September 2026. Nearly all commercial surveillance vendors identified in a 2024 Google government spyware report are EU-based.

**Why it matters:** The EU's positioning as the global technology-regulation standard-setter is directly undermined when member states simultaneously export the tools of authoritarian surveillance. The September 2026 evaluation is now under significant pressure, with civil society pushing for binding prohibitions on exports to Parliament-designated human rights violators. Several recipient countries are active EU trade partners, creating diplomatic conflicts. The framework's reliance on member-state self-reporting and voluntary compliance — rather than independent verification — makes systematic evasion practically easy and represents a structural enforcement gap that reform must address.

## [law] Fighting back after the gutting of the Voting Rights Act

**Link:** https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/05/fighting-back-after-the-gutting-of-the-voting-rights-act-/

**Lead:** Legal scholars are exploring the Constitution's largely dormant Guarantee Clause, state-level trigger laws, and economic sanctions as potential tools to fight back after Louisiana v. Callais dramatically narrowed Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act — a ruling that has already enabled Tennessee to eliminate its only Democratic congressional seat and split Memphis's Black-majority county.

**Context:** In Louisiana v. Callais, Justice Alito's majority required plaintiffs' redistricting maps to satisfy states' own "specified political goals" — a new standard making most Section 2 challenges in partisan gerrymandering contexts nearly impossible. Tennessee already used this to eliminate its only Democratic seat and split Memphis's majority-Black county. Chicago-Kent professor Carolyn Shapiro argues the Guarantee Clause — Article IV, Section 4's guarantee of a "Republican Form of Government" to every state — could provide a congressional basis to outlaw partisan gerrymandering for state and federal districts. State-level proposals include trigger laws conditioned on reciprocal adoption by other states, and economic sanctions restricting commerce with anti-democratic redistricting states. Shapiro acknowledges the current Court would likely reject such legislation.

**Why it matters:** The Guarantee Clause has been largely dormant since 1912, when the Court held such claims were non-justiciable political questions. Any legislative strategy faces substantial doctrinal headwinds even if it is constitutionally valid in theory. The analysis makes clear that conventional legal tools for protecting minority voting rights have been systematically narrowed — from Shelby County (2013) to Brnovich (2021) to Callais — and restoration requires either a changed Supreme Court or untested legal strategies. With 2026 midterms rapidly approaching, states are moving quickly to exploit the Callais window, making the political timeline as pressing as the legal one.

## [tech law] FCC reverses course on foreign router and drone update ban, extends deadline to 2029

**Link:** https://therecord.media/fcc-pushes-ban-on-updates-to-foreign-routers-drones-2029

**Lead:** The FCC extended its waiver allowing security and firmware updates for foreign-made consumer routers and drones — primarily TP-Link and DJI — until January 1, 2029, reversing earlier policy that would have cut off security patches to tens of millions of already-deployed devices by early 2027 and conceding the ban would have created worse cybersecurity risks than it solved.

**Context:** The FCC's Office of Engineering and Technology issued Public Notice DA 26-454 on May 8, 2026, extending the update waiver for Covered List devices. The original deadlines — January 1, 2027 for drones and March 1, 2027 for routers — are now extended to January 1, 2029. The waiver covers both minor security patches (Class I) and more substantial firmware work designed to mitigate consumer harm (Class II, newly added). Eligible devices are those authorized before the December 22, 2025 cutoff for drones and March 23, 2026 cutoff for routers. The FCC stated explicitly that blocking security patches on already-deployed devices would create a larger attack surface than the national security threat the Covered List was designed to address.

**Why it matters:** The reversal exposes the central tension in US policy toward Chinese technology: there are no domestic equivalents to DJI consumer drones, and TP-Link holds commanding US market share — meaning the ban was always more political posture than executable policy. Cutting off updates would have left millions of devices permanently unpatched, creating attack vectors that adversaries — including China — could exploit on the very devices the ban was meant to protect against. The 2029 deadline extends rather than resolves the dilemma; the political pressure to complete the ban will return. The FCC's explicit acknowledgment that the ban would have produced worse security outcomes than the problem it targeted is a rare instance of a federal agency publicly admitting a policy's unintended consequences before implementation.
