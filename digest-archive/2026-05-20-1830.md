## [tech law] FTC issues first Take It Down Act enforcement letters to 12 major platforms including Meta, Google, and TikTok

**Source:** <https://therecord.media/ftc-warns-12-firms-of-violating-take-it-down-act>

**Lead:** The FTC sent warning letters to 12 major tech companies on May 20 for failing to comply with the Take It Down Act, which became enforceable May 19, 2026, requiring platforms to remove nonconsensual intimate images within 48 hours.

**Context:** The Take It Down Act (TIDA), enacted in May 2025 and enforceable as of May 19, 2026, requires online platforms to provide victims with a process to request removal of nonconsensual intimate images (NCII) — including AI-generated deepfakes — and to delete the flagged content within 48 hours of receiving a request. FTC Chairman Andrew Ferguson dispatched warning letters to Alphabet (Google/YouTube), Amazon, Apple, Automattic (WordPress), Bumble, Discord, Match Group, Meta, Microsoft, Pinterest, Reddit, SmugMug, Snapchat, TikTok, and X for failing to meet the law's requirements. The FTC's letters urged each company to implement photo-hashing technology to detect and remove duplicate images simultaneously, share hashes with the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children for minor images and with StopNCII.org for adult images, display clear TIDA compliance notices on their homepages, allow victims to submit removal requests directly from images, and provide tracking numbers so victims can monitor the status of removal requests. Non-compliance carries civil penalties of up to $53,088 per violation. Ferguson stated the FTC stands "ready to monitor compliance, investigate violations, and enforce the Take It Down Act," signaling that these letters are a warning, not the final enforcement action.

**Why it matters:** This is the first major regulatory salvo under TIDA and marks a significant moment in platform governance for intimate-image abuse: after years of voluntary compliance programs producing inconsistent results, federal law now mandates enforceable timelines and specific technical mechanisms. The 48-hour removal window is the shortest mandatory response time yet imposed on US platforms for any category of content, and the $53,088 per-violation penalty structure creates a strong financial incentive to build robust automated detection rather than rely on manual review queues. The cross-platform hash-sharing requirement — channeled through NCMEC and StopNCII — creates a shared detection infrastructure that, if fully implemented, would significantly raise the floor for all covered platforms simultaneously. For major AI companies developing generative image tools, this enforcement posture means the FTC is unlikely to treat image-generation products as exempt from TIDA just because they don't host images long-term. EU-based observers note that TIDA's architecture mirrors elements of the DSA's mandatory content-detection provisions, suggesting converging regulatory standards on both sides of the Atlantic.

**Context:** ShinyHunters — an organized cybercriminal group that the FBI describes as "specializing in large-scale data breaches and extortion" across the technology, finance, and retail sectors — claimed responsibility for breaching 7-Eleven's internal systems. 7-Eleven discovered the unauthorized access on April 8, 2026, and launched an investigation that determined cybercriminals had entered "certain 7-Eleven systems used to store franchisee documents." The compromised data includes names, addresses, Social Security numbers, and unspecified additional records; 7-Eleven declined to disclose the total number of individuals affected. The company filed mandatory breach notifications with state data protection regulators in Maine, Vermont, and Massachusetts, and declined media requests for further comment. ShinyHunters has been notably active in recent months — the same group recently attacked Instructure, the company behind the Canvas learning management platform, disrupting course access at thousands of US schools and colleges. **Why it matters:** The 7-Eleven breach matters for several overlapping reasons. First, franchisee-document repositories are often overlooked in corporate cybersecurity programs — they sit outside the main customer-data pipelines but contain highly sensitive personally identifiable information including Social Security numbers, which cannot be changed like passwords and enable long-term identity fraud. Second, ShinyHunters' continued operational pace despite FBI designation and prior prosecutorial attention against affiliated members signals that the group has sufficient operational resilience to sustain large-scale attacks on major consumer brands. Third, 7-Eleven's filing with three state regulators but refusal to disclose victim counts will likely attract scrutiny under state AG offices increasingly requiring specific numerical disclosures; California's CPRA and New York's SHIELD Act both have provisions that can trigger follow-on investigations for opaque breach disclosures. Fourth, the incident adds to the cluster of retail and developer-tool supply-chain breaches in May 2026 — including the GitHub internal-repos theft and the 314-npm package compromise — suggesting that coordinated or copycat campaign activity is elevating baseline breach risk across sectors.

## [tech law] Discord enables end-to-end encryption for all voice and video calls by default, bucking a platform trend toward less privacy

**Source:** <https://therecord.media/discord-migrates-users-to-end-to-end-encryption>

**Lead:** Discord has migrated all users to end-to-end encryption for voice and video calls by default, contrasting sharply with Instagram and TikTok, which recently discontinued their own E2E encryption offerings.

**Context:** Discord's rollout of mandatory end-to-end encryption (E2E) for all voice and video messages covers all devices and platforms — laptops, iOS, Android, PlayStation, Xbox, and web browsers — with the sole exception of Stage Channels, which are used for live broadcast-style events. The company began experimenting with the underlying encryption protocol in August 2023, introduced an independently audited version in September 2024, and completed the full mandatory migration this week without requiring any user opt-in. Discord executives cited the technical complexity of the rollout as a distinguishing challenge: "A single Discord call can have someone on a laptop, someone on their phone, someone on a PlayStation, someone on an Xbox, and someone in a web browser, all in the same conversation," making it substantially harder to enforce consistent encryption than on mobile-only or single-platform services. The rollout comes as the broader platform landscape moves in the opposite direction: Instagram and TikTok both announced in recent months that they are removing their end-to-end encryption options for direct messages, citing safety and moderation concerns. Apple and Google, by contrast, expanded their cross-platform E2E encryption to Android-iPhone messaging interoperability last week.

**Why it matters:** Discord's move matters both as a privacy milestone and as a regulatory signal. The platform has over 600 million registered accounts and is used heavily by gaming communities, academic groups, and increasingly by corporate teams — making mandatory E2E encryption a meaningful expansion of the population whose voice and video communications are protected from server-side access, legal intercept without cryptographic workarounds, and bulk data compromise in breach scenarios. The contrast with Instagram and TikTok is pointed: two of the world's largest social platforms are retreating from E2E on messaging, citing law enforcement access and child safety obligations, while Discord — which faces similar regulatory pressures — is expanding it. This divergence will force a policy conversation about whether E2E encryption and child safety are inherently in tension or whether, as Discord appears to be arguing, proactive safety tools like content hashing can coexist with encryption. For European regulators, Discord's rollout raises fresh questions under CSAM regulations requiring platforms to scan communications for child exploitation material — regulations that are technically incompatible with true E2E encryption and are currently the subject of litigation before the EU Court of Justice.

**Lead:** Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson said at a Washington lawyers' gathering on May 19 that the Supreme Court's accelerated timeline in Louisiana v. Callais undermined its appearance of impartiality by clearing the way for Republican redistricters before the 2026 midterms.

**Context:** In remarks to a lawyers' gathering in Washington, D.C., on May 19, 2026, Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson criticized the Supreme Court's decision to expedite its ruling in Louisiana v. Callais — the major Voting Rights Act case decided 6-3 in late April — arguing that the shortened deliberative timeline made the Court appear partisan. Jackson contended that the accelerated schedule "paved the way for Republicans in Louisiana" to draw new congressional maps before the 2026 midterm elections, giving a structural benefit to one political party. The Callais decision, written by Justice Samuel Alito, struck down Louisiana's two-majority-Black-district congressional map, holding that the Constitution "almost never permits" race-based map-drawing. Justice Elena Kagan's dissent called the ruling an effective gutting of the Voting Rights Act. The NAACP subsequently called for boycotts of athletic programs in seven states affected by post-Callais redistricting. Jackson's remarks are the latest in a series of public statements by justices addressing the Court's legitimacy — but she has been notably more willing than colleagues, including Chief Justice Roberts, to criticize the institution directly and by name.

**Why it matters:** Jackson's public criticism is legally significant because it ventilates, from inside the Court, an argument that the majority's procedural choices — not just its doctrinal holdings — are producing partisan effects. The Purcell principle, which the Court invokes to avoid changing election rules close to voting dates, was applied here to expedite rather than delay, creating a timeline that benefited Republican mapmakers in Louisiana and potentially in Virginia and other states. Critics have argued the Court has selectively invoked Purcell depending on which party benefits, and Jackson's remarks lend institutional weight to that critique. The downstream effects of Callais are already materializing: Alabama has moved to use a previously blocked congressional map, Virginia's redistricting battles have reached the Supreme Court, and the NAACP's boycott campaign reflects the perception that the ruling forecloses Voting Rights Act remedies for minority communities nationwide. For Court-watchers, this moment signals deepening internal tensions between the conservative majority and the three liberal justices that could shape how future requests for expedited decisions are handled — or challenged.

## [law] First Amendment victory: Tennessee man jailed 37 days for Facebook Trump meme wins $835,000 settlement from county

**Source:** <https://www.fire.org/news/victory-tennessee-man-jailed-37-days-trump-meme-wins-835000-settlement-after-first-amendment>

**Lead:** Retired law enforcement officer Larry Bushart, jailed for 37 days by Perry County, Tennessee after posting a Facebook meme quoting Donald Trump, won an $835,000 First Amendment settlement after FIRE filed a federal civil rights lawsuit on his behalf in December 2025.

**Context:** Larry Bushart, a retired Tennessee law enforcement officer, posted a Facebook meme quoting Donald Trump saying "We have to get over it" in response to a vigil for assassinated conservative activist Charlie Kirk. Though the meme referenced a 2024 shooting in Perry, Iowa, Perry County, Tennessee Sheriff Nick Weems obtained an arrest warrant claiming the post was a threat against Perry County High School — a school located over 500 miles from the Iowa event referenced in the meme. Bushart was arrested and held in jail for 37 days. In December 2025, FIRE (the Foundation for Individual Rights and Expression) and the law firm Phillips & Phillips filed a federal civil rights lawsuit in federal court on Bushart's behalf. The settlement of $835,000, paid by Perry County and the involved law enforcement officials, was announced on May 20, 2026. FIRE attorney Adam Steinbaugh stated: "No one should be hauled off to jail in the dark of night over a harmless meme just because authorities disagree with its message."

**Why it matters:** This case illustrates a documented pattern of law enforcement officers using facially neutral "threat" statutes to punish political speech that officials personally find objectionable — an abuse particularly dangerous when targeted at commentary on public officials or political figures. The $835,000 payout by a small Tennessee county constitutes a substantial deterrent signal to local law enforcement agencies that content-based arrests of protected political speech will generate significant civil liability under 42 U.S.C. § 1983. For First Amendment practitioners, the case is notable because the meme clearly constituted political speech — a quote from a sitting ex-president about a political event — with no objectively reasonable basis for interpreting it as a specific, credible threat. The case also highlights the disconnect between the jurisdictions named in the alleged "threat" (Iowa school shooting) and the jurisdiction making the arrest (Tennessee county), suggesting the arrest was motivated by the speech content rather than any genuine threat assessment. As AI-generated political content becomes more prevalent and authorities increasingly cite threats of online speech to justify enforcement actions, cases like Bushart's — with outsized settlements — establish important precedents about the financial consequences of viewpoint-discriminatory policing.

**Why it matters:** The Lithuania incident matters as both a security milestone and a precedent-setting test of NATO's collective posture in the Eastern flank. The incident will intensify pressure on NATO to formalize its rules of engagement for counter-drone operations in member state airspace — a legal and military gap that has already been tested by the Estonia drone shootdown earlier this month.
