## [law] The Supreme Court's selective abandonment of Purcell has effectively nullified VRA Section 2, Chemerinsky argues

**Source:** [scotusblog.com](https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/05/rethinking-a-supreme-court-principle-used-to-undermine-the-voting-rights-act/)

**Lead:** Berkeley Law Dean Erwin Chemerinsky argues that the Supreme Court's conservative majority has selectively applied the Purcell election-timing principle only when doing so blocks Voting Rights Act plaintiffs, exposing the doctrine as a political tool rather than a neutral judicial rule.

**Context:** The Purcell principle — originating from Purcell v. Gonzalez (2006) — holds that federal courts should hesitate to enjoin election laws on the eve of voting to avoid disrupting election administration. The Court invoked it in 2020 to stay a Wisconsin order extending mail-in ballot deadlines, and expanded its reach significantly in Merrill v. Milligan (2022), when it stayed a lower-court finding that Alabama's congressional map violated Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act through racial gerrymandering — even though the five conservative justices did not dispute the legal conclusions below. In Abbott v. League of United Latin American Citizens (December 2025), the Court similarly stayed a Texas redistricting injunction invoking Purcell despite the election being months away. Then came Louisiana v. Callais, decided April 29, 2026, fewer than three weeks before Louisiana's congressional primary and with mail-in ballots already distributed — a far more acute timing problem than any of the earlier cases. Justice Alito's majority opinion did not mention Purcell at all. Governor Landry then suspended the primary entirely, and the ruling triggered cascading disruptions: Florida, Tennessee, and South Carolina each redrew or postponed primaries after Callais. Chemerinsky identifies the core contradiction: the same justices who wielded Purcell repeatedly against minority plaintiffs ignored it completely when the doctrinal tool would have worked against a result they favored.

**Why it matters:** The Callais decision has already prompted a structural cascade — three states disrupted their primary schedules — which is precisely the harm Purcell was meant to prevent. If the principle's real function is to protect incumbents and majority-favored maps rather than to safeguard election administration generally, it has become a one-sided legal weapon rather than a neutral doctrine. Chemerinsky argues the Court should either apply Purcell consistently or abandon it, because selective application destroys the institutional legitimacy the rule is supposed to preserve. For Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act, the practical effect of the Callais ruling — combined with the pattern of Purcell stays blocking remedial redistricting — is that minority plaintiffs who win at trial may still be unable to obtain electoral relief before the courts grant yet another last-minute stay. The decision arrives as Section 5 preclearance has been gutted since Shelby County v. Holder (2013), leaving Section 2 litigation as the primary remaining federal voting-rights tool. If that tool is effectively nullified by strategic use of Purcell, the VRA's operational capacity is substantially diminished regardless of its text.

## [surveillance law] Trump administration misses May 15 deadline to declassify secret FISA court ruling documenting Section 702 constitutional violations

**Source:** [techdirt.com](https://www.techdirt.com/2026/05/19/senator-wyden-again-tells-trump-administration-it-owes-the-public-access-to-a-section-702-ruling/)

**Lead:** Senator Ron Wyden is calling out the Trump administration for ignoring a congressional commitment to declassify a March 2026 FISA Court opinion that reportedly documents serious constitutional violations in the FBI and NSA's use of Section 702 surveillance powers.

**Context:** Section 702 of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act authorizes the NSA to collect communications of foreign targets abroad, but the program also sweeps up American citizens' data, which the FBI can then query without a warrant. A FISA Court judge issued an opinion on March 17, 2026, imposing conditions on the program's continued operation and raising concerns about filtering tools the FBI, NSA, and other agencies use to process raw Section 702 data — tools the court found were being applied in ways that violated the program's guidelines and, according to Wyden, Americans' constitutional rights. When Congress passed a 45-day extension of Section 702 in late April, Senate Intelligence Committee chairs Tom Cotton and Mark Warner committed to pushing for declassification of the opinion within 15 days — i.e., by May 15, 2026. The Trump administration has declined to comply. Wyden publicly stated the administration "cannot be trusted with unfettered, warrantless surveillance of Americans," and noted that the executive branch has ignored a formal request from both the chair and vice-chair of the Senate Intelligence Committee. The administration has also refused to implement the reforms the FISA Court required as a condition of its limited blessing for continued Section 702 use.

**Why it matters:** Congress is now approaching a binding reauthorization vote on Section 702 without access to the court opinion that documented what went wrong with the program — precisely the classified information lawmakers need to make an informed decision. The pattern is structurally similar to the pre-Snowden era: an executive branch that claims surveillance authorities are legally sound while courts find constitutional violations in sealed proceedings that the public and most of Congress cannot read. If the Trump administration can refuse to declassify an opinion even when both Senate Intelligence Committee leaders formally request it, the legal oversight mechanism for Section 702 is effectively a closed loop — agency, secret court, and executive branch — with no meaningful democratic check. The Section 702 authority expires unless reauthorized, giving Congress formal leverage; but Wyden's letter implies the administration expects to win reauthorization regardless, betting Congress will not allow the program to lapse even without transparency. Surveillance law reformers are watching whether Tom Cotton — who requested declassification but also supports Section 702 — will use his committee chair position to enforce the commitment or quietly defer to the administration.

## [cybersecurity] Microsoft dismantles Fox Tempest, a malware-signing-as-a-service platform that provided Rhysida, Akira, and Qilin ransomware gangs with fraudulent code-signing certificates

**Source:** [therecord.media](https://therecord.media/microsoft-disrupts-fox-tempest-malware-signing-service)

**Lead:** Microsoft has unsealed a federal court case and executed a takedown of Fox Tempest, a sophisticated malware-signing-as-a-service operation that created over 1,000 fraudulent code-signing certificates and charged ransomware affiliates thousands of dollars per transaction to make their malware appear legitimate.

**Context:** Fox Tempest emerged in May 2025 and operated as a well-resourced criminal enterprise with dedicated departments for infrastructure, customer relations, and finances. The platform allowed ransomware affiliates to upload malware, receive short-lived fraudulent certificates from Microsoft's own Artifact Signing infrastructure, and distribute signed payloads through spoofed websites impersonating legitimate software providers such as AnyDesk, Teams, PuTTY, and Webex. Major ransomware groups that relied on the service include Rhysida, INC, Qilin, and Akira. Microsoft's May 2026 takedown seized Fox Tempest's website, took hundreds of virtual machines offline, blocked access to hosting infrastructure, and revoked more than 1,000 fraudulent certificates. The company identified malware families distributed through the platform including Oyster, Lumma Stealer, and Vidar. Steven Masada, Microsoft's assistant general counsel, noted that "when attackers can make malicious software look legitimate, it undermines how people and systems decide what's safe." Cryptocurrency analysis revealed the platform generated millions in revenue.

**Why it matters:** Fox Tempest's disruption highlights a structural vulnerability in the code-signing trust model: criminal actors found a way to weaponize Microsoft's own legitimate certificate infrastructure against Windows security controls, allowing ransomware to "slip through the front door." The service-model approach — letting ransomware gangs outsource the hard problem of appearing legitimate — lowers the technical barrier for groups that can pay but lack the expertise to forge certificates independently. Microsoft's use of the federal court system to execute a technical takedown via injunctive relief reflects an increasingly proactive legal strategy the company has used against previous criminal infrastructure. For enterprises, the takedown is a reminder that signed software is not the same as safe software: the same security controls that flag unsigned executables can be bypassed by a fraudulent certificate indistinguishable to most endpoint tools from a legitimate one. The revocation of 1,000+ certificates creates a remediation challenge for any organization that may have deployed Fox Tempest-signed software in a legitimate-seeming form.

**Source:** [safedep.io](https://safedep.io/mini-shai-hulud-strikes-again-314-npm-packages-compromised/)

**Lead:** A coordinated supply-chain attack on May 19 published malicious versions of 314 npm packages via a single compromised maintainer account, deploying a 498KB obfuscated payload that harvests AWS credentials, npm tokens, SSH keys, password manager vaults, and targets Claude Code hooks and GitHub Actions pipelines.

**Context:** The attack, documented by SafeDep researchers and dubbed "Mini Shai-Hulud," unfolded across two rapid waves totaling 22 minutes. The compromised account — "atool" — published 637 malicious versions across 317 packages. High-profile targets include size-sensor (4.2M downloads/month), echarts-for-react (3.8M/month), and numerous @antv scoped packages. The malware was delivered through two redundant paths: a preinstall script executing via Bun, and optional GitHub dependencies pointing to orphan commits in the legitimate antvis/G2 repository. The payload harvested credentials across multiple layers: AWS (including EC2 metadata and ECS container credentials), Kubernetes service tokens, HashiCorp Vault, npm access tokens, GitHub personal access tokens, SSH private keys, and vaults from 1Password, Bitwarden, pass, and gopass. Exfiltration ran through dual channels — GitHub's API disguised as legitimate commits, and encrypted HTTPS posts to t.m-kosche[.]com masquerading as OpenTelemetry trace data. Additional capabilities included GitHub Actions workflow injection, Claude Code hook hijacking, VS Code task injection, Docker container escape attempts, and persistent systemd/LaunchAgent backdoors.

**Why it matters:** The attack illustrates npm's structural single-point-of-failure problem: one compromised account token cascaded across 547 packages, weaponizing 314 of them with identical obfuscated payloads. The explicit targeting of Claude Code hooks and VS Code tasks marks a concerning evolution — attackers are now specifically engineering malware to exploit the trust relationship between AI coding assistants and the local environment they run in. The dual-exfiltration design (GitHub API plus HTTPS) makes detection harder because both channels resemble normal developer activity. Organizations relying on semver ranges for any affected package should assume compromise if they installed a new version on May 19, and should rotate AWS IAM credentials, npm tokens, GitHub PATs, and SSH keys immediately. The matching toolkit to the prior SAP attack suggests a persistent, well-resourced threat actor.

**Context:** Hash matching converts images into digital fingerprints stored in industry databases, enabling platforms to block future uploads of identical or near-identical material. Ofcom's new codes will require platforms to expand use of this technology specifically to cover NCII and AI-generated deepfakes. The announcement follows legislation introduced by PM Keir Starmer in February 2026, which imposed criminal liability on individuals who create or share NCII and required platforms to remove such material within 48 hours or face service blocks. **Why it matters:** The Ofcom mandate is among the first regulatory frameworks globally to impose technical — not merely procedural — obligations on platforms for AI-generated NCII at scale. Hash-matching databases require inter-platform cooperation, creating infrastructure questions about database maintenance, hash contribution, and error remediation. The 48-hour removal deadline with service-block penalties creates significant compliance pressure for smaller platforms that may lack the engineering capacity to deploy hash matching rapidly. The EU's simultaneous retreat from its CSAM scanning mandate creates a UK-EU regulatory divergence that complicates compliance for platforms in both markets. For AI companies, the rules signal that image-generation models with insufficient safeguards will increasingly trigger platform-level liability. The UK precedent may influence forthcoming US regulatory discussions, particularly around TAKE IT DOWN Act implementation.

## [tech law] TAKE IT DOWN Act turns one year old — researcher calls for transparency reporting on a law with no required appeals process

**Source:** [techdirt.com](https://www.techdirt.com/2026/05/19/hey-platforms-add-take-it-down-to-your-transparency-reports/)

**Lead:** Stanford Internet Observatory researcher Riana Pfefferkorn argues that platforms should voluntarily publish detailed TAKE IT DOWN Act compliance statistics in their transparency reports, warning that the law's 48-hour removal mandate, absent any required appeals process, is ripe for abuse against LGBTQ individuals, sex workers, and political dissidents.

**Context:** The TAKE IT DOWN Act (TIDA), signed one year before this May 2026 article, requires online platforms to remove non-consensual intimate imagery — whether real or AI-generated, adult or minor — within 48 hours of a valid removal request, and to make efforts to eliminate duplicates. The law applies to user-generated content platforms while exempting ISPs and email providers. Pfefferkorn argues platforms should voluntarily document: TIDA notices received, compliance rates, breakdown by victim age, average removal timeframes, reversal statistics, information about authorized agents, appeals policies, and account-strike systems. The law itself imposes no such transparency obligations. Her core concern is structural: the law has no required appeals process, no penalties for bad-faith requests, and exceptionally tight removal deadlines. A platform that wants to stay compliant has a strong incentive to remove content first and ask questions later.

**Why it matters:** The practical asymmetry in TIDA — tight removal deadlines, no appeal requirement, no abuse penalty — mirrors earlier content-removal regimes that were quickly weaponized: DMCA takedowns are regularly used to suppress criticism and political commentary. LGBTQ creators, sex educators, and activists documenting abuse are all vulnerable to bad-faith TIDA requests that a platform must honor within 48 hours or risk liability. Pfefferkorn's transparency-reporting proposal would create empirical data to evaluate the law's actual operation — how often is it used for genuine victim protection versus potential misuse — data that Congress would need to assess whether the law requires modification. Without transparency reports, the law's effects are largely opaque to researchers and lawmakers.

**Context:** The outbreak is centered in Ituri Province's Bunia, Rwampara, and Mongbwalu health zones and was declared a PHEIC — the WHO's highest alert designation, previously reserved for COVID-19, polio, and the 2022 mpox outbreak — on May 17, 2026.
