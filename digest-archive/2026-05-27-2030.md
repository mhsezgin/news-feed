## [law] SCOTUS reverses 4th Circuit: immigration judges' union speech not protected under *Garcetti*

**Link:** https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/05/court-sides-with-trump-administration-in-dispute-over-immigration-judges/

**Lead:** The Supreme Court held 6–3 that immigration judges speaking through their union about court policies and administration are speaking pursuant to their official duties under *Garcetti v. Ceballos*, stripping that speech of First Amendment protection and handing the Trump administration a significant labor relations victory.

**Context:** The National Association of Immigration Judges (NAIJ) had argued that union activity — including public statements criticizing DOJ policies on docket management, case quotas, and judicial independence — constituted citizen speech on matters of public concern entitled to constitutional protection. The Fourth Circuit agreed, distinguishing union advocacy from the judges' day-to-day official duties. The Supreme Court reversed, applying an expansive reading of the "official duties" prong of *Garcetti*: because immigration judges' core function is administering the immigration court system, speech about how that system operates falls within their official responsibilities regardless of the union vehicle through which it is expressed.

**Why it matters:** The ruling narrows First Amendment protection for public-sector union speech broadly. Federal and state agencies can now use *Garcetti* to suppress union advocacy that touches on the substance of employees' work — not just purely personal grievances. For immigration courts specifically, the decision removes a significant check on DOJ control over what immigration judges can say publicly about court administration, workload, and independence. Dissenting justices warned the majority opinion's logic would effectively immunize government employer retaliation against any substantive professional criticism channeled through union activity.

## [law] Alabama asks SCOTUS to greenlight racially discriminatory congressional map for 2026

**Link:** https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/05/alabama-urges-supreme-court-to-allow-for-use-of-congressional-map/

**Lead:** Alabama filed an emergency application with the Supreme Court seeking permission to use a congressional district map that a federal district court has already found likely violates Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act — arguing the *Purcell* principle bars courts from changing election rules too close to an election.

**Context:** After the Supreme Court's 2023 *Allen v. Milligan* ruling required Alabama to draw a second majority-Black congressional district, Alabama passed a remedial map that the district court found still packed and cracked Black voters in violation of the VRA's results test. The state now invokes *Purcell v. Gonzalez* — the doctrine that courts should not disrupt election administration close to an election — to argue the 2026 midterm cycle is already underway and any further redistricting would create confusion. Civil rights groups counter that *Purcell* cannot be weaponized to perpetuate a constitutional violation Alabama has been litigating in bad faith since 2021.

**Why it matters:** If the Court grants the application, Alabama would run the 2026 midterms under a map already adjudicated as racially discriminatory, effectively nullifying *Allen v. Milligan* for this cycle. The case tests the boundaries of *Purcell* as a doctrine: whether the principle against election-eve changes applies when the change at issue is remedying a pre-existing VRA violation rather than introducing a novel rule. A grant would also signal the current Court's appetite for revisiting VRA enforcement in a post-*Milligan* world.

## [law] The Roberts Court and the First Amendment: religion wins 100%, pure speech wins 40%

**Link:** https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/05/the-roberts-courts-record-on-the-first-amendment/

**Lead:** A comprehensive empirical study of the Roberts Court's First Amendment record through the Barrett era finds that religious liberty claimants win 100% of the time, while traditional free speech claimants — excluding religion — succeed in roughly 40% of cases, a dramatic asymmetry that reflects the Court's ideological realignment.

**Context:** The analysis, drawing on every Roberts Court First Amendment decision from 2005 to 2026, documents a steep shift beginning with Justice Barrett's 2020 confirmation. Before Barrett, the Court's free speech decisions were more ideologically diverse; since 2020, the Court has never ruled against a religious claimant in a case it accepted on the merits. Meanwhile, pure-speech cases — involving press freedom, political speech, government employee speech, and compelled speech — show much lower and more variable success rates, often depending on whether the challenger is a conservative or progressive actor. The study finds the Court has essentially developed two parallel First Amendment doctrines: an expansive, nearly absolute protection for religious exercise and expression, and a more balancing-heavy, context-sensitive framework for secular speech claims.

**Why it matters:** The doctrinal asymmetry has concrete consequences for government regulation: it is far easier constitutionally to regulate secular speech (including professional speech, commercial speech, and public employee speech) than to impose any burden on religious practice that touches expression. For practitioners, the data suggest that framing arguments in religious liberty terms — wherever available — dramatically improves odds at the current Court. Critics argue the Court has effectively created a constitutionally privileged tier for religiously motivated actors, in tension with the Establishment Clause's original anti-favoritism principle.

## [cybersecurity] FBI warns US law firms: Silent Ransom Group physically visiting offices to steal data

**Link:** https://therecord.media/fbi-warns-hackers-visit-law-firms-to-steal-data

**Lead:** The FBI issued a private industry notification warning that Silent Ransom Group — a successor to the Conti ransomware operation — has escalated beyond phishing to physically dispatching operatives to law firm offices in the US, impersonating clients or service personnel to harvest credentials and map internal systems.

**Context:** Silent Ransom Group (also tracked as Luna Moth and Chatty Spider) pivoted away from encryption-based ransomware toward data-theft-only extortion around 2022. Their standard playbook involved vishing campaigns targeting paralegal and administrative staff. The FBI's new notification documents a further escalation: physical social engineering visits in which operatives enter law firm lobbies, request to use conference rooms or computers, or engage receptionists in pretextual conversations designed to capture login credentials via shoulder-surfing or dropped USB devices. The group specifically targets firms with high-value client data — M&A, litigation, regulatory matters — that can be monetized through extortion or sold to financial actors.

**Why it matters:** Law firms are among the softest high-value targets in the economy: they hold extraordinarily sensitive client data, often have weaker physical security than financial institutions, and face attorney-client privilege constraints that complicate breach disclosure. The shift to physical intrusion represents a meaningful escalation of threat sophistication and means that purely technical defenses (MFA, phishing-resistant auth) are insufficient. Firms need physical security protocols — visitor logging, clean-desk enforcement, no-tailgating policies — as part of their cybersecurity posture. Bar associations and legal malpractice insurers will likely be paying close attention.

## [cybersecurity] Iranian MOIS behind destructive March 2026 hack of LA County Metro

**Link:** https://therecord.media/iranian-intelligence-behind-hack-of-la-transit-system

**Lead:** US and allied intelligence agencies have formally attributed the March 2026 destructive cyberattack on the Los Angeles County Metropolitan Transportation Authority to Iran's Ministry of Intelligence and Security (MOIS), marking one of the first publicly attributed Iranian destructive attacks on US civilian critical infrastructure.

**Context:** The March attack wiped servers managing fare collection and parts of the real-time operations network for LA Metro, disrupting service on several lines for days and affecting an estimated 1.5 million daily riders. Attribution to MOIS rather than IRGC-affiliated contractors is significant: MOIS is Iran's civilian foreign intelligence service, and its direct involvement in infrastructure destruction (as opposed to espionage) represents an escalation in operational mandate. The attack occurred amid heightened Iran-US tensions over nuclear negotiations, and investigators believe it may have been intended as a signaling operation — demonstrating Iranian capability to cause civilian disruption without triggering a kinetic response threshold.

**Why it matters:** The attribution establishes a new category of Iranian hybrid warfare: civilian infrastructure destruction in a major US city, executed by the state intelligence service, calibrated to cause disruption without crossing into clear acts of war. For critical infrastructure operators — particularly transit authorities, which have historically been underfunded on cybersecurity — the LA Metro case provides a concrete case study for what a destructive attack looks like and how quickly it can affect civilian life. Expect DHS CISA to issue sector-specific guidance and for Congress to revisit transit cybersecurity funding in the next appropriations cycle.

**Lead:** President Trump told reporters the United States is "not satisfied" with the current state of negotiations with Iran over its nuclear program, citing unresolved disputes over highly enriched uranium stockpiles and independent verification mechanisms as obstacles to a final agreement.

**Context:** Talks in Muscat and Vienna have produced a framework agreement on enrichment levels but have stalled on two issues: Iran's insistence on retaining a stockpile of 60%-enriched uranium (below weapons-grade but easily upgradeable) and the scope of IAEA access for third-party verification. **Why it matters:** A breakdown in Iran nuclear talks would have immediate consequence for oil markets, regional security architecture, and the Biden-era JCPOA precedent. If the US walks away, Iran is widely assessed to be within weeks of a weapons-grade breakout — the fundamental redline for both the US and Israel. An Israeli strike on Iranian nuclear facilities, long threatened, becomes significantly more probable in a no-deal scenario. For international law and nonproliferation norms, a failed deal after this level of high-profile engagement would further erode the NPT framework and embolden other near-nuclear states.

## [tech_law] UK Ofcom mandates hash-matching deepfake detection under Online Safety Act

**Link:** https://www.biometricupdate.com/202605/uk-regulator-backs-automated-systems-to-detect-explicit-deepfakes

**Lead:** UK communications regulator Ofcom has issued binding guidance under the Online Safety Act requiring major platforms to deploy proactive automated technology — specifically hash-matching and AI classifiers — to detect and remove AI-generated explicit deepfakes, including non-consensual intimate imagery and synthetic child sexual abuse material.

**Context:** The guidance implements the Online Safety Act's "proactive technology" duty, which requires Category 1 platforms (large social media, video sharing, search) to use automated systems to identify illegal content without relying solely on user reports. For explicit deepfakes specifically, Ofcom is requiring: (1) PhotoDNA-style hash-matching against known NCII and CSAM databases; (2) AI classifier deployment to identify likely-synthetic explicit imagery for human review; and (3) quarterly reporting on detection volumes and false-positive rates. Platforms have approximately six months to demonstrate compliance, with fines of up to 10% of global annual turnover for non-compliance. The guidance explicitly addresses generative AI as a distinct threat vector — not just user-uploaded content — and requires platforms to consider how their own AI generation features may be weaponized.

**Why it matters:** This is one of the first concrete, technically specific enforcement actions under any major online safety law targeting AI-generated illegal content, and it will serve as a template for regulators in the EU (DSA), Australia, and Canada who are watching Ofcom's implementation closely. The hash-matching requirement creates a practical challenge: synthetic content does not have stable hashes unless the exact same image is re-uploaded, so the burden on AI classifiers is high and false-positive risk is significant. Platforms that have invested in CSAM detection infrastructure (Meta, Google, Microsoft) will find compliance easier; smaller platforms and new entrants face substantial technical build costs. The guidance also raises extraterritoriality questions for non-UK platforms serving UK users.
