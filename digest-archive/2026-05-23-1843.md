## [law] Federal judge dismisses Abrego Garcia smuggling charges, ruling DOJ prosecuted him vindictively for winning deportation case

**Source:** npr.org  
**Link:** https://www.npr.org/2026/05/22/nx-s1-5831958/federal-judge-dismisses-criminal-charges-against-kilmar-abrego-garcia

**Lead:** A federal judge dismissed human-smuggling charges against Kilmar Abrego Garcia on May 22, finding the Trump administration reopened a 2022 traffic-stop investigation specifically because courts ordered his return from El Salvador — a constitutional violation of due process the judge called vindictive prosecution.

**Context:** Kilmar Abrego Garcia, a Salvadoran national living in Maryland, was illegally deported to El Salvador in March 2025 despite a standing immigration court protection order. After multiple federal courts — including the Supreme Court — ordered the Trump administration to facilitate his return, the Justice Department unsealed a human-smuggling indictment charging him with conspiring to transport immigrants between 2016 and 2025, based on a 2022 Tennessee traffic stop that had produced no charges at the time. U.S. District Judge Waverly Crenshaw Jr. in the Middle District of Tennessee granted Abrego Garcia's motion to dismiss, concluding that the government reopened the investigation as retaliation for his successful judicial challenge to the deportation order. Judge Crenshaw relied heavily on Attorney General Todd Blanche's own public statement, which directly confirmed the executive branch had reopened the investigation because the judicial branch required his return — language the judge found was textbook evidence of improper prosecutorial motive. The DOJ immediately announced plans to appeal.

**Why it matters:** The vindictive prosecution doctrine — rooted in Fifth Amendment due process — prohibits the government from filing or escalating charges against defendants specifically because they exercised constitutional rights, including the right to seek judicial relief. This ruling is a significant judicial rebuke of the Trump administration's practice of using criminal prosecution as a deterrent against individuals who successfully challenge immigration enforcement in court. The judge's reliance on the Attorney General's own words as primary evidence creates a durable precedent: senior executive branch officials' public statements about prosecutorial intent will be scrutinized as direct evidence against the government in subsequent criminal proceedings. If the DOJ's appeal fails, it confirms that the executive branch cannot use the charging power as a punitive tool against people who win in Article III courts — placing a constitutional ceiling on the retaliatory prosecution strategy. This case is a live test of whether separation-of-powers principles can constrain executive branch actors who publicly announce what would otherwise be inadmissible prosecutorial motive.

## [law] SCOTUS conference: Daubert expert-testimony gatekeeping and AEDPA habeas limits for a combat veteran in two consequential procedural cases

**Source:** scotusblog.com  
**Link:** https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/05/daubert-dust-up-and-aedpa-angst/

**Lead:** The Supreme Court's May 21 conference included two high-stakes procedural cases: Union Carbide v. Sommerville, testing how strictly federal courts must filter expert witnesses after a 2023 Federal Rules amendment, and Walters v. Coleman, asking whether the Fourth Circuit exceeded AEDPA's limits when it ordered plenary resentencing for an Iraq veteran with untreated PTSD.

**Context:** Union Carbide Corp. v. Sommerville arises from a toxic-tort class action in which Lee Ann Sommerville alleged ethylene oxide exposure from a South Charleston chemical plant increased her cancer risk. The district court excluded her expert's air-dispersion model as speculative, but a divided Fourth Circuit reversed — reasoning that disputes about expert inputs go to weight of the evidence, not admissibility. That outcome conflicts with how the Fifth, Sixth, Eighth, Ninth, and Federal Circuits apply the Daubert standard, particularly after a 2023 amendment to Federal Rule of Evidence 702 was designed specifically to require courts to verify sufficient factual basis before admitting expert testimony. In Walters v. Coleman, Christopher Coleman — an Iraq and Afghanistan veteran with PTSD and traumatic brain injury — received a 28-year sentence for violent crimes committed in Roanoke, Virginia in 2011. The Fourth Circuit issued a 99-page opinion ordering full resentencing after finding his trial counsel failed to present available mitigation evidence about his service-related conditions; Virginia argues that ruling disregards AEDPA's stringent de novo review prohibition and improperly extended habeas relief to claims not properly before the court.

**Why it matters:** The Daubert question has outsized practical stakes: the admissibility threshold for expert testimony determines whether plaintiffs in environmental, pharmaceutical, and products-liability class actions can survive summary judgment in federal court. A ruling tightening the standard to match the 2023 Rule 702 amendment would significantly advantage defendants in toxic-tort litigation by allowing courts to exclude contested experts at the pre-trial stage rather than letting juries weigh conflicting methodologies. For the Walters AEDPA case, the question is how far federal courts can go in correcting state sentences for defendants with documented combat-service impairments — a ruling narrowing Fourth Circuit habeas authority would reduce post-conviction relief for veterans and others whose trial counsel failed to develop mitigation evidence in violation of Strickland. Both cases also reflect broader tensions about whether circuit courts are correctly implementing recent procedural reforms: if the Court grants both cases, it signals dissatisfaction with the Fourth Circuit specifically and would mark the second term in a row the Court reversed that circuit's unusually expansive remedial approach.

**Context:** Magyar was sworn in as Prime Minister on May 9, 2026, ending Viktor Orbán's 16-year rule after Tisza won 141 of 199 parliamentary seats — the largest electoral majority in Hungary's post-Communist history. But winning a supermajority did not deliver a blank slate: Orbán's 2011 Fundamental Law was designed with entrenched provisions, a packed Constitutional Court staffed by Fidesz loyalists, and 27 cardinal laws requiring two-thirds majorities to amend. Legal scholars writing on Verfassungsblog argue that using the same Fidesz-written rules to dismantle Fidesz structures risks both conferring legitimacy on those rules and replicating the constitutional shortcuts Orbán himself used. The proposed alternative is to treat EU law — the EU Charter of Fundamental Rights, ECHR obligations, and treaty-based rule-of-law requirements — as an operative interim constitution, providing enforceable floors for democratic governance while a legitimately drafted new Fundamental Law is negotiated. The European Commission's 27 rule-of-law milestones condition €18 billion in frozen EU funds on Hungary's compliance by August 2026, giving the new government powerful external incentives to align with EU standards immediately.

**Why it matters:** The novel argument that EU law can substitute as a national constitution is untested in this form — it would require Hungarian courts to apply EU Charter provisions as the primary constitutional standard over domestic law, which is legally contentious but not unprecedented in rule-of-law conditionality contexts. If the Magyar government succeeds in using EU conditionality to anchor democratic reconstruction, it becomes a proof-of-concept for how EU institutions can function as constitutional backstops in post-autocratic transitions — a model directly applicable to any future democratic regression in Poland, Slovakia, or other EU members. The stakes of the August 2026 deadline are concrete: €18 billion in unlocked funds would transform Hungary's public finances and give the Magyar government the fiscal room to reverse 16 years of Fidesz patronage networks. Failure risks not just a democratic backslide but a 'constitutional boomerang' — scholarly shorthand for the risk that constitutional reforms imposed without broad legitimacy will themselves be vulnerable to reversal by the next government that achieves a temporary supermajority.

## [tech law] France's Hadopi copyright enforcement system is functionally dead — gutted by EU privacy rulings — but rights-holder lobbying keeps the corpse warm

**Source:** techdirt.com  
**Link:** https://www.techdirt.com/2026/05/22/frances-terrible-copyright-law-hadopi-is-not-quite-dead/

**Lead:** France's Hadopi copyright graduated-response system has been rendered unenforceable by European court rulings finding its bulk metadata retention and administrative data access violate EU privacy law, exposing a 15-year, €82 million enforcement infrastructure that generated just €87,000 in fines and a single unenforceable disconnection order.

**Context:** Hadopi — Haute Autorité pour la Diffusion des Œuvres et la Protection des droits sur Internet — launched in 2009 as Europe's model graduated-response copyright enforcement authority. Its three-strikes mechanism sent warning letters to IP addresses flagged for infringement; a third allegation within a year could trigger internet suspension or a €1,500 fine. By 2013 it had issued 2 million first notices and 200,000 second notices, but the system's enforcement record was catastrophic: one disconnection order ever, which courts subsequently refused to enforce, and a total fine haul of approximately €87,000 against annual operating costs exceeding €5 million. European courts subsequently identified two foundational defects: Hadopi's enabling legislation permitted bulk retention of connection metadata in violation of the EU's e-Privacy Directive, and its data access procedures allowed investigators to obtain subscriber data without independent judicial or quasi-judicial review. The French Conseil d'État confirmed both defects in 2026, effectively suspending enforcement, though the system has not been formally abolished due to ongoing rights-holder lobbying.

**Why it matters:** Hadopi's trajectory is a cautionary template for any IP enforcement regime that relies on mass surveillance of network metadata. The EU court rulings establish that even a narrowly targeted copyright enforcement purpose cannot justify bulk IP-address retention and administrative self-authorization for data access — independent oversight is required. This directly constrains how successor copyright enforcement systems can be designed in France and across the EU: platforms and ISPs cannot be deputized as enforcement agents through automated notice systems that rely on bulk surveillance, at least without independent review at the access stage. The UK, which inherited Hadopi-adjacent provisions in its Digital Economy Act, now faces the same structural problem post-Brexit — its enforcement architecture was never tested in ECHR courts, but any replication of Hadopi's model would face identical European Convention on Human Rights challenges. For VPN providers and privacy advocates, the ruling provides additional precedent against government demands for subscriber identification in copyright enforcement contexts, reinforcing the principle that IP addresses alone cannot be presumed to identify individual infringers.

## [tech law] Social media age bans rest on methodologically weak research, and courts are already pushing back on First Amendment grounds

**Source:** techdirt.com  
**Link:** https://www.techdirt.com/2026/05/21/the-science-is-not-settled-how-weak-evidence-is-fueling-a-national-push-to-ban-social-media/

**Lead:** A detailed analysis of the studies cited to justify state social media age bans — now enacted or advancing in Utah, Florida, California, Minnesota, and Massachusetts — finds they consistently fail to establish causation, with a 100,000-person multi-country meta-analysis showing no consistent measurable association between platform adoption and adolescent well-being decline.

**Context:** The national legislative push to ban minors from social media platforms follows prominent advocacy by researchers including Jonathan Haidt, who argues in 'The Anxious Generation' that digital platforms drove the post-2012 teen mental health crisis. But a detailed methodological review identifies multiple recurring failures in the cited research: selection bias (teens with pre-existing mental health problems use platforms more, not vice versa, making correlation unreliable); no consistent causal direction across jurisdictions; and a U-shaped association in a 100,000-subject study where moderate use correlated with better outcomes than either abstinence or heavy use — the opposite of what prohibition assumes. UC Irvine and Brown University researchers, as well as Professor Candace Odgers, have consistently rated the evidence as 'less than ironclad.' A cross-national meta-analysis failed to show a consistent measurable association between social media rollout and well-being decline across dozens of countries over time. Despite this, Utah enacted the first state ban, with Florida, California, Minnesota, and Massachusetts advancing similar or broader legislation, requiring platforms to verify minors' ages through biometric data or government ID.

**Why it matters:** The constitutional stakes are significant: courts in multiple jurisdictions have already issued injunctions blocking portions of enacted social media age-restriction laws on First Amendment grounds, reasoning that minors have independent speech rights that states cannot eliminate without a compelling government interest supported by demonstrable evidence of harm. If the scientific record does not establish a reliable causal link between platform use and harm — rather than mere correlation driven by pre-existing conditions — the compelling interest test cannot be met, and the laws will continue to fall. Independently, the age-verification mechanisms required by these laws — biometric scanning or government ID upload — impose a separate privacy harm that may exceed the risk the laws address: creating centralized databases of child-to-platform associations that become high-value targets for data breaches. An especially underappreciated policy cost: LGBTQ+, rural, and disabled youth disproportionately rely on social platforms for community access that in-person alternatives cannot replicate, making sweeping bans fall most heavily on already-vulnerable populations. California's competing proposal — education-based 'digital wellness' curricula teaching algorithmic self-regulation — may be both constitutionally sounder and more empirically grounded.
