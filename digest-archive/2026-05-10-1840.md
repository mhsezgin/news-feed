## [ai] Anthropic unveils Claude Mythos Preview, an AI that autonomously exploits zero-day vulnerabilities across every major OS

**Source:** https://red.anthropic.com/2026/mythos-preview/

**Lead:** Anthropic's Claude Mythos Preview autonomously discovered and exploited zero-day vulnerabilities in every major operating system and web browser during internal testing, prompting the company to withhold public release and launch Project Glasswing, a controlled-access defensive coalition of approximately 40 organizations.

**Context:** Claude Mythos Preview was announced April 7, 2026, as Anthropic's most capable general-purpose model to date, with exceptional capability on computer security tasks. During internal evaluation, Mythos fully autonomously identified and exploited a 17-year-old FreeBSD NFS remote code execution vulnerability allowing unauthenticated root access—with no human involvement after the initial request. On Firefox JavaScript exploits, the previous model (Opus 4.6) succeeded 2 times in several hundred attempts; Mythos succeeded 181 times, roughly a 90-fold improvement. Mythos also constructed sophisticated multi-stage attacks including JIT heap sprays and privilege escalation chains that would take human experts weeks to build. Project Glasswing, launched simultaneously, gives approximately 40 partner organizations including AWS, Apple, Microsoft, Google, CrowdStrike, and Palo Alto Networks access to Mythos for coordinated defensive vulnerability disclosure. Anthropic commits to 90-day coordinated disclosure timelines, but fewer than 1 percent of Mythos-discovered vulnerabilities have been patched to date, indicating an enormous backlog of critical unresolved flaws.

**Why it matters:** The gap between Mythos's offensive capability and current defensive patch rates is the key danger signal: the lab itself acknowledges that fewer than 1 percent of discovered vulnerabilities have been remediated, meaning the model has effectively mapped a vast attack surface that humanity cannot currently defend at speed. CEO Dario Amodei has publicly characterized this as a "narrow window" for tech firms, governments, and banks to fix vulnerabilities before adversaries independently develop comparable AI offensive tools. The decision to restrict Mythos rather than release publicly represents a significant departure from Anthropic's prior approach, and the Glasswing model may become the template for managing dual-use AI capabilities across the industry. The EU AI Act's general-purpose AI provisions include systemic-risk obligations for models like Mythos, and the case tests whether voluntary self-governance by a frontier lab is a sufficient compliance posture.

## [tech law] Bambu Lab sends cease-and-desist to OrcaSlicer developer who restored cloud-printing feature company had disabled via firmware

**Source:** https://www.xda-developers.com/developer-restored-orcaslicers-features-bambu-lab-killed-legal-threats-arrived/

**Lead:** Bambu Lab sent a cease-and-desist to Polish developer Pawel Jarczak after he published an OrcaSlicer fork restoring cloud-printing features the company had disabled via firmware in January 2025, raising unresolved questions about whether hardware manufacturers can weaponize AGPL copyright law to remove features from software they open-sourced.

**Context:** In January 2025, Bambu Lab pushed firmware updates that severed OrcaSlicer's direct cloud-printing capability, forcing users to route connections through Bambu Connect, its proprietary bridge application. Developer Pawel Jarczak created OrcaSlicer-bambulab, a fork built on Bambu Studio's AGPL-3.0 licensed source code, to restore the disabled feature. Bambu Lab's cease-and-desist accused Jarczak of impersonating Bambu Studio, bypassing authorization controls, violating terms of use, reverse engineering proprietary software, and enabling modified clients to send "arbitrary commands" to printers. Jarczak disputed each allegation, arguing his fork built exclusively on publicly available AGPL-3.0 code without redistributing any proprietary libraries. Despite believing the accusations were legally unfounded, Jarczak voluntarily removed all releases from GitHub, citing inability to afford litigation costs. Right-to-repair advocate Louis Rossmann publicly offered to pay the first $10,000 of legal costs if Bambu Lab pursued formal action.

**Why it matters:** The Bambu Lab dispute crystallizes a legal gray area never cleanly resolved in US courts: whether a hardware manufacturer can invoke DMCA Section 1201's anti-circumvention provisions to prevent owners of a physical device from using community software to communicate with that device over APIs the manufacturer controls. The AGPL-3.0 license requires source code disclosure and allows derivative works—Jarczak's core argument—but Bambu Lab's "authorization controls" claim potentially invokes computer fraud provisions rather than pure copyright, with broader chilling effect. Prior case law on printer cartridges (Lexmark) and garage door openers (Chamberlain) established that functional locking mechanisms can constitute DMCA violations, but courts have increasingly narrowed those rulings. Right-to-repair laws in California, New York, and the EU focus on physical repair access and spare parts—not software interoperability with connected devices—leaving this category legally exposed. The broader pattern—a company open-sources software to build adoption, then uses legal threats to suppress community features that undermine its subscription model—is being watched carefully by makers of connected hardware across consumer electronics, home automation, and industrial equipment.

## [law] SCOTUSblog: The "two-three-four wild card court" and what Blanche v. Lau tells us about new SCOTUS coalition dynamics

**Source:** https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/05/a-2-3-4-wild-card-court-and-blanche-v-lau-made-easy/

**Lead:** Legal analyst Rory Little's "two-three-four wild card court" framework redraws the traditional 3-3-3 model of the Supreme Court, identifying Roberts, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, and Barrett as genuinely unpredictable swing voters whose case-specific judgments now determine most outcomes.

**Context:** Little argues the Thomas-Alito bloc (solid 2) and the Sotomayor-Kagan-Jackson bloc (solid 3) are stable, but Roberts, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, and Barrett vote as swing votes rather than a unified bloc, making coalition-building unpredictable case by case. The most surprising repositioning is Gorsuch: once assumed to be a reliable fifth conservative vote, Little identifies him as a wild card based on his textualism applied in ways favoring criminal defendants, immigrants, and Native American interests against government overreach. Blanche v. Lau illustrates the model: Muk Choi Lau, a lawful permanent resident since 2007, was detained at JFK upon return from China when immigration officers found a felony trademark counterfeiting indictment in an FBI database; the Second Circuit ruled the government lacked "clear and convincing evidence" at the time of reentry as required. The government appeals to SCOTUS, asking whether a pending felony indictment alone suffices for border officers to detain an LPR. Little proposes a narrow holding—pending charges should suffice for preliminary parole pending removal proceedings—that could attract a supermajority while avoiding a broad proof-standard ruling.

**Why it matters:** The 2-3-4 wild card framework has immediate practical significance for lawyers and advocates: if four justices are genuinely unpredictable rather than two, the Court becomes harder to model strategically, making oral argument more influential. For immigration law, Blanche v. Lau asks whether LPRs returning after international travel can be detained based on criminal database hits without a full evidentiary hearing—a question affecting hundreds of thousands of permanent residents who travel internationally. If the Court validates the government's position, it would effectively give border officers broad detention authority over LPRs with pending criminal charges regardless of the underlying case's strength. A narrow ruling along Little's proposed lines—parole pending removal proceedings is acceptable without full "clear and convincing" proof—would give both sides a partial win and is the outcome most consistent with the 2-3-4 model. The case is also a test of how Gorsuch's textualism plays out in immigration law, with the INA's "at the time of" language central to the dispute.

## [global politics] Australia's One Nation wins historic first lower house seat in Farrer by-election, ending 77-year Coalition hold

**Source:** https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cwy2e4z4npro

**Lead:** Australia's far-right One Nation party won its first-ever seat in the House of Representatives on May 10, with David Farley capturing 59.1 percent of the vote in the Farrer by-election and ending a 77-year Coalition hold on the rural New South Wales electorate.

**Context:** The Farrer by-election was called after Sussan Ley, leader of the centre-right Liberal Party, resigned the seat in February 2026. Farley, a former agribusiness consultant, defeated independent Michelle Milthorpe with 59.1 percent to 40.8 percent in the sprawling southern New South Wales electorate covering agricultural and rural communities long dominated by the Nationals and Liberals. One Nation, founded by Senator Pauline Hanson in 1997, has held Senate seats and state legislature seats but had never previously won a federal lower house seat in its 30-year history—making Saturday's result historic. The win does not materially shift power in the lower house, where Labor holds 94 of 150 seats, but it marks a consolidation of far-right populist support in regional Australia that analysts have tracked since One Nation's strong Senate performance in 2016. Farley ran on immigration restriction, domestic energy independence, and rural economic grievances—themes that resonate in an electorate with high fuel costs, declining agricultural margins, and skepticism of urban-driven climate policy.

**Why it matters:** One Nation's lower house breakthrough confirms that the 2016 Senate surge was not an aberration and that far-right populism has embedded itself structurally in Australian federal politics for the first time. The Farrer result fits a pattern visible in France (Le Pen's National Rally), Italy (Fratelli d'Italia), Hungary (Fidesz's consolidation), and now Australia—regional communities feeling economically marginalised by urban policy priorities are shifting toward nationalist-populist parties previously viewed as protest vehicles rather than governing forces. For the Liberal-National Coalition, the result signals a serious challenge: Farrer was a safe Coalition seat for nearly eight decades, and its loss to One Nation rather than Labor or an independent suggests the Coalition's rural base is fragmenting from the right, not just the centre. The result comes as Australia debates immigration levels, net-zero emissions targets, and housing costs—where One Nation's positions have measurable public support in non-metropolitan areas, making the party a potential kingmaker in future minority government scenarios. The international coverage of the result reflects growing attention to Australia as a test case for whether right-wing populism can achieve durable legislative presence in Westminster systems with compulsory voting.
