Project Glasswing is Anthropic implicitly arguing that Mythos has crossed a capability threshold that makes voluntary defensive programs by developers insufficient — and that proactive scanning by the AI developer is a responsibility that comes with releasing such systems. ## [cybersecurity] FBI warns of Kali365 phishing-as-a-service that defeats Microsoft 365 MFA via OAuth token hijacking

**Link:** https://therecord.media/fbi-warns-of-kali365-phishing-attacks

**Lead:** The FBI issued a warning that Kali365, a Telegram-based phishing-as-a-service platform priced at $250 to $2,000 per month, enabled hundreds of Microsoft 365 account takeovers in April 2026 by bypassing multi-factor authentication through OAuth token hijacking — giving attackers persistent access to Outlook, Teams, and OneDrive without ever capturing a password.

**Context:** Kali365 is a phishing-as-a-service (PhaaS) platform distributed through Telegram that sells complete attack infrastructure for targeting corporate Microsoft 365 accounts. Pricing runs $250 for 30 days, $750 for 90 days, or $2,000 for 365 days, with brand-specific phishing lures mimicking Adobe, DocuSign, and SharePoint in dozens of languages — including AI-generated variants tailored to specific targets. The critical technical feature is OAuth token capture rather than credential theft: attackers send victims emails directing them to what appear to be legitimate Microsoft 365 authorization flows; victims unknowingly grant device authorization to the attacker's application, producing an OAuth token that persists for weeks or months and provides access to Outlook, Teams, and OneDrive without a password or MFA code. The FBI advisory documented hundreds of attacks using Kali365 in April 2026 alone, corroborated by Proofpoint, IBM, Huntress, and Arctic Wolf. Because no credential is transmitted and no traditional MFA challenge is failed, standard security event logs often do not flag the compromise immediately.

**Why it matters:** MFA is embedded in US government and corporate security guidance as the essential defense against credential phishing — widely cited as stopping approximately 99% of automated account attacks. Kali365's OAuth token approach systematically defeats that assumption: because it harvests authorization tokens rather than passwords, it is effective against SMS codes, authenticator apps, and hardware tokens. The FBI advisory signals this technique has crossed from sophisticated targeted attacks into a commoditized service available for $250 per month — historically the threshold at which a threat technique saturates the criminal market and dramatically increases attack volume. For corporate security teams, the advisory necessitates shifting from credential-focused monitoring to token-anomaly detection: watching for tokens issued to unexpected device types, from unusual locations, or with scopes inconsistent with normal user behavior. Microsoft's Conditional Access policies — particularly device compliance requirements and Continuous Access Evaluation — are the primary technical controls that can retroactively limit token abuse, and organizations that have not deployed them are directly exposed to a mass-market attack service. The explicit "AI-generated phishing lures" capability confirms that generative AI is already actively improving the quality and scale of commodity cybercrime tooling, a fact regulators have been slow to address in AI liability frameworks.

## [law] Taiwan's Constitutional Court paralyzed for 18 months as opposition blocks all presidential nominees — a legislated deadlock with direct implications for democratic resilience

**Link:** https://verfassungsblog.de/a-perfect-constitutional-storm/

**Lead:** Taiwan's Constitutional Court has been effectively paralyzed since October 2024 because opposition parties controlling the legislature have rejected all of President Lai Ching-te's judicial nominees and legislated a 10-justice quorum threshold the eight seated justices cannot meet — a deliberate constitutional deadlock that eliminates judicial review of executive and legislative action at a moment of acute cross-strait security tension.

**Context:** Taiwan's Constitutional Court nominally has 15 grand justices with staggered eight-year terms; seven retired in October 2024, leaving eight seated. The opposition Kuomintang (KMT) and Taiwan People's Party (TPP), which together hold a legislative majority, exploited this vacancy by passing amendments raising the minimum deliberation quorum from eight to ten justices and requiring nine votes to invalidate any law — thresholds the eight remaining justices cannot satisfy. President Lai Ching-te submitted a first list of seven nominees in December 2024; the legislature rejected all seven on December 24. He submitted a second list in July 2025; all were rejected again. The legislature simultaneously refused to review the central government's 2026 annual budget, forcing the executive to operate under prior-year appropriations — a fiscal deadlock compounding the judicial one. In May 2026, a KMT lawmaker and the Presidential Office Secretary-General publicly traded accusations over the impasse. Constitutional law scholar Ming-Sung Kuo at the University of Warwick describes the situation as a "constitutional grey hole": the mechanism designed to resolve constitutional disputes has itself been disabled through constitutionally permissible means.

**Why it matters:** A constitutional court that cannot convene is not merely an institutional inconvenience — it is a structural vulnerability with direct implications for Taiwan's capacity to respond legally to emergency legislation, to adjudicate separation of powers disputes, and to constrain any government overreach during a crisis. Taiwan faces a security environment in which China regularly tests its response capacity; constitutional paralysis means any Taiwan government action during a cross-strait provocation — emergency security decrees, mobilization orders, emergency budgetary authority — cannot be challenged or constrained judicially, removing a key institutional check at the moment it is most needed. The opposition's tactic is internally consistent within democratic proceduralism — they used their legislative majority to change quorum rules, then refused nominees who would break the deadlock — which is precisely what makes it so effective as a democratic-backsliding technique: it produces constitutional paralysis without the visibility of formally abolishing the court. This "grey hole" approach is increasingly observed globally, from Poland's Sejm to Hungary's Kúria, and Taiwan's version is notable because it is being deployed by opposition parties worried about executive overreach rather than by a ruling party consolidating power — yet the constitutional effect is identical. For US, Japanese, and EU partners who assess Taiwan's democratic resilience as a component of cross-strait deterrence credibility, the judicial deadlock is a deteriorating institutional indicator that adversaries can exploit rhetorically.
