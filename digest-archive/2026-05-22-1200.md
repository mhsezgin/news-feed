**Source:** https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/5/22/us-pausing-14bn-arms-sale-to-taiwan-due-to-iran-war-navy-chief-says

**Lead:** Acting Navy Secretary Hung Cao told a Senate panel Thursday that the US has paused a $14 billion arms sale to Taiwan to ensure sufficient munitions are available for the ongoing Iran war operation known as Epic Fury.

**Context:** Acting Navy Secretary Hung Cao testified before the Senate Appropriations Subcommittee on Defense on May 22 that the authorized $14 billion foreign military sale package to Taiwan — which includes advanced missiles and munitions central to Taiwan's layered defense — has been placed on "pause" to preserve stockpiles for Operation Epic Fury, the ongoing US military campaign against Iran. Cao said the administration has "plenty" of munitions for the Iran operation and promised the Taiwan sale would resume once the administration decided. The announcement followed Trump's May 14 Beijing summit with Chinese President Xi Jinping, where Trump stated the Taiwan sale was on the table and suggested it could function as a "negotiating chip" — a direct break from four decades of US policy against consulting Beijing on Taiwan arms decisions under the Taiwan Relations Act. Taiwan Premier Cho Jung-tai publicly urged Washington to continue the sales as essential for regional stability, while Crisis Group analyst William Yang warned the pause will "exacerbate anxiety and skepticism about US support in Taiwan" and make it politically difficult for Taipei to seek additional defense budget authorizations. The Taiwan Relations Act of 1979 requires the US government to provide Taiwan with "defense articles and defense services in such quantity as may be necessary to enable Taiwan to maintain a sufficient self-defense capability," a statutory obligation now in tension with Pentagon readiness demands.

**Why it matters:** If the US treats a legally mandated arms sale as a diplomatic concession tradeable with China — Beijing's single most emphasized red line — it signals a structural shift in America's deterrence commitment that China's military planners, who have spent decades studying US Indo-Pacific readiness gaps, will immediately register. The public disclosure of insufficient munitions production capacity to sustain simultaneous operations in the Middle East and the Pacific is a strategic intelligence gift: it confirms what was previously only estimated about America's two-theater problem. For Taiwan's defense establishment, the message is that US supply cannot be relied upon when American operational priorities compete — accelerating investment in domestic weapons production and alternative suppliers. Congressional hawks are likely to respond with legislation establishing mandatory floor-level arms delivery timelines that constrain future executive discretion, which may paradoxically tie the next administration's Taiwan policy more tightly. The Iran war's spillover into Indo-Pacific security commitments represents the most significant US credibility test in the region since the Trump administration's first term.

## [global politics] Alberta announces public vote on whether to pursue formal independence referendum from Canada

**Source:** https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cvgze8n5dxko

**Lead:** Alberta Premier Danielle Smith announced an October 19, 2026 referendum asking voters whether to pursue a binding vote on separating from Canada, after separatists delivered over 300,000 signatures, but a court ruling found the process unconstitutional for failing to consult First Nations.

**Context:** Alberta separatists submitted over 300,000 signatures to provincial authorities — nearly double the 178,000 required for a citizen-led referendum petition — reflecting a surge in alienation from federal policies on climate, equalization payments, and natural resource regulation. Premier Danielle Smith announced the United Conservative Party government would hold an October 19, 2026 referendum with several constitutional questions, including one asking voters whether to pursue a formal binding independence referendum. However, on May 13, Alberta Justice Shaina Leonard issued a significant ruling finding the entire referendum process unconstitutional on the grounds that Alberta's First Nations peoples — whose treaty rights predate Canadian confederation and cover substantial portions of the province — were never consulted, and that separation would violate those treaty rights. Premier Smith announced she will appeal the decision and is considering using executive authority to add independence questions to the October ballot over the court ruling. Current polling shows approximately 30 percent of Alberta residents favor independence, up sharply from pre-2024 levels, driven by anger at federal carbon taxes and what many Albertans perceive as structural economic disadvantage within confederation despite generating roughly 17 percent of Canada's GDP with less than 12 percent of its population.

**Why it matters:** Alberta's independence movement is the most consequential secessionist crisis Canada has faced since Quebec's 1995 independence referendum, which failed by fewer than 50,000 votes. Alberta controls the majority of Canada's conventional oil and gas production and anchors the Trans Mountain Pipeline system; independence would fracture the Canadian fiscal union and eliminate billions in equalization transfers redistributed annually to other provinces. The First Nations constitutional barrier is substantive rather than procedural — under the Supreme Court of Canada's precedents, Indigenous treaty rights create duties that cannot be overridden by provincial legislation or referenda, and a vote held in defiance of those duties would have no legal standing. For Canada's federal government, aggressively challenging the movement risks inflaming Albertan grievances; offering fiscal concessions risks incentivizing Quebec and other provinces to make similar demands. For international bond markets and energy sector investors, even a credible 30-percent polling figure introduces sovereign risk pricing into long-term Canadian energy infrastructure assets and the Canadian dollar.

## [global politics] Iran establishes formal Hormuz transit authority to charge ships for passage, directly defying international maritime law

**Source:** https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cq5py64gvwzo

**Lead:** Iran's Supreme National Security Council established a formal Persian Gulf Strait Authority empowered to collect passage fees from ships transiting the Strait of Hormuz, with some vessels reportedly already paying up to $2 million per transit, in direct violation of UNCLOS transit passage rights.

**Context:** Iran initially declared the Strait of Hormuz "closed" in March 2026 and threatened military action against ships attempting to pass; it has since institutionalized its control by establishing the Persian Gulf Strait Authority, a formal governmental body with a mandate to collect transit fees from commercial vessels, with reports indicating some ships have already paid fees reaching $2 million per transit rather than risk confrontation. The Iranian parliament separately approved the toll regime to enforce Tehran's assertion of "sovereign role" over the strait — a claim directly contradicted by the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS), under which all nations have the right of "transit passage" through international straits that connect the high seas, with coastal states explicitly prohibited from suspending or charging for such transit. The Strait carries roughly 20 percent of the world's daily oil and LNG supply; the UK has deployed drones, fighter aircraft, and a Royal Navy warship to an international defensive mission protecting commercial shipping in the waterway. At their May 14 Beijing summit, US President Donald Trump and Chinese President Xi Jinping issued a joint statement declaring Hormuz must remain open, with China explicitly opposing both militarization and tolls — a significant alignment given China's status as the world's largest buyer of Gulf oil.

**Why it matters:** Iran's establishment of a permanent governmental transit authority — rather than ad hoc naval interference — creates an institutional mechanism that will persist beyond any ceasefire and sets an internationally dangerous precedent: that a coastal state can impose fees on transit through an international strait by simply constituting a regulatory body. The UNCLOS framework exists precisely to prevent such rent-seeking behavior at maritime chokepoints, and Iran's direct defiance of it challenges the entire rules-based maritime order that enables $4 trillion in annual seaborne trade. China's joint statement against the toll regime is strategically significant: Beijing's interest in unimpeded Gulf oil imports currently outweighs its interest in the precedent Iran's model might set for Chinese claims in the South China Sea — but that calculus could shift. International shipping insurers have already repriced Gulf routes substantially, and any sustained enforcement of the toll regime could permanently redirect trade through alternative routes, changing global refinery investment and LNG terminal location decisions. Watch for the US and European navies to test Iranian enforcement of the toll regime directly, which would force Iran to either back down or escalate into a formal confrontation with legal implications under the law of naval warfare.

## [cybersecurity] Kimwolf botmaster "Dort" arrested in Canada, charged in US with running 30-Tbps DDoS-for-hire botnet and swatting security researchers

**Source:** https://krebsonsecurity.com/2026/05/alleged-kimwolf-botmaster-dort-arrested-charged-in-u-s-and-canada/

**Lead:** Canadian authorities and the FBI arrested 23-year-old Jacob Butler (alias "Dort") in Ottawa, charging him in both the US and Canada for operating Kimwolf, an IoT botnet that launched DDoS attacks reaching nearly 30 terabits per second and for conducting swatting campaigns against security researchers who exposed it.

**Context:** Jacob Butler, a 23-year-old Ottawa resident operating as "Dort," was arrested by Ontario Provincial Police and now faces criminal charges on both sides of the border: in the United States, aiding and abetting computer intrusion with a maximum 10-year sentence; in Canada, unauthorized computer use and mischief related to computer data. Butler allegedly constructed and operated Kimwolf, malware targeting IoT devices including digital photo frames and web cameras, which he both rented to other cybercriminals as DDoS-for-hire and operated directly, achieving attack volumes of nearly 30 terabits per second — a record at the time — and issuing more than 25,000 attack commands, with individual victim losses exceeding $1 million. On March 19, law enforcement simultaneously dismantled Kimwolf's technical infrastructure and three competing botnets — Aisuru, JackSkid, and Mossad — in a coordinated operation designed to maximize disruption across the DDoS-for-hire market. Beyond the botnet operation, Butler conducted swatting attacks and harassment campaigns against security researchers, including two swatting attempts against Ben Brundage, founder of Synthient, which had publicly disclosed Kimwolf vulnerabilities; Butler was identified through IP address analysis, online account records, payment transaction history, and encrypted messaging data.

**Why it matters:** The simultaneous takedown of four competing IoT botnets signals a shift in law enforcement strategy: rather than disrupting individual actors sequentially, agencies are targeting the entire DDoS-for-hire ecosystem in coordinated operations that deny the market multiple alternatives at once. The swatting charges against security researchers establish an important precedent — using false emergency reports to terrorize defenders is now treated as a distinct criminal offense alongside the underlying cybercrime, raising the cost of researcher intimidation as a counter-tactic. The dual US-Canada prosecution maximizes sentencing exposure across two legal systems, and the FBI's involvement through Ontario Provincial Police creates a template for cross-border coordination that other nations can replicate against actors who shelter in jurisdictions with lower penalties. For ransomware operators, DDoS extortion groups, and other Kimwolf customers, the infrastructure seizure means both service disruption and the risk that transaction records, attack logs, and communications captured from Kimwolf's servers can now be used to identify and prosecute the downstream criminal ecosystem Butler served.

## [tech law] CISA opens Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog to external researcher nominations, eyes cutting patch deadlines from three weeks to three days

**Source:** https://therecord.media/cisa-to-allow-researchers-to-report-vulnerabilities-kev

**Lead:** CISA launched a formal submission portal allowing security researchers, vendors, and industry partners to nominate vulnerabilities for its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog, replacing informal email processes, and is considering reducing mandatory federal patch windows from three weeks to three days for AI-accelerated high-severity threats.

**Context:** The Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency announced a new standardized nomination form enabling external security researchers, software vendors, and industry partners to submit candidate vulnerabilities for the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog — the primary US government list of bugs confirmed as actively exploited in the wild. The process replaces informal email channels with structured evidence-based submissions requiring documentation of active exploitation, incorporating quality controls intended to prevent false reporting while reducing the delay between researcher discovery and catalog listing. Empirical data shows that organizations remediate vulnerabilities listed on the KEV 3.5 times faster than bugs not on the catalog, making the speed of additions a direct driver of defensive response across both federal agencies and critical infrastructure operators. Former CISA CIO Robert Costello said the standardized process enables "faster KEV additions and, ultimately, faster defensive action across the whole ecosystem." CISA is also reportedly considering reducing mandatory federal patch deadlines from the current three-week window under BOD 22-01 to as little as three days for certain high-severity threats — a potential response to AI-driven acceleration of exploitation timelines, where zero-day weaponization now often occurs within hours of public vulnerability disclosure.

**Why it matters:** The KEV catalog is the operational backbone of the November 2021 Binding Operational Directive that requires all federal civilian agencies to patch listed vulnerabilities within set deadlines; it is also widely adopted by critical infrastructure operators, state governments, and international allies as the de facto authoritative list of actively exploited bugs. Opening nominations to the broader security community closes the gap between when researchers discover active exploitation and when CISA can verify and publish — currently measured in days to weeks, during which attackers maintain access while defenders wait for confirmation. The potential three-day patch deadline would be transformative for federal IT operations: moving from three weeks to three days requires automated patch pipelines, real-time vulnerability inventory, and testing infrastructure that most agencies currently lack, effectively forcing a modernization investment. For software vendors, the nomination portal creates new due-diligence pressure — failure to proactively report known exploited vulnerabilities will increasingly be treated as a compliance failure. The EU's ENISA and UK's NCSC both maintain parallel catalogs that shadow the KEV; any acceleration of CISA's process will cascade globally, tightening the international patch cycle across allied critical infrastructure.

## [tech law] UK Ofcom secures child-safety commitments from Meta, Snapchat, and Roblox under Online Safety Act; declares YouTube and TikTok still not safe enough

**Source:** https://therecord.media/tech-giants-promise-ofcom-regulator-changes-child-access

**Lead:** UK regulator Ofcom required six major platforms to implement child-protection changes by April 2026 under the Online Safety Act, securing commitments from Meta, Snapchat, and Roblox, while publicly declaring YouTube and TikTok "still not safe enough" with 10-percent-of-global-revenue fines available for non-compliance.

**Context:** Ofcom, enforcing the UK Online Safety Act, required six platforms — Instagram, Facebook, Snapchat, Roblox, YouTube, and TikTok — to implement specific child safety design changes by end of April 2026. Meta committed to hiding teenagers' connection lists on Instagram by default and to deploying AI systems to detect "likely sexualized conversations" in direct messages before they occur. Snapchat agreed to restrict adult-child contact and eliminate features encouraging connections with unknown users. Roblox committed to allowing parents to disable direct messaging for accounts belonging to users under 16. YouTube and TikTok declined to make significant commitments; Ofcom responded by publicly naming them as platforms that are "still not safe enough" — a calculated naming-and-shaming step that precedes formal enforcement proceedings. The Online Safety Act grants Ofcom the power to impose fines of up to 10 percent of global annual turnover, which would amount to billions of dollars for Meta, TikTok, or YouTube.

**Why it matters:** YouTube and TikTok's refusal to commit to design changes, even under threat of 10-percent-of-global-revenue fines from a major English-speaking market, will become the test case for whether the UK's Online Safety Act has genuine enforcement teeth or is primarily aspirational. Ofcom's public naming approach — declaring specific platforms unsafe before issuing financial penalties — creates reputational and market pressure independent of the formal fine timeline, a model that regulators in Australia, Canada, and EU member states are watching closely for replication. Meta's acceptance of the AI-detection mandate for sexualized messaging, if implemented at scale, creates a benchmark of technical feasibility that regulators can cite against other platforms claiming such detection is impossible. For EU DSA enforcement, the UK approach is diverging deliberately: Brussels focuses on algorithmic transparency and content moderation systems while London targets specific product design features that enable grooming, generating two distinct but complementary regulatory frameworks that platforms must satisfy simultaneously. The consultation on age-based access restrictions signals a potential move toward mandatory age verification — legislation that would directly challenge the technical and privacy architecture of every major social platform operating in the UK.

## [tech law] Two Americans plead guilty to providing call-routing infrastructure to India-based tech-support fraud centers that targeted elderly Americans

**Source:** https://therecord.media/two-americans-plead-guilty-india-call-center-scams

**Lead:** Adam Young and Harrison Gevirtz, operators of C.A. Cloud Attribution, pleaded guilty to supplying phone numbers and call-routing services to India-based tech-support fraud centers from 2016 to 2022, continuing even after receiving law-enforcement complaints in 2017, and face up to three years in prison at sentencing on June 16.

**Context:** Adam Young, 42, and Harrison Gevirtz, 33, co-operators of C.A. Cloud Attribution, pleaded guilty to misprision of a felony for providing telephone numbers, call routing, and call-forwarding infrastructure to India-based tech-support scam operations from 2016 to 2022. The scam centers targeted elderly and disabled Americans with fake malware warnings, routing them to call centers in India where fraudsters pressured victims into paying hundreds of dollars for worthless technical support services; operators then exploited remote device access to steal additional financial information. Young and Gevirtz continued supplying services even after receiving law-enforcement complaints about the scam operations in 2017 and took active steps to help fraudsters evade detection. DOJ prosecutors emphasized that the pair's continued service with actual knowledge of the fraud distinguishes this from ordinary negligent provision of telecommunications infrastructure. The defendants face up to three years in prison at sentencing on June 16.

**Why it matters:** This prosecution is part of a deliberate DOJ strategy to hold US-based "infrastructure enablers" accountable for the full downstream harm of transnational fraud schemes — a significant evolution beyond pursuing only the foreign call-center operators themselves, who are often beyond US extradition reach. Tech-support fraud targeting elderly Americans generates an estimated $1 billion in annual losses; prosecuting domestic choke points — phone number provisioners, payment processors, virtual private server providers — disrupts whole ecosystems rather than individual operatives who can be replaced within days. The guilty plea to "misprision of a felony" rather than conspiracy suggests prosecutors prioritized quick cooperation and information, with both defendants potentially serving as witnesses in future cross-border proceedings against Indian call-center operators. For VoIP providers and telecommunications infrastructure companies, the case establishes a binding standard: knowledge of ongoing fraud combined with continued service provision is now a federal criminal exposure, creating a strong incentive to cut off suspicious customers immediately rather than wait for formal law-enforcement demands. Watch for DOJ to use the cooperation obtained here to bring charges against at least one India-based call center principal before the June 16 sentencing date.
