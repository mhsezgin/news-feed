## [law] Supreme Court unanimously holds freight brokers face negligent-hiring suits under state tort law, resolving circuit split in favor of plaintiffs injured by motor carriers

**Link:** https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/05/court-rules-freight-brokers-can-face-negligent-hiring-suits-under-state-law/

**Lead:** The Supreme Court held 9-0 that the Federal Aviation Administration Authorization Act of 1994 does not preempt state negligent-hiring claims against freight brokers, exposing brokers to tort liability for knowingly selecting unsafe motor carriers and resolving a long-standing federal circuit split.

**Context:** In Montgomery v. Caribe Transport II, Shawn Montgomery lost his leg on December 7, 2017, when a tractor-trailer operated by Caribe Transport II — a carrier with a documented subpar federal safety rating — rear-ended his stopped vehicle on Interstate 70. C.H. Robinson Worldwide, one of the nation's largest freight brokerage firms, had arranged the shipment and selected Caribe. Montgomery sued C.H. Robinson for negligent hiring, alleging the broker knew or should have known that Caribe was an unsafe carrier. The threshold issue was whether the FAAAA — which generally prohibits states from enacting laws "related to a price, route, or service of any motor carrier" — preempted state negligent-hiring claims against brokers. The FAAAA contains a safety exception preserving state authority over "safety regulatory authority of a State with respect to motor vehicles," and the federal courts of appeals had split on whether broker negligent-hiring claims fit within it. Justice Amy Coney Barrett, writing for a unanimous Court, held that "with respect to motor vehicles" means the claim concerns motor vehicle safety — which a negligent-hiring suit plainly does — placing such claims squarely within the safety exception and beyond federal preemption. Justices Kavanaugh and Alito wrote separately to note the case was closer than the majority opinion suggested.

**Why it matters:** The ruling exposes the freight brokerage industry — which arranges well over $100 billion in truck shipments annually — to state negligent-hiring liability for the first time with no prospect of federal preemption shielding major brokers. C.H. Robinson's stock fell on news of the decision, and trade groups have already signaled they will pursue legislative correction through Congress by amending the FAAAA safety exception language. For personal injury plaintiffs, the ruling fundamentally expands the defendant pool in trucking accident cases: rather than suing only an undercapitalized small carrier, victims can now reach the typically well-capitalized broker who vetted and hired that carrier. The practical consequence will be renewed pressure on brokers to conduct rigorous carrier due diligence — checking FMCSA safety scores, insurance levels, and accident histories — creating a new industry compliance standard that plaintiff attorneys will use to define reasonable-broker conduct at trial. The Kavanaugh-Alito concurrence signals that brokers exercising ordinary care with reputable carriers should successfully defend these suits, meaning the ruling creates liability exposure calibrated to broker carelessness rather than strict liability — a distinction that will shape the first wave of post-ruling settlements and jury instructions.

## [law] EU Advocate General's opinion conditionally validates Italy's offshore Albania asylum processing scheme, providing legal roadmap for EU externalisation policy

**Link:** https://verfassungsblog.de/litigating-externalisation-under-the-italy-albania-protocol/

**Lead:** Advocate General Emiliou concluded at the CJEU that Italy's scheme transferring asylum seekers to Italian-operated detention centers in Albania is broadly compatible with EU law, provided detainees retain access to individual assessments and legal remedies — a conditional green light with major implications for European asylum externalisation.

**Context:** The Italy-Albania Protocol, signed in November 2023 and operational since late 2024, allows Italy to transfer migrants intercepted in the Mediterranean to two Italian-run detention centers in Albania — at Gjader and Shëngjin — where Italian officials conduct asylum and return proceedings under Italian jurisdiction on Albanian soil. Italian courts repeatedly blocked transfers in 2024 and early 2025, citing a CJEU August 2025 ruling that tightened limits on "safe country of origin" designations and required individual assessments for fast-track return procedures. The Sedrata case (C-414/25) is the first of three pending CJEU proceedings directly testing the Protocol's compatibility with the EU Procedures Directive and the Charter of Fundamental Rights. On April 23, 2026, Advocate General Emiliou delivered his Opinion, concluding that offshore processing in Albania is lawful under EU law provided that detainees have access to legal remedies, receive individual rather than categorical assessments, and have their claims adjudicated under EU substantive standards even though they are physically outside EU territory. The AG also found that Italy's use of Albanian facilities as border or transit zones — treating them as functionally equivalent to processing at Italy's own physical borders — is permissible as long as the procedural safeguards are operationally maintained.

**Why it matters:** The Advocate General's opinion is non-binding but is followed by the Grand Chamber in the overwhelming majority of cases, making a favorable CJEU judgment highly probable — an outcome that would provide legal cover for analogous schemes across the EU and UK. Denmark's proposed third-country processing model and the UK's Rwanda scheme (invalidated by the UK Supreme Court in 2024) both depend on legal frameworks similar to the Italy-Albania Protocol, meaning a CJEU validation would revive those programs under EU law's equivalent of the AG's safeguard framework. The European Commission's external processing pilot under the 2024 Migration Pact has been developed with the CJEU proceedings in mind: a favorable ruling gives the Commission authority to expand externalisation arrangements to Morocco, Tunisia, and other partner countries without facing immediate judicial invalidation. The AG's safeguard conditions — individual assessments, judicial review, EU-standard adjudication — will set the minimum legal floor that any externalisation arrangement must meet, and migration advocates have already warned that these safeguards are procedurally robust on paper but logistically hollow in practice given language barriers, limited counsel access, and the physical isolation of offshore centers. Italian courts must now stand down from issuing blocking orders, pending the final CJEU judgment expected in late 2026.
