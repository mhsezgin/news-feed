## 1. What the EU AI Omnibus Deal Changes for the AI Act and What Lies Ahead

**Link:** https://www.techpolicy.press/what-the-eu-ai-omnibus-deal-changes-for-the-ai-act-and-what-lies-ahead/

**Lead:** The EU Council and Parliament reached a provisional agreement on May 7 to amend the AI Act, delaying high-risk system compliance deadlines by over a year while adding a new ban on AI nudifier tools and broadening permissions to use sensitive personal data for bias detection.

**Context:** The Digital AI Omnibus package, finalized in early-morning negotiations in Brussels on May 7, 2026, extends the compliance deadline for standalone high-risk AI systems under Annex III — covering biometrics, law enforcement, border control, and critical infrastructure — from August 2, 2026 to December 2, 2027, a 16-month extension. AI systems embedded in regulated products such as medical devices, machinery, and toys receive an even longer runway until August 2, 2028. The deal reflects sustained lobbying by European industry groups backed by German Chancellor Friedrich Merz, who personally pressed for broader industrial exemptions and secured France and Italy's backing; Parliament ultimately limited exemptions to the machinery sector. Parliament's right wing prioritized industry relief; the left wing traded concessions to secure the new nudifier ban, which prohibits AI tools generating realistic non-consensual nude images and takes effect December 2026 — directly responding to millions of deepfake cases linked to Grok. Transparency and AI-generated content watermarking obligations under Article 50 retain the August 2 deadline, though existing deployed systems receive grace periods. SME privileges were extended to small mid-cap companies, and a new EU-level regulatory sandbox was established to allow controlled testing of high-risk AI.

**Why it matters:** The 16-month extension for high-risk AI removes the most immediate compliance pressure from companies developing AI for healthcare, law enforcement, and critical infrastructure — the sectors where certification requirements generated the greatest industry pushback, with compliance cost estimates running to tens of millions of euros per system. The delay is a direct concession to competitiveness arguments that the original August 2026 deadline would disadvantage European companies against US and Chinese competitors operating under lighter AI regulation. The nudifier ban is the deal's meaningful civil society win: placing an affirmative prohibition directly in the AI Act rather than relying solely on the Digital Services Act and criminal law creates a new EU-level enforcement vector against platforms hosting or generating such content. The Data Omnibus — the companion regulation addressing GDPR simplification — remains outstanding and poses greater risks, as it would broaden personal data processing permissions for AI training. The provisional agreement must receive formal approval from EU member states and the European Parliament, with Ireland taking over the Council presidency on June 30, timing that will determine how quickly formal adoption proceeds — and whether the August 2 transparency deadline triggers first compliance actions before high-risk rules take effect.

**Context:** DOGE staffers Justin Fox and Nate Cavanaugh, described as having minimal relevant experience, reviewed National Endowment for the Humanities grants by copy-pasting summaries into ChatGPT and asking 'Does the following relate at all to DEI?' Judge Colleen McMahon of the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York found the process void on three independent grounds. First, DOGE lacked statutory authority: Congress vested termination power in the NEH Chairperson alone, and the staffers had usurped decision-making authority, drafting termination letters under the Chair's name and overriding his recommendations. Second, the process was arbitrary and capricious under the Administrative Procedure Act: Fox testified he never defined 'DEI' for the chatbot, and the resulting terminations flagged a study of ancient Jewish texts as DEI-related and rejected a human rights study documenting Uyghur persecution in China on the same grounds. Third, the terminations constituted viewpoint discrimination under the First Amendment: the government admitted it was eliminating grants promoting a disfavored ideology, and the court stated plainly that 'DEI is, of course, a viewpoint.' The case was brought by the American Council of Learned Societies.

**Why it matters:** The ruling articulates what may be the most precise judicial statement on DOGE's structural constitutional limits: executive-branch advisory bodies cannot exercise decision-making authority Congress never delegated to them, and AI-tool outputs cannot substitute for the reasoned analysis the APA requires. The First Amendment holding is significant because it applies to grant administration — an area courts sometimes treat as executive discretion — establishing that the government cannot condition NEH grants on viewpoint conformity. The arbitrary-and-capricious analysis creates a reproducible legal template: any federal agency whose decisions were influenced by DOGE staffers lacking statutory authority faces identical APA challenges, and the ChatGPT methodology makes those challenges especially easy to document through agency records requests. DOGE has terminated billions of dollars in grants across multiple agencies using similar review processes, and this ruling directly raises the viability of parallel suits challenging those terminations nationwide. The decision also opens a broader debate about government use of AI tools in regulatory and enforcement contexts — whether AI-assisted screening can ever satisfy the APA's requirement for reasoned decision-making, and what level of documentation such processes require to withstand judicial review.

**Lead:** SCOTUSblog's analysis of Louisiana v. Callais establishes that the Supreme Court's April 29 ruling subjects race-conscious redistricting to strict scrutiny even when undertaken to comply with Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act — effectively reversing the 1982 congressional amendment that enabled disparate-impact liability and ending the legal framework that generated majority-minority districts for four decades.

**Context:** Congress amended Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act in 1982 specifically to permit liability based on racially disparate outcomes — a redistricting map could violate the law if it diluted minority voting power as a result, without any showing of intentional discrimination. That framework generated the majority-minority districts producing substantial gains in Black and Latino congressional representation after each census cycle from 1990 through 2020. In Louisiana v. Callais, the Supreme Court held that even race-conscious redistricting undertaken to comply with Section 2 triggers strict scrutiny — the most demanding constitutional standard, under which racial classifications in redistricting almost always fail. Justice Samuel Alito wrote the majority. Justice Elena Kagan's dissent warned the ruling 'renders Section 2 all but a dead letter' because the new standard functionally requires proof of intentional racial discrimination, which Kagan characterized as 'almost impossible' to establish from legislative records. The practical effect is that states can deploy partisan gerrymandering — held immune from federal constitutional challenge in Rucho v. Common Cause (2019) — to achieve the same minority vote dilution Section 2 previously prohibited, since partisan and racial geography overlap substantially in most states.

**Why it matters:** Callais completes a three-decision doctrinal arc stripping the Voting Rights Act of its principal enforcement mechanisms: Shelby County v. Holder (2013) eliminated preclearance requirements; Rucho v. Common Cause (2019) immunized partisan gerrymandering from federal challenge; Callais now removes disparate-impact Section 2 claims. The combined result is that no effective federal judicial remedy currently exists for systematic minority vote dilution through redistricting. The immediate downstream effect is visible in Alabama, which filed an emergency Supreme Court application invoking Callais to escape a district-court remedial order requiring two majority-Black districts — forcing the justices to define Callais's operational scope within weeks. Kagan's framing that Section 2 is now a 'dead letter' will be tested beyond redistricting: plaintiffs challenging voter ID, polling place closures, and registration restrictions under disparate-impact theories must assess whether Callais's logic migrates to those contexts. The only remedy would be congressional rewriting of Section 2, but the current political composition of Congress makes such legislation unlikely.
