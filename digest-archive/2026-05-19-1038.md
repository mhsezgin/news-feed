## [ai] Anthropic acquires Stainless, the company that has built every official Anthropic SDK since the API launched

**Link:** https://www.anthropic.com/news/anthropic-acquires-stainless

**Lead:** Anthropic has acquired Stainless, the SDK-generation company that automatically built every official Anthropic API SDK since launch, bringing the team in-house to deepen Claude agent connectivity across programming languages.

**Context:** Stainless, founded in 2022, specializes in automatically generating software development kits, command-line tools, and MCP (Model Context Protocol) servers from API specifications, supporting TypeScript, Python, Go, Java, and Kotlin. The company has powered Anthropic's official SDKs from the very beginning of the Claude API — meaning the acquisition brings an existing critical infrastructure partner in-house rather than integrating an external technology. Hundreds of additional companies also rely on Stainless to generate production SDKs and API connectors for their own products. Katelyn Lesse, Anthropic's Head of Platform Engineering, framed the strategic logic directly: "Agents are only as useful as what they can connect to." Alex Rattray, Stainless's founder and CEO, has argued that SDKs deserve the same engineering care as the APIs they wrap — a philosophy aligned with Anthropic's push to make Claude's agent capabilities more reliable. Financial terms were not disclosed.

**Why it matters:** For developers building on Claude's API, the acquisition signals that Anthropic is treating its SDK and MCP infrastructure as a core platform investment rather than a developer-relations afterthought — which typically means faster updates, better cross-language parity, and tighter integration between new Claude capabilities and the tooling developers use to connect to external data. The move also positions Anthropic to accelerate adoption of MCP, the open agent-connectivity protocol it introduced in late 2024; with Stainless's generator in-house, Anthropic can ship MCP server connectors for third-party APIs far faster than it could by contracting out. For the broader developer tooling market, the acquisition raises the question of whether Stainless's third-party SDK generation business — currently serving hundreds of non-Anthropic companies — will continue unchanged or be gradually redirected toward Anthropic-adjacent priorities. The tighter SDK-to-model integration Anthropic is pursuing also has competitive implications: a developer whose tools are deeply integrated with Claude's latest capabilities faces higher switching costs when choosing between frontier model providers, and Anthropic is clearly betting that reducing integration friction is a durable moat.

## [law] Hungary after Orbán: scholars debate constitutional repair without repeating his unilateral rewriting playbook

**Link:** https://verfassungsblog.de/hungary-constitutional-repair/

**Lead:** Legal scholars are debating how Hungary's new TISZA-led government should use its historic constitutional supermajority to repair 16 years of democratic backsliding without mimicking the Orbán playbook of unilateral constitutional rewriting.

**Context:** On April 12, 2026, Péter Magyar's TISZA party won 141 seats on nearly 53 percent of the vote in Hungary's parliamentary election, securing the two-thirds supermajority threshold that Orbán himself embedded in the 2011 Fundamental Law as a requirement for constitutional amendment — a provision designed to entrench Fidesz power that now enables its undoing. The victory ended 16 years of Orbán rule and what scholars classify as a "hybrid regime": formally democratic elections combined with systematic capture of courts, media regulators, electoral administration, and public institutions. Verfassungsblog contributors identify four constitutional repair priorities: eliminating obstacles to democratic decision-making; restoring independence of courts and supervisory institutions; removing unjustifiable fundamental rights limitations; and excising ideological content Fidesz wrote into the constitution, including provisions on gender, family structure, and Christian national heritage. Electoral reform is identified as both urgent and transformative, since Orbán's majoritarian system amplified the ruling party's seat share far beyond its actual vote share across more than a decade of elections. Scholars explicitly warn that unilaterally adopting a new constitution — even for democratic purposes — would replicate Orbán's own legitimacy-bypassing move from 2010 and make the resulting document a target for future right-wing challenge.

**Why it matters:** How TISZA handles its supermajority will be watched across the EU as the most important test of post-authoritarian democratic restoration in over a decade, with direct implications for the EU's capacity to enforce its own constitutional values through Article 7 proceedings against member states. Hungary's return to full EU rule-of-law compliance would unlock approximately €20 billion in cohesion funds frozen since 2022, giving TISZA a powerful material incentive to move quickly on judicial independence reforms that Brussels has explicitly demanded. The Polish precedent — PiS defeated in late 2023, followed by a Tusk government with similar constitutional-majority ambitions — provides both a road map and a caution: Polish courts and institutions have resisted the reform agenda on procedural grounds, and international bodies have split on whether the restoration measures themselves respect legal process. For the EU, Hungary's constitutional transition tests whether European law can serve as a backstop for democratic recovery — or whether the constitutional capture Orbán achieved is in practice irreversible without a willing political successor with exceptional electoral resources. Scholars note that even with a supermajority, TISZA will face constitutional courts packed with Fidesz-era appointees who may contest reform legislation before it takes effect.

## [law] D.C. federal court issues second order blocking ICE nationwide warrantless arrests, finding agency rewrote its own policy to evade the first injunction

**Link:** https://www.techdirt.com/2026/05/18/dc-court-orders-ice-to-stop-engaging-in-warrantless-arrests/

**Lead:** U.S. District Judge Beryl Howell issued a second nationwide order blocking ICE warrantless civil immigration arrests after finding the agency had rewritten its internal "escape risk" definition to evade her earlier injunction, allowing arrests at court check-ins and naturalization appointments.

**Context:** Judge Howell of the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia issued the order after determining that ICE had manipulated its own policy guidance in response to her earlier ruling, which found warrantless civil immigration arrests violated Fourth Amendment standards. The core manipulation: ICE rewrote its definition of "escape risk" — the traditional exception that triggered warrant-requirement workarounds — by dropping a key qualifying phrase, effectively narrowing the standard to whether an individual "is likely to remain at the scene of the encounter." Under this shrunken definition, ICE proceeded to arrest individuals attending court-ordered check-ins and pursuing naturalization applications — contexts where the arrestees were voluntarily cooperating with legal processes and self-evidently not attempting to flee. Judge Howell found ICE's reinterpretation constituted "a departure from statutory text and historical understanding" and that the agency was using semantic manipulation to evade judicial oversight rather than complying with the order's substance. She granted expanded discovery to build a fuller factual record, leaving a potential permanent injunction available as a subsequent remedy.

**Why it matters:** The fact that ICE responded to an injunction by rewriting internal policy definitions — rather than complying with the ruling's substance — will likely harden judicial supervision across immigration enforcement: future courts may draft injunctions with more precise definitional anchors, or appoint compliance monitors to review whether policy rewrites constitute covert noncompliance. A permanent injunction after full discovery would impose a nationwide constraint on the administration's deportation pipeline at its earliest enforcement stage — directly limiting the mass-arrest operations that have been central to the Trump administration's enforcement posture since January 2025. The broader pattern of agencies redefining internal terms to evade judicial orders is being litigated simultaneously across multiple enforcement contexts, and the D.C. Circuit's eventual ruling on whether such maneuvers constitute contempt will set precedent affecting executive-branch compliance with court orders government-wide. For immigration lawyers, the expanded discovery phase creates an opportunity to build a detailed factual record of ICE's nationwide operations — information that has historically been opaque and that could support future litigation in other circuits. The ruling also has a constitutional dimension: courts finding that an agency is systematically misrepresenting its own policy to circumvent judicial review may invoke inherent contempt powers, which are available independently of statutory enforcement mechanisms.

## [cybersecurity] CISA contractor left AWS GovCloud admin keys, plaintext passwords, and SSH keys in a public GitHub repo — experts call it one of the worst government credential leaks on record

**Link:** https://krebsonsecurity.com/2026/05/cisa-admin-leaked-aws-govcloud-keys-on-github/

**Lead:** A Nightwing contractor working for CISA maintained a public GitHub repository containing high-privilege AWS GovCloud administrative credentials, plaintext passwords for dozens of internal CISA systems, and SSH keys — credentials that security experts described as among the most egregious government data exposures in recent history.

**Context:** The repository, named "Private-CISA" and maintained by a Nightwing contractor (Nightwing is a Virginia-based government IT services firm), contained a file titled "importantAWStokens" with credentials to three AWS GovCloud servers at high privilege level. AWS GovCloud is Amazon's cloud region restricted to U.S. government use, and administrative-level keys provide root-equivalent access to those servers. Beyond the cloud tokens, the repository included plaintext passwords in CSV files for dozens of CISA internal systems, SSH keys, cloud access tokens, and credentials to CISA's internal "artifactory" — the code package repository used to build and deploy CISA-developed software. Security researchers identified the artifactory access as particularly dangerous: "Backdoor some software packages, and every time they build something new, they deploy your backdoor left and right" — meaning a threat actor with that access could inject persistent malicious code into CISA's own security tools. CISA issued a statement asserting "there is no indication that any sensitive data was compromised," while Nightwing declined to comment, deferring to CISA.

**Why it matters:** The breach hits the specific agency responsible for defending U.S. government cybersecurity and coordinating incident response — the entity that advises all other federal agencies on exactly the credential management practices that this incident violated, making the credibility damage as significant as the technical risk. If the AWS GovCloud keys or artifactory credentials were accessed before discovery, the potential exposure is a software supply-chain attack affecting any CISA-built tool deployed across the federal government — a scenario comparable to the SolarWinds attack of 2020, but originating from CISA's own build pipeline. The incident illustrates a persistent institutional failure: privileged credentials routinely end up in version control despite years of public awareness and tooling investment, because developers face minimal accountability for the practice and automated secrets scanning remains inconsistently deployed even in high-security environments. CISA's own Secure by Design initiative and Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog both address credential hygiene — the fact that the agency's own contractor violated these principles on a public repository creates a serious internal-consistency problem for CISA's credibility as a cybersecurity standard-setter. Congressional oversight committees will likely demand answers about Nightwing's contractor vetting, CISA's code repository auditing practices, and whether any foreign intelligence services accessed the repository before it was taken down.

## [cybersecurity] INTERPOL Operation Ramz: 201 arrested across 13 MENA countries, phishing-as-a-service platform dismantled, 3,867 victims identified

**Link:** https://therecord.media/more-than-200-arrested-interpol-middle-east-scams

**Lead:** INTERPOL's Operation Ramz, coordinated across 13 North African and Middle Eastern countries from October to February, resulted in 201 arrests, identification of 3,867 victims, seizure of 53 servers, and the dismantling of an Algerian phishing-as-a-service platform.

**Context:** Operation Ramz was backed by Qatar and the European Union, with cybersecurity firm Team Cymru providing technical support, and coordinated participating agencies in Algeria, Bahrain, Egypt, Iraq, Jordan, Lebanon, Libya, Morocco, Oman, Palestine, Qatar, Tunisia, and the UAE. Raids uncovered financial fraud operations in which victims were directed to deposit money into fake investment and trading platforms that were subsequently shut down — a pattern known as "pig butchering" in its longer-term variants. Law enforcement seized 53 servers, recovered hard drives containing banking data and phishing software, and dismantled a phishing-as-a-service platform traced to Algeria, which had provided turnkey fraud infrastructure to downstream criminal actors who lacked the technical capacity to build their own tools. A particularly significant finding: in Jordan, investigators discovered that 15 individuals running the scam operations were themselves victims of human trafficking — recruited under false promises of legitimate employment and then coerced into operating fraud networks. In total, 201 people were arrested and an additional 382 suspects were identified but not immediately apprehended, with 3,867 victims formally identified across the 13 participating countries.

**Why it matters:** The dismantling of the Algerian phishing-as-a-service platform is the most operationally significant element: unlike individual fraud operations, phishing-as-a-service infrastructure enables dozens or hundreds of separate criminal groups to run sophisticated campaigns without technical skill, and removing the underlying platform disrupts downstream fraud activity far beyond the specific operation targeted. The human-trafficking-to-cybercrime pipeline revealed in Jordan is increasingly a defining structural feature of large-scale financial fraud networks — particularly those operating from Southeast Asia, the Middle East, and North Africa — and substantially complicates prosecution by blurring the line between perpetrator and victim in ways that existing legal frameworks handle poorly. EU financial backing for Operation Ramz reflects a strategy of projecting the EU Digital Services Act's platform enforcement framework into the MENA region through cooperative law enforcement, though the practical effect of DSA obligations on non-EU platforms facilitating fraud in these markets remains legally contested. For banks and payment processors operating in the MENA region, the 3,867 victim identifications create both legal notification obligations and an opportunity for coordinated account recovery — though cross-border victim notification across 13 jurisdictions with varying data protection regimes is operationally complex. The 382 additional identified suspects who were not arrested represent an intelligence dividend: INTERPOL will share those identities with member agencies for separate prosecution, potentially extending the operational impact of the raid well beyond the 201 direct arrests.

## [law] SCOTUS analysis: Black and Harlan — progressive textualist and conservative rights-expansionist — inverted their labels in Griswold and shaped every unenumerated-rights case since

**Link:** https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/05/strange-judicial-bedfellows-/

**Lead:** A new SCOTUSblog essay traces how Hugo Black and John Marshall Harlan II — ideological opposites and close personal friends — inverted conventional political labels in Griswold v. Connecticut (1965), with "progressive" Black dissenting against contraception rights on textualist grounds while "conservative" Harlan voted to protect them through substantive due process.

**Context:** The essay focuses on Griswold v. Connecticut (1965), which struck down the state's ban on contraceptive use by married couples. Hugo Black, historically categorized as a progressive justice and Franklin Roosevelt appointee, dissented on grounds that the Constitution contains no explicit right to privacy — a position he derived from his strict commitment to constitutional text, arguing courts should not invent rights not written in the document, a stance we would today call originalism. John Marshall Harlan II, labeled a conservative Eisenhower appointee, voted to strike the Connecticut law by arguing that the due process clause protects rights "implicit in the concept of ordered liberty" even when unwritten — the methodology that later produced Roe v. Wade. The two were paradoxically close personal friends: Harlan kept a portrait of Black's namesake in his office, and Black described Harlan as one of the few people who had convinced him "there is such a thing as a good Republican." The essay draws a direct line from this 1965 philosophical split to Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization (2022), in which the Court's majority used Black-style textualism to overrule Roe, vindicating Black's Griswold dissent nearly 60 years later.

**Why it matters:** The essay arrives as the current Supreme Court continues to produce unexpected cross-ideological coalitions — Barrett and Sotomayor aligning on administrative law questions, Thomas joining liberal justices on Fourth Amendment cases — a pattern that becomes more legible when understood through the lens of methodological commitment rather than partisan affiliation. The practical implication for legal practitioners is tactical: framing arguments in textualist terms has strategic value with the current Court's majority regardless of one's normative position, because the methodology tends to control outcomes even when the substantive result is uncertain. For constitutional scholars, the Black-Harlan inversion illustrates the persistent instability of the "conservative/progressive" taxonomy as applied to constitutional adjudication: a justice's methodological commitments — textualism, living constitutionalism, structuralism, moral pragmatism — predict outcomes far better than their appointing president's party. The Dobbs connection gives the analysis immediate contemporary weight: the majority opinion's reasoning tracked Black's Griswold dissent closely, suggesting that Black's jurisprudence, dismissed as extreme in 1965, ultimately set the doctrinal template for the largest constitutional rollback in the Court's modern era. The essay also serves as a useful corrective for commentary that treats the current Court's unenumerated-rights skepticism as unprecedented — it has a lineage running through Black's strict textualism, with the Harlan tradition of substantive due process now in retreat but not eliminated, as Justice Kavanaugh's cautious concurrences in post-Dobbs cases suggest.
