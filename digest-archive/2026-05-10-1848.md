## 1. EU Finds Meta in Breach of Digital Services Act Over Failure to Protect Children Under 13

**Source:** [Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/next/2026/04/29/eu-finds-meta-in-breach-of-digital-rules-over-children-on-instagram-and-facebook) | **Topic:** tech_law

**Lead:** The European Commission preliminarily found Meta's Instagram and Facebook in breach of the Digital Services Act for failing to prevent children under 13 from accessing the platforms, potentially exposing the company to fines of up to 6 percent of its global annual turnover.

**Context:** The Commission announced its preliminary findings on April 29, 2026, concluding Meta had "disregarded readily available scientific evidence" about children's vulnerability to social media harms. Despite Meta's terms of service setting the minimum age at 13, approximately 10 to 12 percent of EU children under 13 are using Instagram or Facebook—roughly one in ten—contradicting the company's own internal assessments. The core failure is structural: children can bypass Meta's age restrictions by entering a false birth date during sign-up, with no verification step, while the reporting mechanism for removing underage accounts is described by the Commission as cumbersome and often ineffective. Meta disputed the findings, stating that "Instagram and Facebook are intended for people aged 13 and older and we have measures in place to detect and remove accounts from anyone under that age." Under DSA rules, Meta now has the right to examine the investigation file, submit a written reply, and propose corrective measures before any formal non-compliance decision is issued.

**Why it matters:** A confirmed non-compliance decision under the DSA would expose Meta to fines of up to 6 percent of its total worldwide annual turnover—which at Meta's current revenue scale could reach several billion euros, ranking among the largest digital-regulation penalties in EU history. The DSA proceeding is structurally distinct from GDPR child-protection cases: the obligation under Article 34 is proactive systemic-risk mitigation, not simply age-gating, meaning Meta must design systems that functionally prevent under-13 access rather than merely prohibit it in terms of service. This is the Commission's first child-protection enforcement action under the DSA, and the outcome will signal how aggressively the EU pursues platform accountability for minor safety across all seven designated gatekeepers. The US Senate simultaneously advanced child-online-safety legislation in May 2026, creating parallel regulatory pressure from two major jurisdictions. Meta's corrective-measures response will likely determine whether the Commission issues a formal penalty or accepts voluntary undertakings—a choice that will define the DSA's enforcement character for every major case that follows.

## 4. The Nine Lives of Employment Division v. Smith

**Source:** [SCOTUSblog](https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/05/the-nine-lives-of-employment-division-v-smith-/) | **Topic:** law

**Lead:** A new SCOTUSblog analysis traces how the Supreme Court has spent thirty-five years systematically narrowing Employment Division v. Smith without overruling it—hollowing out the 1990 free-exercise precedent through successive rulings that have left it technically intact but practically enfeebled.

**Context:** Employment Division v. Smith (1990), written by Justice Antonin Scalia, held that the First Amendment's Free Exercise Clause provides no exemption from neutral, generally applicable laws—even when those laws substantially burden religious practice—and upheld Oregon's uniform drug prohibition as applied to peyote used in Native American Church ceremonies. Congress responded with the Religious Freedom Restoration Act of 1993 (RFRA), restoring a compelling-interest test for federal laws, but the Court in 1997 limited RFRA to federal rather than state government actions. Rather than overruling Smith, the Court has pursued doctrinal erosion: Tandon v. Newsom (2021) held that strict scrutiny applies whenever a law treats "any comparable secular activity" more favorably than religious exercise, and Fulton v. City of Philadelphia (2021) established that any law containing any exception at all cannot qualify as "generally applicable." Three justices—Alito, Thomas, and Gorsuch—have publicly called Smith "regrettable" and urged overruling; Justices Barrett and Kavanaugh have expressed skepticism about the precedent but acknowledged that overruling requires an agreed-upon replacement framework that a five-justice majority has not yet coalesced around.

**Why it matters:** The "nine lives" framing captures the Court's institutional ambivalence: a near-majority appears to believe Smith was wrongly decided, but the inability to agree on a replacement standard has preserved a precedent being steadily drained of content through qualification. The practical consequence for state and local governments is substantial uncertainty: under Tandon and Fulton, any regulatory scheme containing any exception or accommodation—however minor or unrelated to religion—may now face strict scrutiny from religious claimants, chilling otherwise lawful regulation across healthcare, licensing, and non-discrimination law. Democratic proposals to narrow RFRA—specifically by excluding federal healthcare and LGBTQ non-discrimination obligations from its protection—could force the Court's hand by presenting cases where RFRA itself is the battleground rather than a state law. The 21 states with their own RFRA analogs have independent trajectories, meaning even a definitive Supreme Court ruling on federal doctrine would leave state-level religious exemption law evolving separately. The next case presenting a genuinely neutral and universally applicable law with no exceptions whatsoever would force a direct confrontation with Smith that the Court has artfully avoided for three decades.

## 6. DMA at Two: EU's Big Tech Law Produced Real Market Changes—but Compliance Theater Blunts Its Impact

**Source:** [Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/my-europe/2026/05/07/dma-how-the-eus-big-tech-law-changed-citizens-lives) | **Topic:** tech_law

**Lead:** Two years after the EU Digital Markets Act entered into force, analysis finds the regulation has produced tangible gains—a 500 million euro Apple fine, cross-platform messaging rights, measurable browser competition—but up to 66 percent of frequent users report that gatekeeper compliance increases friction rather than delivering the promised consumer benefits.

**Context:** The Digital Markets Act took effect in May 2023, designating seven platforms as "gatekeepers"—Apple, Google, Meta, Amazon, ByteDance, and two others—and imposing interoperability, data-sharing, and anti-steering obligations designed to open their ecosystems to competition. Concrete changes for EU consumers include the ability to set alternative browsers and payment apps as iPhone defaults, enforceable cross-platform messaging (a Latvian startup called BirdyChat now allows users to message WhatsApp contacts from outside Meta's ecosystem), and choice screens that have measurably shifted browser market share toward privacy-focused alternatives like Firefox. The Commission fined Apple 500 million euros in April 2025 for anti-steering violations that blocked App Store developers from directing users to cheaper purchases outside the App Store, and fined Meta 200 million euros for its "consent-or-pay" advertising model—both fines have been appealed. The Commission's April 2026 first formal DMA review declared the law "fit for purpose" while acknowledging a "ping-pong" enforcement dynamic: gatekeepers submit changes, the Commission requests improvements, and the cycle repeats without conclusive resolution. The Commission is now investigating Meta's integration of AI features into WhatsApp without a user opt-out, testing whether the DMA's systemic-risk provisions can reach AI-embedded features that post-date the regulation's drafting.

**Why it matters:** The DMA's two-year record reveals the gap between structural obligation and behavioral change: Apple and Meta have technically complied with most formal requirements while engineering compliance to increase friction and discourage users from exercising the rights the law creates—a pattern up to 66 percent of frequent users report experiencing as longer searches and less relevant results, and critics describe as "compliance theater." The 500 million euro Apple fine and 200 million euro Meta fine are significant but represent small fractions of both companies' annual revenues; credible deterrence requires the Commission to demonstrate willingness to escalate toward the DMA's ceiling of 10 percent of global annual turnover, a threshold that has not yet been invoked. The AI integration investigations are the most consequential test of the DMA's scope since enactment: if embedding AI features constitutes a new market-access barrier under Article 34 systemic-risk obligations, the regulation's reach will extend far beyond what the 2023 text explicitly anticipated and could require legislative clarification. The "fit for purpose" review signals no legislative revisions are imminent, meaning enforcement-through-fines remains the primary lever for at least two more years, making the size and sequencing of upcoming penalty decisions the decisive variable for the DMA's deterrence effect. Transatlantic tensions complicate enforcement: the Trump administration has signaled it views DMA actions targeting American companies as discriminatory trade policy, creating diplomatic pressure the Commission must navigate alongside its legal proceedings.
