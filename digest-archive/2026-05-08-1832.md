## 1. DOGE used ChatGPT in a way that was both dumb and illegal, judge rules
**Source:** theverge.com | **Topic:** tech_law
**Link:** https://www.theverge.com/policy/927071/doge-chatgpt-grants-canceled

**Lead:** A federal judge ruled that DOGE violated the First and Fifth Amendments by using ChatGPT to screen over $100 million in National Endowment for the Humanities grants for "DEI" content, calling it a textbook case of unconstitutional viewpoint discrimination and ordering rescission of termination letters to grantees.

**Context:** U.S. District Judge Colleen McMahon issued a blistering ruling on May 7, 2026, finding that the Department of Government Efficiency had no lawful authority to terminate more than $100 million in congressionally appropriated National Endowment for the Humanities grants. DOGE officials fed grant descriptions into ChatGPT with a binary classification prompt: "Does the following relate at all to DEI? Respond factually in less than 120 characters. Begin with 'Yes.' or 'No.' followed by a brief explanation." Grants where ChatGPT answered "Yes" were placed on a mass termination list. The government argued that ChatGPT — not federal officials — had determined what constituted DEI, and that this automated delegation insulated the process from constitutional challenge. McMahon rejected the argument outright, finding that outsourcing viewpoint-based classification to an AI tool does not strip the government of responsibility for the First Amendment violation the tool produced. She characterized the case as "a textbook example of unconstitutional viewpoint discrimination" and ordered the government to rescind termination letters, though she declined to require immediate payment of grant funds pending appeal. The ruling is among the first federal judicial opinions to address AI-assisted administrative decision-making at scale.

**Why it matters:** The decision establishes that an agency cannot launder unconstitutional viewpoint discrimination through an LLM prompt and then claim the algorithm, not the government, made the call — a principle that will be cited against any future AI-assisted regulatory screening that applies content-based criteria to protected expression. The ruling also implicates the Appropriations Clause: Congress allocated these NEH funds and DOGE possessed no delegated authority to terminate them, anchoring a broader challenge to executive impoundment of legislatively appropriated spending that tracks concurrent litigation challenging DOGE terminations at USAID, NIH, and the Department of Education. The administration has signaled it will appeal, meaning the circuit court's ruling will become the controlling precedent for whether AI-assisted administrative decisions face First Amendment review under the same standards as human decisions — a question with enormous downstream implications for algorithmic government action across regulatory agencies. Hundreds of humanities scholars, nonprofits, and cultural institutions whose grants were canceled face continuing uncertainty, because McMahon's order to rescind termination letters does not compel immediate reinstatement of funding while the government litigates the appeal, leaving them in legal limbo for potentially years.

**Source:** theverge.com | **Topic:** tech_law
**Link:** https://www.theverge.com/policy/927002/abc-disney-fcc-first-amendment-the-view

**Lead:** Disney's ABC filed a letter authored by prominent Supreme Court litigator Paul Clement accusing the Trump-aligned FCC of unconstitutionally chilling protected speech by launching an "equal time" investigation into The View and ordering accelerated renewal of all eight ABC station licenses — a process not due until 2028 — as part of a coordinated campaign linked to Trump's pressure to fire Jimmy Kimmel.

**Context:** The FCC under Trump-appointed chair Brendan Carr sent ABC a "letter of inquiry" examining whether The View violated the FCC's equal-time rules by featuring political candidates without offering equivalent airtime to opponents. The FCC had previously determined in 2002 that The View qualified as "a bona fide news interview program" exempt from equal-time obligations — an exemption the new inquiry appeared to reverse without explanation or rulemaking. Simultaneously, the FCC issued an extraordinary order requiring Disney to file license renewals for all eight of its ABC-owned television stations within 30 days — by May 28, 2026 — when those licenses were not due for renewal until 2028. ABC's response letter, signed by Paul Clement, argued that "uncertainty as to the scope of broadcast licensees' editorial discretion threatens to limit news coverage of political candidates and chill core First Amendment-protected speech for years and potentially decades to come." Senate Democrats including Markey, Cantwell, Schumer, and Luján filed a separate letter demanding the FCC stop what they characterized as "First Amendment attacks on Disney, ABC."

**Why it matters:** The dispute is far more consequential than a single network's station licenses because the FCC's authority to regulate broadcast content rests on the spectrum-scarcity rationale, which has been eroding legally for decades; if the FCC uses license renewal threats as leverage over editorial content, it could face a First Amendment challenge that reaches the Supreme Court and potentially dismantles the legal foundation for broadcast content regulation altogether. Hiring Paul Clement signals that Disney is treating this as a precedent-setting constitutional battle, not an administrative dispute to be settled quietly through licensing negotiation. The FCC's actions fit a broader pattern of using regulatory and financial pressure on media institutions as a substitute for direct prior restraint, which courts have consistently found unconstitutional. If the Court ultimately hears a constitutional challenge to FCC content-based enforcement, the outcome could determine whether Congress retains the authority to impose any editorial obligations on over-the-air broadcasters — a ruling that would reshape the regulatory landscape for the remaining broadcast television audience of approximately 50 million American households.

**Context:** Canvas, owned by Instructure and used by approximately 41% of North American higher education institutions, suffered a second breach on May 7–8, 2026 at the hands of ShinyHunters — a prolific extortion group previously responsible for breaches at Ticketmaster, AT&T, and Snowflake. The initial breach had already exposed 3.6 terabytes of data from over 9,000 institutions affecting an estimated 275 million individuals. ShinyHunters' ransom deadline is May 12, with threatened release of "several billions of private messages" between students and teachers if unpaid.

**Why it matters:** The timing — disrupting finals at roughly 9,000 institutions simultaneously — maximizes institutional pressure to pay by making every day of platform unavailability directly harmful to students whose academic futures depend on the system, a calculation ShinyHunters almost certainly made deliberately. The breach illustrates the structural vulnerability of edtech consolidation: a single vendor's compromise instantly paralyzes an entire sector with no readily available fallback infrastructure. Privately held by KKR, Instructure is not subject to the SEC's four-day material breach disclosure rules, potentially slowing notification to affected schools and families. Student messaging data constitutes a qualitatively different privacy harm than name-and-email exposure, encompassing private communications from minors federally protected under FERPA, creating distinct legal exposure for every institution that processed student data through Canvas.

## 9. Will the Supreme Court end nitrogen gas executions?
**Source:** scotusblog.com | **Topic:** law
**Link:** https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/05/will-the-supreme-court-end-nitrogen-gas-executions/

**Lead:** Three liberal justices have filed written dissents in three separate nitrogen hypoxia execution cases characterizing the method as cruel and unusual punishment, with witnesses reporting "violent thrashing" and more than 225 "agonized breaths" during Alabama's first-in-the-nation nitrogen executions — raising questions about whether the Court is approaching a grant of certiorari on the constitutional question.

**Context:** Alabama became the first state to execute a prisoner using nitrogen hypoxia in January 2024, when Kenneth Eugene Smith died after more than 225 documented "agonized breaths" and involuntary convulsions reported by witnesses — contradicting state officials' characterization of nitrogen gas as a peaceful alternative to lethal injection. The Supreme Court declined to stay his execution, but Justices Sotomayor, Kagan, and Jackson all issued written dissents. Alabama executed Anthony Boyd in October 2025 despite continued liberal bloc dissents. The Court has not yet accepted certiorari on the Eighth Amendment question; under the rule of four, four justices must vote to grant review, and three consistent liberal votes plus Justice Gorsuch's separate dissent in one case places the Court within striking distance of that threshold. Multiple other states — Florida, Idaho, Mississippi, and Oklahoma — have already authorized nitrogen gas and are prepared to proceed.

**Why it matters:** A grant of certiorari would carry enormous practical consequences because a ruling against nitrogen gas would simultaneously invalidate statutes in all five states that have authorized the method and force them to find alternative execution approaches. The central legal question is whether the Court applies the Baze/Glossip framework requiring challengers to identify a known and available alternative — a framework designed for lethal injection that sits awkwardly against a method the state itself chose by arguing it was the most humane option, which then produced prolonged visible suffering. The SCOTUSblog analysis argues the witness-documented suffering creates the factual predicate for a constitutional ruling, but existing doctrine may need revision. A ruling would also reopen the "evolving standards of decency" analysis at a time when national polling shows declining public support for capital punishment broadly, potentially inviting the Court to address the death penalty's constitutional status more directly than it has in decades.
